**Direct answer:** Agarwood is wounded *Aquilaria* heartwood that has filled with resin; sandalwood is the naturally aromatic heartwood of *Santalum* trees. One is an injury response, the other a built-in oil reserve. Buyers who treat them as two grades of the same "fragrant wood" misread both markets.

## Key facts

| | Agarwood (沉香) | Sandalwood (檀香) |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical family | Thymelaeaceae, *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* | Santalaceae, *Santalum* |
| Why it smells | Wound- and microbe-triggered resin | Heartwood oil laid down as the tree matures |
| Age at harvest | Years after induction; wild material much older | Commonly 15 years or more |
| Main legal instrument | CITES Appendix II | IUCN status and national rules |
| Dominant risk | Resin substitution and oil loading | Species substitution and oil dilution |

The two woods sit next to each other on incense shelves and almost nowhere else. Agarwood is a pathological product: no wound, no resin, no scent. Sandalwood is a physiological one: a healthy tree builds the oil as it grows, and the oil is already there when the trunk is cut. That difference explains why the fraud patterns, the price signals, and the sustainability arguments diverge so sharply.

## Key Takeaways

- **Agarwood is made by injury.** The tree does not produce resin until something damages it; fungal communities then shape what the resin becomes.
- **Sandalwood is made by time.** A planted tree is not a harvestable tree. The heartwood has to mature before the oil profile is worth the wait.
- **The grading vocabulary is not a single ladder.** Resin load and fibre condition are two measurements, and 奇楠 confuses almost every English guide that treats it as "grade A+."
- **Species is the first fact to establish.** "Indian sandalwood" is a trade description, not a botanical guarantee; "agarwood" covers 21 accepted *Aquilaria* species and several *Gyrinops*.
- **Legality is not a vibe.** Aquilaria and Gyrinops are CITES Appendix II; cross-border movement needs the right permits, and domestic rules add another layer.
- **Price has four components.** Mass, oil content, provenance, and craft. A quote that will not separate them is a quote you cannot audit.

## Two woods, two failure modes

**Direct answer:** Agarwood fraud usually adds something to the wood; sandalwood fraud usually takes something away from the oil. That asymmetry is the quickest way to interrogate a supplier.

Agarwood's value is concentrated in resin that is not uniformly distributed. A trader can therefore improve a poor piece by adding resin — boiling it in oil, injecting it under pressure, soaking it in dye — and the outside may look better than the inside. Sandalwood's value is concentrated in an oil that is expensive to produce and easy to dilute. A trader can stretch a genuine oil with a cheaper woody base, or sell a different *Santalum* species under the Indian name, and the stick may still smell pleasant.

The buyer's first question therefore differs by wood:

- For agarwood: **"What was done to this piece?"**
- For sandalwood: **"What species is this, and how much of the oil is actually *S. album*?"**

Neither question can be answered by a photograph. Both can be narrowed by a short, disciplined inspection — which is what the rest of this page sets out.

## What agarwood actually is

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is the resin-bearing heartwood of *Aquilaria* trees, formed after wounding and microbial colonisation. Healthy wood is pale, light, and close to scentless.

The genus *Aquilaria* contains 21 accepted species, native to rainforests from northeast India through southern China, mainland Southeast Asia, and Indonesia. *Aquilaria sinensis* is the Chinese species; *A. malaccensis* is the classic Southeast Asian trade species; *A. crassna* dominates Vietnamese plantations. *Gyrinops* species, a separate genus in the same family, also produce agarwood and are covered by the same CITES listing.

The formation sequence matters because every trade claim maps onto a stage of it:

1. **Wound.** Wind damage, insect borers, lightning, or a deliberate cut opens the trunk.
2. **Colonisation.** Bacteria and fungi enter the wound. The community is mixed, not a single "agarwood fungus," and the literature is still arguing about which organisms matter most.
3. **Chemical response.** The tree deposits sesquiterpenes and chromones — two very different families of molecules — into the surrounding wood.
4. **Maturation.** Resin concentration rises over years. The wood darkens, gains mass, and begins to smell when warmed.
5. **Death and burial (in wild material).** Trees that die and lie in the forest can develop the dense, soft, unusually complex material the trade calls 熟结.

The distinction between 生结 (resin in a living tree) and 熟结 (resin in dead or fallen wood) is not romantic taxonomy. It describes different chemistry and different risk. Wild 熟结 is scarce, expensive, and almost impossible to verify from a photograph. Plantation 生结 is more predictable, more available, and usually less complex.

A 2022 review in *Molecules* describes agarwood aroma as the product of "complex biotic, abiotic, and physical stress" and puts the number of fragrant molecules at roughly 150. That figure is a useful corrective to the single-note "oud" language of perfume marketing: the material is a mixture, and the mixture changes with species, wound type, microbial community, and time.

## The four names on the label

**Direct answer:** 沉香, oud, gaharu, and kyara are not synonyms. They come from different trade languages and imply different things about species, geography, and grade.

| Term | Language / trade | What it usually signals | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| 沉香 (chénxiāng) | Chinese | Resinous *Aquilaria* wood; the classical material | Species, origin, or resin load |
| Oud / oudh | Arabic | Fragrant wood used in Gulf perfumery | That the oil is wood-distilled rather than compounded |
| Gaharu | Malay / Indonesian | The Southeast Asian trade commodity | Sustainable harvest or CITES paperwork |
| Kyara / 奇楠 | Japanese / Chinese | A rare, soft, high-aroma condition | That the piece is wild, or that "grade" is meaningful |

The English phrase "agarwood oil" is similarly loose. It can mean oil distilled from resinous wood, oil distilled from inoculated plantation wood, or a fragrance compound built around a small amount of either. The label rarely tells you which.

For the Chinese grading vocabulary in detail, see the [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/). For the specific buying traps around the top grade, see [qinan buying and authentication](/blog/qinan-buying-authentication/).

## The grading staircase is really two gauges

**Direct answer:** One gauge measures how much resin is in the wood; the other measures what the resin has done to the fibre. A single "grade" number collapses both and hides the interesting cases.

The familiar water test sorts pieces into sinking, half-sinking, and floating. That is a resin-load gauge, and it is genuinely useful: resin is denser than the dry wood it replaces, so a resin-rich piece displaces less water. But the test has a blind spot. A piece can be heavy because it is loaded with injected oil, and a genuine 奇楠 piece can sit high in water because its resin is soft and evenly distributed rather than concentrated in dense bands.

The second gauge is textile rather than numeric. Ordinary agarwood is often described as 木包油 — wood wrapping oil, with hard fibre and visible resin veins. 奇楠 is described as 油包木 — oil wrapping wood, with fibre that has been transformed until it can be shaved into curls or kneaded into a pellet. That is a different material state, not a higher rung on the same ladder.

This is why "Is 奇楠 better than 沉水?" is the wrong question. The two words answer different questions. A useful buying rule follows: **grade the piece you can inspect, not the word on the listing.** If the seller cannot tell you whether the claim is about resin load or fibre state, the claim is decoration.

## Sandalwood is a different supply chain

**Direct answer:** Sandalwood is the aromatic heartwood of *Santalum*, a hemiparasitic genus that draws water and nutrients from neighbouring plants. The oil is laid down slowly, and the tree must be old enough for the heartwood to be worth cutting.

*Santalum album* — Indian or white sandalwood — is the benchmark species. It is native to Indonesia, the Philippines, and Western Australia according to Kew's Plants of the World Online, and it has been planted across South and Southeast Asia for so long that "Indian sandalwood" has become a trade name rather than a reliable origin statement. The species is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List.

Other species enter the same market:

- *Santalum spicatum* — Australian sandalwood, generally lower in santalol and more variable in aroma.
- *Santalum austrocaledonicum* — New Caledonian sandalwood, often sold as a regional specialty.
- *Santalum paniculatum* — Hawaiian sandalwood, small volumes and high prices.
- *Santalum yasi* — Fijian sandalwood, important in Pacific trade.

The genus is hemiparasitic: it photosynthesises, but it also taps the roots of host plants for water and minerals. That makes plantation design unusually important. A sandalwood plantation is really a mixed-species system, and the host species can influence growth rate and heartwood formation. It also makes "just plant more trees" a less complete answer than it sounds.

The age question is the one buyers underestimate. A sandalwood tree does not produce a merchantable heartwood core in a few years. Growers commonly speak in terms of 15 years or more, and the best material comes from older trees. That long wait is the structural reason genuine *S. album* oil is expensive, and the reason diluted or species-substituted oil is so common.

## The species map

**Direct answer:** Learn six names and most of the market becomes legible. The rest is provenance detail and marketing.

| Trade name | Botanical species | Typical use | Buyer note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chinese agarwood | *Aquilaria sinensis* | Incense, medicine, carving | Plantation supply now dominates |
| Malaccan agarwood | *A. malaccensis* | Incense, oil | Classic wild trade species; CITES-listed |
| Vietnamese agarwood | *A. crassna* | Incense, oil | Large plantation sector |
| Indian sandalwood | *Santalum album* | Incense, oil, carving | Vulnerable; heavily regulated in India |
| Australian sandalwood | *S. spicatum* | Oil, incense | Cheaper, different aroma profile |
| Red sandalwood | *Pterocarpus santalinus* | Dye, carving, some incense | Not a *Santalum*; different material entirely |

That last row causes more confusion than any other. Red sandalwood is a legume, not a sandalwood, and its value comes from colour and density rather than santalol. The two are sold in the same craft channels. The [sandalwood vs red sandalwood comparison](/blog/sandalwood-vs-red-sandalwood/) lays out the difference; the short version is that a red, dense, almost scentless block is not a cheap substitute for *S. album*.

## What you are actually buying

**Direct answer:** Four product forms dominate, and each has a different relationship to the original wood: chips, powder, oil, and finished incense. The further you move from the chip, the harder authentication becomes.

**Chips** are the closest thing to an inspectable asset. You can see the resin bands, smell the piece at room temperature, heat a corner, and weigh it. The limitations are real — a dark exterior can be dyed, and a heavy chip can be loaded — but chips leave more evidence than any other form.

**Powder** removes the evidence. Once the wood is ground, you cannot see resin distribution, and blending is trivial. This is why agarwood powder is a favourite vehicle for substitution. The [powder vs chips comparison](/blog/agarwood-powder-vs-chips/) is worth reading before any bulk purchase.

**Oil** is a distillation product, and its quality depends on the feedstock, the distillation method, and what happened after the condenser. A genuine oil can be diluted; a poor feedstock can be dressed up with isolates. The [agarwood incense guide](/blog/agarwood-incense/) covers how oil-based sticks differ from wood-based ones.

**Finished incense** is the most compound product. A stick labelled "agarwood" may contain agarwood powder, agarwood oil, a synthetic oud accord, or a mixture. The [natural vs synthetic incense](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/) and [what makes incense natural](/blog/what-makes-incense-natural/) pages deal with that layer directly.

A practical rule: **buy the form whose evidence you can actually examine.** If you cannot inspect chips, do not pay chip prices for powder.

## The four components of price

**Direct answer:** A defensible agarwood or sandalwood price separates into mass, oil or resin content, provenance, and craft. When a seller quotes a single number, ask which component is doing the work.

1. **Mass.** Agarwood is sold by weight, and resin-rich wood is denser. Sandalwood is also sold by weight, but the relevant variable is heartwood yield, not resin.
2. **Oil or resin content.** In agarwood, this is the water test and the visual resin band. In sandalwood, it is the oil percentage and santalol profile.
3. **Provenance.** Wild versus plantation, region, and species. Provenance is the easiest component to claim and the hardest to verify.
4. **Craft.** Carving, grading, sorting, and finishing. A cleanly sorted lot of uniform chips costs more than a mixed bag, and the difference is labour rather than chemistry.

The framework is useful because it turns a vague argument about "quality" into a set of questions. A high price with no provenance detail is a red flag. A low price with a specific species and a plausible plantation story may be a legitimate commodity. The [how to buy agarwood for incense manufacturing](/blog/how-to-buy-agarwood-for-incense-manufacturing/) page applies this to bulk purchasing.

## The authentication ladder

**Direct answer:** Inspect in the order that costs the least and destroys the least: story, surface, water, heat, cut. Stop when a claim fails; do not let a later test rescue an earlier contradiction.

**1. Story.** Ask for species, harvest region, year, and the name of the supplier one step back. A real supply chain has boring answers. A fabricated one tends to have a dramatic answer and no documents.

**2. Surface.** Look for resin distribution that follows the grain and accumulates around wounds. Uniform darkness, paint-like edges, or resin pooled in cracks are not natural patterns. For sandalwood, look for a pale-to-brown heartwood with a fine, even grain; a red or purple block is probably *Pterocarpus*.

**3. Water.** Use a clear glass. A sinking agarwood chip is a data point, not a verdict. Record the result and compare it with the seller's claim. A piece sold as "sinking" that floats is a failed claim regardless of what else is true.

**4. Heat.** Warm a corner gently, ideally on a heater rather than with a flame. Agarwood should release a complex, sweet-woody scent that develops over minutes. Acrid smoke, a sharp perfume note, or a smell that collapses immediately suggests oil loading or a fragrance compound. Sandalwood should smell creamy and soft, not like a candle.

**5. Cut.** This is the destructive test, and it is the one that settles arguments. A clean interior should show resin penetration that matches the exterior. If the dark surface gives way to pale, dry wood a millimetre in, the piece was dressed. For sandalwood, the heartwood should be continuous and oily-looking, not a thin scented shell around pale sapwood.

The ladder is deliberately ordered by cost. Most bad purchases can be rejected at step one or two. The cut is for material you already have reason to trust, or for a sample you can afford to lose.

## Legality and sustainability, without the greenwash

**Direct answer:** *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* are CITES Appendix II. That means international trade is regulated, not banned. A legal shipment needs the correct permits, and a domestic sale may still be legal without them — which is exactly why "CITES" is not a quality grade.

Appendix II listing means the species is not currently threatened with extinction but could become so if trade is not controlled. For agarwood, the practical consequences are:

- Export from a range state generally requires a CITES export permit.
- Import into a party state generally requires an import permit for Appendix II specimens in some jurisdictions and not others; the details are national.
- Processed products can fall under different rules than raw wood.
- A permit covers a shipment, not a species. A seller who says "CITES-certified" without a permit number has told you nothing verifiable.

Sandalwood sits under a different regime. *Santalum album* is IUCN Vulnerable, and India regulates harvest and transport heavily; Australian *S. spicatum* has its own state-level controls. There is no single global certificate that makes a sandalwood stick "sustainable." The useful questions are local: which species, which state, which permit, and which year.

A buyer's legality check has three parts: **species, document, and chain.** Species tells you which rules apply. The document tells you whether this shipment was cleared. The chain tells you whether the document belongs to this material. Most "sustainable agarwood" marketing answers none of the three.

## What goes wrong in a first order

**Direct answer:** First orders fail in predictable ways. The failures are boring, which is why they are avoidable.

- **Buying a grade word instead of a sample.** "沉水" and "奇楠" are claims. Ask for a 10–20 g sample and test it before the bulk order.
- **Confusing oil smell with wood smell.** A stick that smells strongly at room temperature may be oil-dressed. Genuine high-resin wood is often quiet until warmed.
- **Ignoring the substrate.** A beautiful dark chip with a pale core is a surface treatment. Ask for a cut edge photograph or cut a sample yourself.
- **Treating "plantation" as a synonym for "poor."** Plantation agarwood can be clean, consistent, and honestly sold. The problem is not plantation; it is a wild-forest story attached to a plantation product.
- **Assuming a certificate proves species.** A CITES permit proves a shipment was declared and cleared. It does not prove the species on the label, and it does not prove the resin load.

## Storage: the quiet variable

**Direct answer:** Both woods change after purchase. Agarwood usually improves with careful storage; sandalwood oil can oxidise and turn flat. The difference is chemistry, not sentiment.

Agarwood chips keep best in a closed container, away from light and strong odours, at stable humidity. The resin is not volatile in the way an essential oil is, but the surface aroma can fade with exposure. A modest rest after shipping is normal; a piece that smells of nothing for months may simply be low-resin material.

Sandalwood is more fragile. The oil is in the wood, and the wood is hygroscopic. Repeated wet-dry cycles can crack carvings and open checks in sticks. For oil, oxidation is the enemy: a half-full bottle with a lot of headspace will lose the creamy top notes faster than a full one. Store it cool, dark, and full.

Neither wood benefits from the refrigerator. Condensation is a real risk when the container comes back to room temperature, and the aroma does not improve.

## How to compare two pieces without a lab

**Direct answer:** Use the same three-minute protocol for both, and write down the answers. Comparison is a discipline, not a nose talent.

1. **Weigh and photograph.** Note the mass, dimensions, and colour under neutral light. A phone photo with a grey card is enough for a record.
2. **Smell cold, then warm.** Record the cold scent, then warm the same corner for 30 seconds and record again. The change matters more than the first impression.
3. **Test the claim, not the vibe.** If the seller claimed sinking, put it in water. If the seller claimed species, compare the grain and colour with a reference image.
4. **Keep the sample.** Label it with the date, seller, and claim. A physical reference library is more useful than a folder of product photos.

This protocol will not turn a beginner into an appraiser. It will do something more useful: it will make the seller's claims testable, and it will give you a record when a claim fails.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** I would rather buy a modest, honestly described plantation chip than a dramatic "wild 奇楠" with no chain of custody. The first is a material you can learn from; the second is a story you cannot audit.

**Reasoning:** First, the market's biggest information asymmetry is not species — it is treatment. Oil loading, dyeing, and pressure injection are cheap, fast, and hard to see in a photograph, and they cluster exactly where prices are highest. Second, the "wild" premium is doing too much work in most listings. Wild agarwood is genuinely scarce, but the word is also the easiest thing to print on a box, and a buyer who pays for it without a chain of custody is paying for a narrative. Third, the plantation sector has improved. Clean, species-identified, properly processed plantation wood is now a real category, and treating it as inherently inferior keeps buyers chasing unverifiable wild material.

**Disclosure:** This is an editorial judgment about buying strategy, not a laboratory finding or a legal opinion. Reasonable buyers weigh provenance and craft differently, and the right choice depends on whether you are buying to burn, to collect, or to resell.

## FAQ

**Is agarwood the same as oud?**
Oud is the Arabic trade name for fragrant agarwood, and "oud oil" usually means an oil distilled from agarwood or a fragrance built to smell like it. The word does not specify species, origin, or whether the oil is wood-distilled.

**Is sandalwood a type of agarwood?**
No. Agarwood comes from *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* (Thymelaeaceae); sandalwood comes from *Santalum* (Santalaceae). They are unrelated plants that happen to share a market shelf.

**Why is agarwood so expensive?**
Because the resin is a small fraction of a slow-growing tree, wild supply is limited, and demand from incense, perfume, and carving is global. The 2022 *Molecules* review notes a global trade estimate of US$6–8 billion, while cautioning that reliable data are hard to obtain.

**Does sinking mean top grade?**
It means high resin load. It does not mean the piece is 奇楠, wild, or untreated. A heavy piece can also be oil-loaded.

**Is all agarwood CITES-listed?**
*Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* are listed in CITES Appendix II. The listing covers the genus, not a single species, and it regulates international trade rather than possession.

**Is Indian sandalwood always from India?**
No. *Santalum album* is native to Indonesia, the Philippines, and Western Australia as well as being widely planted in India. "Indian sandalwood" is a trade name; the botanical species and the harvest location are separate facts.

**Can I tell real agarwood by burning it?**
Burning gives useful clues — resinous wood should smell complex and sweet, not acrid or perfumed — but it is destructive and not definitive. A cut edge and a warm-up test are usually more informative.

**What is the single most useful question to ask a new supplier?**
"Which species, which region, which year, and what was done to the wood after harvest?" A supplier who can answer all four is at least operating with a traceable story. One who cannot is asking you to buy a word.

## Summary

Agarwood and sandalwood are both fragrant woods, and that is where the similarity stops. Agarwood is a wound response; sandalwood is a slow accumulation. Agarwood's main risk is what has been added; sandalwood's is what has been taken away or substituted. Learn the species, separate the price into its components, and test claims in the cheapest order. The rest is patience.

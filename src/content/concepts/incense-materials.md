**Direct answer:** An incense material is any substance that carries or releases scent when warmed or burned — wood, resin, root, leaf, flower, seed, animal secretion, or mineral. The useful question is never "is it natural?" It is "which material is this, in what form, at what grade, and what was done to it after harvest?" Those four answers can be checked. The word "natural" cannot.

## Key facts

| Layer | What it decides | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Which plant, animal, or mineral | Botanical name, DNA, microscopy |
| Form | Chip, powder, resin, oil, absolute, stick | Physical inspection, labelling |
| Grade | Purity, particle size, oil load, age | Sieve data, assay, sensory panel |
| Treatment | Cleaning, soaking, dyeing, oil loading | Cut surface, solvent test, lab assay |
| Legality | CITES, national export rules, pharmacopoeia | Permits, monograph compliance |

The five layers are independent. A material can be botanically correct and heavily oil-loaded; legally exported and chemically diluted; traditionally named and physically misdescribed. Almost every bad purchase happens because a buyer collapsed two layers into one question.

## Key Takeaways

- **Identity is the only layer with a hard answer.** Species can be tested. "Premium" cannot.
- **Form predicts failure.** Whole chips fail on treatment; powders fail on dilution; oils fail on both.
- **Grade language is local.** 沉水, 老山, first grade, and "A+" are not comparable across materials or markets.
- **Concealed substitution is the fraud.** Open substitution — declared, priced, and used deliberately — is normal trade.
- **The certificate is a scope document.** It proves what was tested on the sample submitted, nothing more.
- **Price per kilogram is the wrong denominator.** Price per unit of aroma-bearing fraction is the number that travels.

## What the word "material" hides

**Direct answer:** Incense materials are usually sorted by scent family in consumer writing and by plant part in trade. Neither sort tells you how the material fails. Sorting by physical class does.

A resin, a wood, a root, and an oil can all smell of the same forest and behave nothing alike in storage, blending, or verification. The classification that earns its keep is the one that predicts the failure mode:

| Class | Examples | What "pure" means | Dominant failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resinous wood | agarwood, sandalwood | species + resin load | oil loading, dyeing |
| Resin | frankincense, myrrh, benzoin, copal | species + harvest grade | species mix, bark content |
| Root and rhizome | vetiver, spikenard, iris | species + age | substitution with cheaper roots |
| Leaf and herb | patchouli, artemisia, mint | species + harvest timing | stem content, mould |
| Flower | rose, jasmine, osmanthus | species + picking stage | solvent residue, synthetic reconstruction |
| Seed and fruit | cardamom, clove, star anise | species + volatile oil content | spent material, re-oiling |
| Animal and mineral | ambergris, musk, realgar | origin + legal status | prohibition, synthetic stand-ins |

This is the first page of the site's material layer: 150 ingredient records, each with a botanical name, a Chinese name, an aroma profile, and a related-materials list. The records are the atoms. This page is the grammar — how to read a claim about any one of them.

## The four questions, in order

**Direct answer:** Ask identity, then form, then grade, then treatment. Reversing the order is how buyers end up arguing about price with someone who has not yet established what the material is.

**1. Identity.** Which species? For woods this is decisive: *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* for agarwood, *Santalum* for sandalwood, *Pterocarpus* for the red "sandalwood" that is not sandalwood at all. For resins it is equally decisive: *Boswellia sacra*, *B. frereana*, and *B. papyrifera* are three different commercial materials with one English name.

**2. Form.** A kilogram of chips, a kilogram of powder, and a kilogram of oil are not three presentations of one product. They are three products with different verification routes and different substitution economics. Powder hides species; oil hides everything.

**3. Grade.** Grade is a measurement, not a compliment. It should resolve into something countable: mesh size, volatile oil percentage, resin density, age, or the absence of foreign matter. If a grade cannot be expressed as a number or a named test, it is marketing.

**4. Treatment.** What happened between harvest and the bag? Cleaning, fumigation, soaking, dyeing, oil loading, blending, re-oiling spent material. This is the layer most often left out of the conversation and most often responsible for the disappointment.

Write the four answers down before negotiating. A supplier who can answer three of four is a supplier with a traceable chain. A supplier who answers none is selling a word.

## Why "natural" is not a material property

**Direct answer:** "Natural" describes an origin, not a composition. A natural material can be adulterated with another natural material, and the resulting product is still natural.

This is the single most exploited gap in incense retail. A stick can contain only botanical ingredients and still be mostly charcoal dust, binder, and a few drops of a cheap essential oil. The label "100% natural" survives because every component technically came from a plant.

There is no legal definition of "natural incense" in most markets. There are definitions for food flavourings, cosmetics, and proprietary medicines, and they do not transfer to a burning stick sold as a home fragrance. That regulatory vacuum is why the useful questions are structural rather than adjectival:

- What percentage of the stick's mass is aromatic material, as opposed to binder, charcoal, and water?
- Is the scent from the wood or resin itself, or from an added oil?
- If an oil was added, is it the same species as the named material?
- Is there a batch record linking the stick to the material lot?

None of those questions requires a laboratory. All of them require a supplier willing to answer in numbers.

## Grade language across five markets

**Direct answer:** Grades in the incense trade measure five different things under one word: density, purity, age, particle size, and species prestige. Confusing them is the most common analytical error in buying.

**Density grades** dominate agarwood. 沉水 (sinks), 半沉 (half-sinks), 浮水 (floats) describe resin load through average density. The test is cheap and crude: it measures the whole piece, not the distribution of resin inside it.

**Purity grades** dominate resins and powders. Tears versus siftings, "first grade" versus "second grade," clean versus bark-adhered. These are visual and hand-sorted, and they are honest as long as the sorting criteria are stated.

**Age grades** dominate sandalwood and some roots. "Old mountain" 老山 refers to a trade category of mature Indian material; it is not a botanical name and not a certificate. Vetiver and spikenard gain depth with age, and age claims are almost never documented.

**Particle grades** dominate powders. Mesh number is a real measurement — 80 mesh, 100 mesh, 200 mesh — and it changes burn behaviour, extraction yield, and how much material you need per stick. A powder sold as "fine" without a mesh figure is unspecified, not premium.

**Prestige grades** dominate flower absolutes and specialty oils. "Grand cru," "first flush," "absolute vs concrete" carry real process differences, then get stretched into price language that outruns the process.

The practical rule: whenever you see a grade word, ask which of the five it belongs to, then ask for the measurement behind it. If the answer is another adjective, the grade is decorative.

## The authentication ladder

**Direct answer:** Authentication methods form a ladder ordered by cost and destructiveness. Start at the cheapest rung that can falsify the claim, and stop as soon as the claim is decided.

| Rung | Method | Cost | Destructive | Answers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Documents and chain | low | no | legality, declared identity |
| 2 | Senses (sight, smell, touch) | low | no | gross substitution, treatment clues |
| 3 | Physical tests (water, heat, cut) | low | partly | density, resin distribution, surface treatment |
| 4 | Wet chemistry and microscopy | medium | yes | adulterant classes, tissue identity |
| 5 | Instrumental (GC-MS, LC-MS, Raman, DNA) | high | yes | molecular profile, species |

Two rules make the ladder useful. **First, the ladder is not a hierarchy of truth.** A document can be forged and a nose can be fooled, but both are cheap and both eliminate whole categories of fraud before you spend on a laboratory. **Second, stop at the first rung that settles your decision.** If a €40 bag of frankincense has bark in it, you do not need GC-MS; you need a sieve.

The ladder also has a blind spot worth naming. Rungs 1–3 test the sample in front of you. They say nothing about the next bag. Only a retained sample, a batch record, and a supplier who will repeat the result give you consistency — and consistency is what a production buyer is actually purchasing.

## What each method can and cannot prove

**Direct answer:** Every authentication method proves one narrow thing well. Buying a test that does not match the claim is as wasteful as buying no test at all.

**Documents** establish that a shipment was declared and cleared. A CITES permit for *Aquilaria* proves the wood moved legally; it does not prove species, resin load, or that the permit refers to this piece. Permits attach to consignments, and consignments get split.

**Sensory assessment** is stronger than it is usually given credit for, within limits. A trained nose can separate *Boswellia* species, detect kerosene or synthetic musks, and notice the flat, thin profile of re-oiled spent material. It cannot quantify adulteration at 5%, and it degrades with fatigue — which is why professional graders work in short sessions with reference samples.

**Water tests** measure average density. They are informative for agarwood and useless for powders, oils, and resins. They can be gamed by loading a light piece with dense oil, which is precisely the fraud the test is supposed to catch.

**Microscopy** identifies tissue and can reveal foreign starch, dye crystals, and wood anatomy inconsistent with the declared species. It is cheap, underused in the incense trade, and limited by the skill of the operator.

**Chromatography** — GC-MS for volatiles, LC-MS for the less volatile chromones and polar compounds — gives a molecular fingerprint. It is the standard method for essential oil authentication and the reason adulterated oils are detectable at all. It cannot tell you whether a piece of wood was wild or planted, and it cannot certify "vintage."

**DNA barcoding** answers species, and it has become practical for agarwood specifically: Tanaka and Ito used *trnL–trnF* and *matK* sequences to identify source species in commercial samples, and a follow-up study applied barcoding to Indonesian agarwood. The limitation is the material itself. Wood is dead tissue with degraded DNA, and processed powders and oils often contain none that is amplifiable. Barcoding works best on chips and unfinished wood; it is weakest exactly where fraud is easiest.

**Raman spectroscopy with chemometrics** sits at the top of the ladder for oils. Huang and colleagues used it to authenticate agarwood essential oils and detect adulteration, which matters because agarwood oil is one of the most expensive and most stretched oils in the trade. The method is fast and non-destructive, but it needs a validated model — a Raman spectrum without a reference library is a picture of a spectrum, not an answer.

## Form changes the test

**Direct answer:** The same material requires different verification depending on whether you buy it as chips, powder, resin, oil, or a finished stick.

**Chips and pieces.** You can see grain, cut them, smell the fresh surface, and run a water test. This is the most verifiable form and the one where treatment fraud is most visible — oil loading leaves a sheen, dye pools in cracks, and the interior tells a different story from the exterior.

**Powder.** You lose anatomy and gain homogeneity. Powder is where species substitution and spent-material blending hide, because a fine powder looks like a fine powder. Sieve data, colour under magnification, and a solvent extract are the practical checks.

**Resin.** Tears can be inspected, weighed, and smelled. Resin is often the most honest form in the trade and the easiest to mix with bark, sand, or a cheaper copal. Water solubility and alcohol solubility separate resin classes quickly.

**Essential oil and absolute.** Only chromatography or a validated spectroscopic method says anything reliable. Specific gravity helps against the crudest dilution; it does not detect a well-built reconstruction.

**Finished sticks.** The stick is a composite, and the composite is the point. A stick can be 70% charcoal and binder with 3% aromatic material and still smell pleasant. The useful disclosures are mass composition, aromatic percentage, and whether the scent is wood-derived or oil-added. Most brands disclose none of the three.

## Substitution: the normal case and the fraud case

**Direct answer:** Substitution becomes fraud at the moment it stops being disclosed. The material question is not "was something substituted?" but "was the substitution declared, priced, and fit for purpose?"

Open substitution is everywhere and mostly legitimate. A formulator replacing expensive Indian sandalwood with Australian *S. spicatum* in a test batch is doing product development, not fraud. A supplier selling "sandalwood powder" that is 60% *S. spicatum* at a price consistent with that blend is making a market offer. The buyer's job is to decide whether the blend meets the brief.

Concealed substitution has four recurring mechanisms:

1. **Species substitution.** A cheaper species sold under a premium name. The classic cases are Indian versus Australian sandalwood, *Boswellia papyrifera* sold as *B. sacra*, and *Pterocarpus* sold as red sandalwood.
2. **Part substitution.** Heartwood replaced by sapwood, rhizome replaced by root, flower replaced by leaf. The species may be correct and the part wrong, which is why "100% vetiver" can still be a bad buy.
3. **Dilution.** A genuine material stretched with a neutral carrier — fractionated coconut oil in absolutes, propylene glycol in fragrance oils, water in "pure" hydrosols. Dilution is the dominant oil fraud because it is cheap, hard to see, and often technically legal if the label says "blend."
4. **Reconstitution.** Spent material re-oiled, or a synthetic accord built to mimic a natural profile and sold with a botanical name. This is the fastest-growing category, because modern flavour and fragrance chemistry can produce convincing reconstructions at a fraction of the natural cost.

The disclosure test sorts them. Ask: if I analysed this, would the seller's description still be accurate? If the answer is no, the issue is not substitution. It is misdescription.

## Adulteration patterns by class

**Direct answer:** Each material class has a signature adulteration pattern. Knowing the pattern tells you which test to run and which claim to distrust.

**Agarwood: loading and dyeing.** Oil boiling, pressure injection, dye soaking, and scenting non-resinous wood with synthetic oud accords. The tell is a piece that smells strong on the outside and weak on a fresh cut, or dye that bleeds on a damp cloth. Density can be raised without resin.

**Sandalwood: species and oil dilution.** *Santalum album* replaced by *S. spicatum*, *S. austrocaledonicum*, or *S. paniculatum*; oil stretched with a cheaper woody base or a santalol-enriched fraction. The tell is a santalol profile that does not match the declared species, which requires chromatography to see.

**Frankincense and myrrh: species and grade mixing.** *Boswellia sacra* versus *B. frereana* versus *B. papyrifera*; tears mixed with siftings and bark. The tell is visual sorting plus burn behaviour, since the resin fractions differ in how they melt and smoke.

**Florals: solvent and reconstruction.** Absolute cut with a cheaper absolute, or a synthetic accord sold as "rose." The tell is a profile that is too clean, too linear, or missing the heavy wax notes of a true concrete.

**Herbs and roots: spent material and mould.** Steam-distilled herb re-sold as fresh, or material stored damp. The tell is a flat smell with no top notes, plus visible fungal growth or a musty undertone that survives blending.

**Powders: dilution with inert fillers.** Starch, chalk, sawdust, and colourants. The tell is behaviour in water and under magnification, plus a burn that leaves more ash than the material should.

## Price signals that survive scrutiny

**Direct answer:** Absolute price per kilogram is nearly meaningless across forms. Convert every quote to price per unit of aroma-bearing fraction, then compare.

Consider two sandalwood offers. One is $120/kg for powder at 4% volatile oil; the other is $400/kg for powder at 2% volatile oil. Per kilogram, the first looks cheaper. Per kilogram of oil, the first costs $3,000 and the second $20,000 — the opposite conclusion. The denominator decides the answer, and sellers know most buyers never change it.

Three price signals are worth watching:

- **A price below the cost of the raw material** is not a bargain; it is a different material. This is the most reliable single indicator in the trade.
- **A price that does not move with grade** means the grade is decorative, or the material is blended to a constant specification regardless of input.
- **A price that moves too much between batches** means the supplier is buying spot material rather than controlling a supply chain. For production, that is a consistency risk even when the material is genuine.

The fourth signal is silence. A supplier who will not break a price into material, processing, and certification components is either unable to or unwilling to, and both are relevant to your risk.

## The regulatory layer

**Direct answer:** Legality and quality are separate questions, and a legal shipment can still be a bad material.

**CITES.** *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* have been on CITES Appendix II since 2005, which means export permits and certificates of origin for cross-border movement. The permit system regulates trade; it does not grade resin. A legally exported bale can contain poor wood, and a superb piece without paperwork is a legal problem regardless of quality.

**National rules.** India restricts sandalwood harvest and export; Indonesia and Malaysia regulate agarwood harvest and quota; China regulates the use of protected species and certain animal materials. These rules change, and they bind the buyer as well as the seller in most jurisdictions.

**Pharmacopoeia.** Where a material is also a medicine — 沉香, 檀香, 乳香, 没药 — the national pharmacopoeia sets identity tests and marker assays. The Chinese Pharmacopoeia monograph for 沉香 specifies thin-layer identification and a chromatographic assay, which is a useful benchmark even for incense buyers: it tells you what a competent laboratory would test.

**Combustible product rules.** Finished incense is regulated as a consumer product in some markets, with labelling and safety requirements that have nothing to do with the material's botanical identity. Material compliance does not imply product compliance.

## Storage and shelf life by class

**Direct answer:** Resins age well, woods age slowly, powders age badly, and oils age unpredictably. Storage decisions should follow the class, not the price.

| Class | Main enemy | Practical life | Storage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resin tears | heat, fusion | years to decades | cool, dry, wrapped, away from light |
| Resinous wood | drying, scent loss | years | sealed, stable humidity, no sunlight |
| Powder | moisture, oxidation, pests | 6–18 months | airtight, cool, desiccant, small batches |
| Essential oil | oxygen, light, heat | 1–3 years, varies | amber glass, full headspace, refrigeration for delicate oils |
| Absolute | solvent loss, light | 2–5 years | sealed glass, dark, stable temperature |
| Finished sticks | humidity, binder degradation | 1–3 years | dry, upright, away from odour |

Two details are worth more than the table. First, powder is the perishable form, and most buyers treat it as if it were a wood chip. A year-old powder has lost top notes and gained moisture; a fresh batch of the same material will behave differently in the same formula. Second, "aged" is not automatically better. Sandalwood and some roots improve; citrus oils and many florals degrade. Ageing is a material-specific claim, not a universal virtue.

## Batch consistency: the production buyer's real problem

**Direct answer:** A single good sample is a data point. A production line needs a distribution.

The three variables that move most in real shipments are moisture, particle size, and volatile oil content. All three are cheap to measure and rarely reported.

- **Moisture** changes weight, burn rate, and mould risk. A 2% shift in moisture is a 2% shift in the material you actually bought, and it can be enough to change how a stick burns.
- **Particle size** changes packing density, air flow, and extraction yield. Two powders at "100 mesh" from different mills can differ enough to require a formula adjustment.
- **Volatile oil content** changes intensity and cost per stick. A batch at half the usual oil content is not a discount; it is a different material at the same price.

The practical instrument is a batch record: retained sample, measurement date, moisture, mesh, oil content, supplier lot, and the name of the person who signed it. This is unglamorous and it is the entire difference between a hobby and a supply chain.

## How to read a certificate of analysis

**Direct answer:** A COA is a scope document. It tells you what was tested, on which sample, using which method — and it is silent on everything else.

Read it in this order:

1. **What is the sample?** A certificate for "agarwood oil" is not a certificate for the chips you are buying.
2. **Which method?** "GC-MS" without a column, programme, and library is a claim, not a result. A named ISO or pharmacopoeial method is stronger.
3. **What are the limits?** A result of "conforms" means nothing without the specification it conforms to. Look for numbers and units.
4. **Who issued it?** An in-house COA from the seller is a statement, not an independent verification. Third-party laboratories differ enormously in scope.
5. **What is absent?** If a COA reports identity but not adulterants, it has not addressed the main risk. Absence of a test is information.

The most common failure is scope mismatch: a genuine certificate for the wrong material, or for a sample that was never part of the shipment. Certificates travel with paper, not with bags.

## Sourcing: three questions that predict the relationship

**Direct answer:** Supplier quality shows up in how they answer questions about the boring parts — lots, storage, and failure.

Ask what happens when a batch fails your incoming check. A supplier with a real quality system will describe a return process, a credit, and a root-cause conversation. A trader will describe a discount. Ask for the last three lot numbers and their measured oil content. A supplier who cannot produce them is not tracking the variable that determines your product. Ask how the material was stored before it reached you. The answer predicts mould, pests, and scent loss more reliably than any grade word.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** I think the incense trade's obsession with "natural" is a distraction from the only question that changes outcomes — identity — and buyers who keep asking "is it natural?" will keep being sold blends as single materials.

**Reasoning:** First, "natural" has no enforceable definition for a burning stick in most markets, so it costs a seller nothing to print and gives a buyer nothing to test. Second, the fraud patterns that actually hurt people — species substitution, oil loading, spent material — are all committed with natural ingredients, so the adjective cannot exclude any of them. Third, identity is the one claim that can be settled with a named test, a reference sample, or a DNA sequence, which means it is the one claim worth negotiating over.

**Disclosure:** This is an editorial judgment about how to buy, not a statement of law or a guarantee about any specific product. "Natural" still carries meaning in regulated categories such as food flavouring and cosmetics; it simply does not carry a testable meaning in unregulated incense retail.

## FAQ

**Is natural incense always safer than synthetic?**
No. Safety depends on what is burned and how much smoke is inhaled, not on the origin of the molecule. Some natural resins produce more particulate matter than a clean synthetic; some synthetic fragrance oils contain ingredients with their own exposure limits. The safety page covers smoke and ventilation in detail.

**How can I tell agarwood from a fake without a lab?**
Cut a fresh surface, warm it gently, and compare the exterior and interior smell. Loading and dyeing usually show as a strong surface scent, an oily sheen, or dye on a damp cloth. None of those tests is definitive; they are filters, not proof.

**Is "essential oil" the same as the wood it came from?**
No. An oil is a distillate or extract with a different molecular profile, different intensity, and different cost. A stick scented with agarwood oil is an oil-scented stick, not a piece of agarwood, and the price should reflect that.

**What does mesh mean in incense powder?**
Mesh is the sieve size that the powder passes through. Higher mesh means finer particles. It affects burn rate, packing, and extraction, and it should be stated as a number, not as "fine" or "superfine."

**Do CITES permits guarantee quality?**
No. A permit proves that a CITES-listed material was authorised for trade. It says nothing about species purity within the allowed genus, resin content, age, or treatment.

**Why is sandalwood powder sometimes cheaper than sandalwood chips?**
Usually because the powder is a different species, a blend, or includes sapwood and milling waste. Genuine *Santalum album* heartwood powder is not a cheap by-product; it is the same material, milled.

**Can I trust a supplier's certificate of analysis?**
Treat an in-house COA as a statement by the seller. It becomes evidence when the method is named, the limits are numeric, the sample is identified, and the laboratory is independent or accredited.

**How long does incense powder keep?**
Six to eighteen months in airtight, cool, dry storage, depending on the material. Resins and whole woods last far longer. Powder is the form that most deserves a date label.

**Is aged sandalwood always better?**
Ageing can round off the sharp top notes and deepen the base, which many people prefer. It can also mean the oil has oxidised. "Aged" is a description of history, not a guarantee of quality.

**What is the single cheapest check on a new material?**
Ask for the botanical name and the plant part, then compare the price to the market for that species and part. Most misdescription fails at that comparison before any test is needed.

## Summary

Materials are not sorted by how they smell. They are sorted by how they fail. Establish identity, then form, then grade, then treatment, and run the cheapest test that can falsify the claim. Substitution is normal; concealment is the fraud. A certificate is a scope document, a grade word is a measurement or a decoration, and "natural" is an origin story that answers none of the four questions.

> **Direct answer:** 芥子 (jièzǐ) is the seed of a cabbage-family mustard — most commonly the pale seed of white mustard (*Sinapis alba*) or the darker seed of brown mustard (*Brassica juncea*). Nothing about it is fragrant in the ordinary sense: the seed is odourless until it is crushed and moistened, at which point an enzyme unlocks a sharp, rising heat. It is a kitchen material, and the *Bencao Gangmu* files it with the vegetables, praising the white seed (白芥子) as "甚辛美" — very pungent and fine.

Two rows in that table carry most of the page. The first is the trigger: water, not heat, is what releases the smell. The second is the role: this is a kitchen material that happens to sit in the same broad Chinese category as aromatic substances used with food, and it never entered the burned-incense repertoire.

## What the name 芥子 actually covers

**Direct answer:** 芥子 names a seed type rather than one species. Three mustards matter historically — white (*Sinapis alba*), brown or leaf (*Brassica juncea*) and black (*Brassica nigra*) — and Chinese usage separates the white seed as 白芥子 while calling the leafy vegetable 芥菜 and the prepared condiment 芥末.

The genus boundaries have moved over time, which is why the botanical names look inconsistent across older texts. White mustard has been placed in *Sinapis* and, in some treatments, inside *Brassica*; brown mustard sits in *Brassica*. The practical consequence for a reader is small but worth stating: a label that gives only "mustard" is not telling you which plant you have, and the difference is not cosmetic.

White mustard is native to the Mediterranean basin and western Asia. Its seed is larger and paler than the brown type, and its heat is milder and slower to arrive. Brown mustard, *Brassica juncea*, has been cultivated across Asia for so long that its wild range is hard to reconstruct; Flora of China treats it as a cultivated annual, sometimes naturalised, grown both for the seed and for the leaf vegetable 芥菜. Black mustard, *Brassica nigra*, is the species that dominated European mustard-making until the twentieth century and still appears in blends; its seed is small, dark and sharply pungent.

| Name | Plant | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| 芥子 (jièzǐ) | The seed, generically | The material this page describes |
| 白芥子 (bái jièzǐ) | White mustard seed | Milder, paler, the classic table mustard base |
| 芥菜 (jiècài) | The leafy form of *B. juncea* | A vegetable, not a spice — same plant, different crop |
| 芥末 (jièmò) | The prepared paste or powder | The condiment, heat already developed or waiting for water |

That last row is the one that causes the most confusion in translation. 芥末 is not a synonym for the seed; it is the finished or semi-finished product, and its pungency depends on how it was made. A jar of 芥末 can be mild or fierce for reasons that have nothing to do with the seed's quality.

The leaf crop deserves a sentence of its own because it explains part of the plant's economic history. 芥菜 is grown for greens, stems and pickles across southern China and Southeast Asia, and the same species also yields the seed. A farmer's decision between leaf and seed is a decision about which market to serve, not about botany. The two products travel through different supply chains even when they come from the same field.

## How the plant is grown and harvested

**Direct answer:** Mustards are fast annuals: sown in spring or autumn, they flower within weeks, set their seed in slender pods, and are harvested once those pods dry. The seed is the crop, and everything about the harvest is organised around getting it dry without splitting the pods in the field.

The plant is a classic brassica in structure. A rosette of leaves, then a flowering stem, then four-petalled yellow flowers arranged in a cross — the old name for the family, Cruciferae, comes from that cross. Pollination is largely by insects, though the plants self-pollinate readily, which is one reason they have travelled so widely as crops.

The fruit is a silique: a narrow pod with a central partition, carrying a row of small round seeds on each side. When the pod ripens it turns from green to straw, then brown, and the seeds inside darken and harden. Timing the harvest is the grower's main decision. Cut too early and the seed is shrivelled and low in oil; leave it too long and the pods shatter, scattering the crop.

Harvesting is therefore a compromise between ripeness and loss. Growers cut or swath the plants when most pods have turned colour and let them finish drying in the field or under cover, then thresh. The seed is cleaned, graded and stored dry. From that point the material's whole future depends on moisture: dry seed is stable, damp seed heats, moulds or sprouts.

The agronomy explains a quality fact that would otherwise seem arbitrary. Seed from a hot, dry finish tends to be sound and well filled; seed from a wet harvest is more likely to carry mould or to have heated in storage. A buyer cannot see the difference reliably, which is why the wet test and a trustworthy supplier matter more than a photograph of the sack.

Mustard is also grown as a cover crop and a green manure, and as an oilseed. Those uses are adjacent to the spice trade rather than identical with it, but they explain why the crop is so widely established: the same plant can be harvested for leaf, for seed, for oil, or simply ploughed back into the soil.

## Why a dry seed smells of almost nothing

**Direct answer:** The seed does not store a smell; it stores two stable things that make a smell when combined. Glucosinolates sit in the cells as inert precursors, myrosinase sits apart as an enzyme, and only crushing plus moisture brings them into contact.

The sequence is worth following in order, because every practical rule on this page follows from it:

intact seed → precursors and enzyme kept in separate compartments → crushing or grinding breaches the cell walls → water mobilises myrosinase → the enzyme hydrolyses the glucosinolates → isothiocyanates form within seconds → the sharp, volatile note reaches the nose → it fades as the compounds escape or react.

The chemistry differs by species. Brown and black mustard are dominated by sinigrin, which yields allyl isothiocyanate — the aggressive, horseradish-like compound that catches in the sinuses. White mustard carries sinalbin, which yields a different isothiocyanate, far less volatile and correspondingly gentler. That single difference explains most of what cooks notice: brown seed hits fast and hard, white seed arrives late and soft, and a blend of the two produces a heat with a beginning, a middle and a fade.

Isothiocyanates are small, volatile and chemically reactive. They are also irritants, which is not an accident of nature but the point: the seed is defending itself against being eaten, and the defence works on the eyes and airways of anything that crushes it. A cook who has ground fresh mustard and leaned too close knows the effect.

The dry seed, by contrast, is almost inert. This is the fact that most surprises people who know mustard only as a condiment. Sniff a bowl of whole seed and you get a faint, dusty, oily smell at most. The pungency is potential, not actual, and the difference between the two states is the difference between a locked door and an open one.

## Water, heat, acid and fat: the four controls

**Direct answer:** Four variables decide how much heat a given batch of mustard delivers: how much water reaches the crushed seed, how warm that water is, how acidic the mixture becomes, and how much fat is present. Each one acts on the enzyme reaction rather than on the seed itself.

Water is the trigger. Without it, nothing happens, which is why dry mustard powder can sit in a jar for months and still be formidable when mixed. Add cold water and the reaction starts within seconds and continues for several minutes, building toward a peak. Add too little water and the reaction stalls; add too much and the products are diluted.

Temperature cuts both ways. Warm water speeds the enzyme at first, then denatures it as the temperature rises further. The practical result is a curve rather than a straight line: lukewarm water gives a fast, strong development, while near-boiling water can stop the enzyme before much isothiocyanate has formed. This is the mechanism behind an old kitchen trick:

hot liquid → myrosinase denatured → hydrolysis halted early → less isothiocyanate in the paste → a milder mustard from the same seed.

Acid works in the other direction, more slowly. Vinegar, wine or lemon juice lowers the pH and slows the enzyme, which is why a mustard made with vinegar develops its heat gradually and holds a brighter, sharper profile than one mixed with water alone. A cook who wants a fierce mustard mixes with water first and adds the acid later; one who wants a mild, stable condiment adds the acid early.

Fat does not stop the reaction, but it changes how the products are experienced. Isothiocyanates are soluble in oil, so a mustard made with oil carries the heat differently — softer on the first taste, longer on the finish. The oil also extracts aroma compounds from the seed that water alone would leave behind.

| Variable | Effect on the enzyme | Practical result |
|---|---|---|
| More water | Activates and mobilises | Stronger heat, up to a point |
| Hot water | Denatures the enzyme | Milder paste, faster fade |
| Acid | Slows the enzyme | Delayed, brighter heat |
| Fat | Dissolves the products | Softer attack, longer finish |
| Grinding finer | More cells broken | Faster, more complete development |

The table is a set of levers, not a recipe. The same seed can produce a sharp condiment or a gentle one depending on which levers are pulled, and a taster who judges the seed by one preparation is judging the preparation as much as the material.

## Three forms, three behaviours

**Direct answer:** Mustard moves through commerce as whole seed, ground powder, prepared paste and pressed oil. Each form holds the potential differently, and the form a buyer chooses should follow from how soon the heat needs to appear.

Whole seed is the most durable form. The enzyme and the precursors remain separated inside intact cells, so the potential survives storage that would ruin a powder. It is also the form that gives a cook the most control: grind coarse for texture, fine for a smooth paste, and decide the liquid separately. Pickling and tempering use whole seed precisely because the heat arrives slowly and unevenly, which is often what a dish wants.

Ground powder is convenience with a clock attached. Once the cells are broken, the precursors and the enzyme are in contact, and any moisture that reaches the powder starts the reaction prematurely. A powder that has been open in a humid kitchen for months has already spent part of its potential. The smell of the powder is a poor guide, because the volatile products disperse while the precursors may still be present.

Prepared paste is the finished argument. Water, acid, salt and often colour have already been added, the reaction has run, and what remains is a stable condiment with a set level of heat. The cook's control is gone, which is why paste is an ingredient in its own right rather than a substitute for powder.

Pressed oil is a different material again. Mustard oil is mostly a fixed oil pressed from the seed, and its pungency depends on whether enzyme action happened before or during pressing. Distilled "mustard essential oil" is another thing entirely — a concentrate of allyl isothiocyanate, extremely powerful and not interchangeable with the culinary oil. The two are sold under names close enough to confuse a shopper, and the confusion is worth flagging.

| Form | What it holds | Shelf behaviour | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole seed | Intact cells, full potential | Long, if dry | Pickling, tempering, coarse grinding |
| Ground powder | Precursors plus enzyme, mixed | Months, declining with humidity | Pastes, sauces, blends |
| Prepared paste | Finished heat, stabilised | Stable, but fixed | Table condiment, sandwiches, dressings |
| Pressed oil | Fixed oil, some volatiles | Long, but the aroma fades | Cooking, dressings, regional dishes |

## Mustard in the kitchen: three traditions

**Direct answer:** Mustard appears in three broad culinary shapes — a tempered whole seed, a ground paste, and a pickled leaf — and each exploits a different part of its chemistry. The seed, the powder and the vegetable are not interchangeable, even when a recipe uses the same word for all three.

In South Asian cooking the whole seed is tempered in hot oil at the start of a dish. The seeds pop, darken and release a nutty, sharp aroma into the fat, which then carries it through the other ingredients. This is a use of whole seed, and its effect is closer to a spice base than to a condiment: the heat is distributed rather than concentrated, and the oil becomes the carrier.

In Europe the seed is ground and mixed with liquid to make a table condiment. The variables described earlier — water temperature, acid, fat — are the whole craft here. A mustard maker chooses a liquid, an acid and a grind, and the same seed can become a sharp, pale sauce or a dark, slow-burning paste. Colour comes from the seed and from what is added; heat comes from the reaction, not from the colour.

In China the leaf is as important as the seed. 芥菜 is salted, pickled and dried, and it appears in soups and stir-fries as a vegetable with a bitter, mustardy edge. The ground seed appears as 芥末, a paste served with cold dishes and dumplings, sharp and short-lived once mixed. The two products come from the same species and behave nothing alike.

What the three traditions share is a preference for small quantities. Mustard is assertive, and its role is corrective: it cuts through fat, lifts a bland base, and gives a dish a sharp edge that fades rather than lingering. That is a different job from the one a lead spice does, and it explains why mustard is so often paired with rich food rather than used alone.

## The Chinese record: 芥 among the vegetables

**Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) places 芥 in its vegetable section (菜之一), among cultivated food plants, and singles out the white seed as 白芥子, "甚辛美". That placement is the most important fact about the material's status: it belongs to the food-and-medicine layer of the record, not to the incense canon.

The encyclopedia's vegetable section is a catalogue of things grown to eat, with notes on varieties, cultivation and uses. 芥 appears there with several kinds described, and the white seed is picked out for the quality of its pungency. The phrase "甚辛美" is a culinary judgement — very pungent, and fine — not a pharmacological one.

This matters because Chinese aromatic vocabulary is wide. 香药, "fragrant medicine", covers a large class of aromatic substances used in food, preservation, medicine and personal scent; it is not a synonym for incense material. 香材, by contrast, names the materials of the blending tradition, and 合香 is the practice of composing them. 芥子 sits firmly on the 香药 side. A reader who sees the character 香 attached to mustard should not conclude that it was burned.

The same distinction explains why the 香乘 (Xiang Cheng), the great Ming compilation of blended-incense formulas, has no role for mustard seed. Its absence is not an omission by a careless editor. The book is organised around resins, woods, balsams and animal secretions — materials that carry a scent into smoke — and a seed whose signature compound is a water-triggered irritant does not belong to that grammar.

Traditional medicinal uses of 芥子 are recorded in the materia medica. They are treated here as historical claims, not as clinical findings. Nothing in this entry is medical advice, and no therapeutic effect is asserted.

## The boundary: a culinary spice, not an incense material

**Direct answer:** Mustard seed has no place in the classical incense repertoire, and the reason is chemical rather than cultural. It carries no resin or gum to hold a blend together, its volatile product is an irritant rather than a rounded aroma, and burning it produces acrid smoke instead of the sweet, resinous smoke that incense materials are chosen for.

Start with what incense materials have in common. Woods such as sandalwood and agarwood carry aromatic resins in a fibrous matrix; gums and balsams melt and release scent slowly; animal materials fix and extend. A working blend needs materials that release volatiles gradually and leave a pleasant residue. Mustard seed offers none of that. It is a food seed with a high oil content and a defence chemistry built for deterrence.

What happens when it burns follows from the same chemistry:

seed heated → cell structure breaks down → glucosinolates and oil decompose → sulphur-containing volatiles and smoke are released → the smoke is sharp and irritant rather than sweet → nothing remains to carry a base note.

That is not a description of a subtle aromatic experience. It is a description of a material doing what it evolved to do, at the wrong temperature and in the wrong context.

There is a temptation, when writing a material encyclopedia, to fill gaps by inference: the source says mustard was used in food, so perhaps it was also used in incense. This page declines that inference. The documentary record places 芥 in the vegetable section and the blending manuals ignore it. A gap in the record is evidence about the practice, and filling it with speculation would misrepresent both.

## Judging quality: what the seed can and cannot tell you

**Direct answer:** Appearance sorts seed into grades; only the fresh-grind-and-wet test measures pungency. A buyer who sniffs dry seed is testing the wrong thing, because the compound that matters does not exist yet.

The physical checks come first, because they are cheap and fast. Sound seed is dry, uniform in size, free of dust and broken fragments, and free of the musty smell that signals mould. Colour varies by species — pale yellow for white mustard, reddish-brown to dark brown for the brown types — and a batch with wide colour variation is likely a blend or a poorly sorted lot.

The functional check comes second and cannot be skipped. Grind a small quantity, add a little water, wait a minute, and smell. A strong, clean, sharp heat that catches in the nose indicates live enzyme and intact precursors. A weak, flat or dusty result indicates seed that has lost its potential, whatever it looks like.

| Check | What good looks like | What it does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Colour | Even for the species | Pungency, freshness, or species purity on its own |
| Size and uniformity | Consistent, plump seed | Enzyme activity |
| Dryness | Flows freely, no clumping | Whether the seed was stored warm |
| Dry smell | Faint, oily, clean | The heat that will develop on wetting |
| Fresh-grind-and-wet | Sharp, clean, immediate heat | Absolute strength in another preparation |
| Foreign matter | None visible | Adulteration of a later powder |

That last row is the honest limit of any visual inspection. Seed can be graded by eye, but powder cannot: once the seed is ground, colour can be adjusted and texture can be stretched. The buyer's protection is provenance and a supplier who will name the species and the batch, not a photograph.

## Storage, and the slow loss of potential

**Direct answer:** Whole seed keeps its potential for a long time because nothing is reacting; powder begins to spend it immediately. Moisture, warmth and air are the three accelerants, and they work together.

The mechanism is the same one that makes the spice work in the first place:

powder exposed to humid air → traces of water activate myrosinase in patches → isothiocyanates form and evaporate → the precursor pool shrinks → the powder smells of mustard but no longer bites.

A jar that has been opened many times in a steamy kitchen is a demonstration of this. The powder still looks right and still smells faintly of mustard, but the heat has gone flat, and no amount of adding more powder recovers the missing pungency — it only adds bulk.

The practical rules follow directly: keep whole seed in an airtight container away from light and heat, grind close to use, and treat an opened bag of powder as a material with a shelf life rather than a permanent ingredient. Whole seed stored cool and dry can hold its potential for years; powder is measured in months at best.

Temperature deserves a note of its own. A cupboard above a stove combines warmth and humidity, the two conditions that degrade the material fastest. A cool, dry drawer is better than a convenient shelf, and the difference is measurable in the wet test rather than in appearance.

## Confusion table: five things with mustard in the name

**Direct answer:** Mustard seed, mustard powder, prepared mustard, mustard oil and mustard greens are five different products, and only the first is the material this page describes. Horseradish and wasabi are not mustards at all, though they deliver a similar sensation.

| Term | What it is | Relationship to 芥子 | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mustard seed | The whole seed of *Sinapis* or *Brassica* species | The material itself | Assumed to smell strongly when dry |
| Mustard powder | Ground seed, sometimes blended | A processed form of the seed | Treated as pure seed without checking the label |
| Prepared mustard | Paste of powder, water, acid, salt and colour | A finished condiment | Used as a substitute for powder in cooking |
| Mustard oil | Oil pressed from the seed | A different fraction of the same plant | Confused with distilled mustard essential oil |
| Mustard greens | Leaves of *B. juncea* (芥菜) | Same species, different crop | Assumed to carry the seed's heat |
| Horseradish / wasabi | Roots of unrelated plants | No botanical relation | Grouped with mustard because the sensation matches |

The last two rows are the ones that produce the most correspondence from readers. 芥菜 and 芥子 are the same species but not the same food: the leaf is a vegetable with a mild, mustardy bitterness, and it does not deliver the seed's volatile heat. Horseradish and wasabi belong to different families entirely, and their pungency comes from a parallel glucosinolate system rather than from mustard.

The oil row is a genuine safety-adjacent confusion. Culinary mustard oil and distilled mustard essential oil are not interchangeable: the essential oil is a concentrate of allyl isothiocyanate, and treating it like a cooking oil would be a serious mistake. The naming is close, the materials are not.

## Pungency across the spice cabinet

**Direct answer:** Four warm spices, four chemistries. Pepper's heat is piperine, cinnamon's is cinnamaldehyde, ginger's is gingerol and its relatives, and mustard's is isothiocyanate generated on wetting. Only the last is created rather than released.

This is the comparison that makes mustard's behaviour legible. Pepper and cinnamon store their pungent compounds ready-made; crushing or heating releases them. Ginger stores gingerols, which change into shogaols as the root is dried or cooked, so its heat shifts character rather than simply fading. Mustard stores no pungent compound at all — it stores the machinery to make one.

| Spice | Pungent principle | Present before use? | Trigger | Behaviour in cooking |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mustard (芥子) | Isothiocyanates from glucosinolates | No | Water plus crushing | Heat appears on wetting; fades on standing |
| Pepper (胡椒) | Piperine | Yes | Crushing, heat | Stable, persistent warmth |
| Cinnamon (肉桂) | Cinnamaldehyde | Yes | Heat, grinding | Sweet warmth; volatile, lost with long cooking |
| Ginger (姜) | Gingerols, converted to shogaols | Yes | Crushing, drying, heat | Changes character as it dries or cooks |

The table also explains why "hot" is a poor category. A dish seasoned with pepper, cinnamon, ginger and mustard is seasoned four times over, in four different registers, and substituting one for another changes the dish rather than repeating it. A formulator or a cook who treats them as interchangeable heat sources loses exactly the information that makes each useful.

## Pairing logic: why mustard cuts fat and sweetness

**Direct answer:** Mustard's heat is volatile and short, which makes it a counterweight rather than a foundation. It cuts richness because the sharp volatiles rise at the front of the nose, and it balances sweetness because the sharpness arrives before the sugar does.

The mechanism is simple enough to state as a chain:

fat coats the tongue → volatile isothiocyanates rise through the nose → the sharp note arrives before the fat's weight is registered → the dish reads lighter → the richness is still there but no longer dominant.

That is why mustard accompanies fatty meats, oily fish, cheese and fried food across many cuisines. It is not neutralising the fat; it is interrupting the perception of it. The same logic explains why mustard pairs well with honey and fruit: the sharp note lands first, the sweetness answers it, and the two together read as more complex than either alone.

The practical consequence for a cook is that mustard should be added late when its sharpness is wanted and early when it is meant to mellow. Heat drives off the volatile products, so a mustard stirred into a hot sauce at the end keeps its edge, while one cooked from the start becomes a background acidity with a faint bitterness.

There is a limit to this, and it is the same limit that keeps mustard out of incense. The compound that makes the seed useful in food is volatile, reactive and irritating in concentration. In a dish that is a feature. In smoke it is a problem, which is why the culinary case and the incense case point in opposite directions.

## Safety and smoke

**Direct answer:** Mustard seed is an ordinary food ingredient, and the safety questions that matter here concern concentrated volatiles and combustion smoke, not the spice on a plate.

Isothiocyanates are irritants by design. In a kitchen they are mild and brief; in concentrated form they sting the eyes, the nose and the airways. Grinding large quantities in a closed room is unpleasant rather than dangerous for most people, but the principle is worth keeping: the compound is a defence chemical, and it behaves like one. Distilled mustard oil deserves particular respect, and it should never be treated as a culinary oil.

Burning any plant material, mustard included, produces smoke and fine particulate matter. The precautions are the general ones: ventilate, keep a distance from the burner, do not inhale concentrated smoke, and keep burning material away from children and animals. The [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case. Mustard is not a traditional incense material, so there is no ritual practice to describe — and there is no reason to invent one.

Nothing on this page is medical advice. Traditional medicinal uses recorded in the materia medica are reported as historical claims, and no therapeutic benefit is asserted or implied.

## Reading a mustard label

**Direct answer:** Mustard labels describe a style and a grind more often than they name a species, so the ingredient list is the only reliable guide. "Yellow", "brown", "wholegrain", "Dijon-style" and "English" are words about preparation and colour, not botanical guarantees.

The vocabulary is small and worth learning. "Yellow mustard" usually signals a mild condiment built on white seed, often with turmeric for colour. "Brown mustard" points to *Brassica juncea*, the sharper seed. "Black mustard" is rarer now but still names *Brassica nigra*. "Wholegrain" means the seed was crushed rather than milled to flour, so the heat arrives unevenly and the texture stays coarse. "Dijon-style" describes a method and a smooth grind; it does not name a protected seed or a single recipe. "English mustard" describes a hot powder or paste, typically from a blend, mixed with water rather than vinegar.

The ingredient list is where the real information sits. A jar that lists mustard powder, water, vinegar, salt and turmeric is telling you the whole construction: the seed, the liquid that triggered it, the acid that tempered it, the salt that preserved it and the colour that made it yellow. A jar that lists "mustard" alone has told you less, because the word can mean seed, powder or prepared condiment depending on the product.

Two label habits cause most of the confusion. The first is treating colour as a heat signal: yellow is a colour, not a pungency grade, and a bright yellow mustard can be fierce or mild. The second is reading "natural" as a statement about the seed, when it is usually a statement about the absence of certain additives.

For a buyer, the practical rule is to read the list rather than the front of the jar. The front sells a style; the list records what is inside. Where the two disagree, the list is the document.

## FAQ

**What is mustard seed (芥子)?** A small oil-bearing seed from one of the cabbage-family mustards — white (*Sinapis alba*) or brown (*Brassica juncea*) most often. Cooks use it as a condiment, the Chinese record treats it as a food and medicine material, and nobody in the classical tradition burned it as incense.

**Why does dry mustard seed have almost no smell?** Because the pungent compounds do not exist in the intact seed. Glucosinolates and the enzyme myrosinase are kept apart in the cells, and only crushing plus water brings them together. Until then the seed is a locked box, and sniffing it tells you about the box, not the contents.

**Why does hot water make mustard milder?** Heat denatures myrosinase. If the enzyme is destroyed before it can hydrolyse the glucosinolates, fewer isothiocyanates form, and the paste is milder even though the seed was the same. Cold or lukewarm water gives the enzyme time to work; near-boiling water shuts it down.

**Is mustard a classical incense ingredient?** No. The *Bencao Gangmu* places 芥 among the vegetables, and the 香乘 and the 合香 tradition have no role for the seed. Its volatile product is a water-triggered irritant, and burning it yields acrid smoke rather than the resinous sweetness incense materials are chosen for.

**What is the difference between white and brown mustard?** White mustard (*Sinapis alba*) has a larger, paler seed and a milder, slower heat from sinalbin; brown or leaf mustard (*Brassica juncea*) has a smaller, darker seed and a sharper, faster heat from sinigrin. The white seed is the classic base for pale table mustard; the brown seed is behind many Asian mustards and the vegetable 芥菜.

## Summary

A cabbage-family seed with a defence chemistry inside it: that is the short version of 芥子. White mustard (*Sinapis alba*) and brown mustard (*Brassica juncea*) supply most of the world's crop, and their seeds behave unlike any other warm spice in the cabinet. The seed's heat is not stored but generated: glucosinolates and myrosinase sit apart in the intact seed, and crushing plus water brings them together to release isothiocyanates. Dry seed is nearly scentless; wet, ground seed is fierce; hot liquid shuts the reaction down and acid slows it. The *Bencao Gangmu* records 芥 among the vegetables and praises 白芥子 as "甚辛美", which places the material in the food-and-medicine layer of the Chinese record rather than in the incense canon. There is no 合香 role for it, and the absence is chemical as much as documentary: a water-triggered irritant with no resin to carry a blend does not belong in a burned offering. What remains is a precise, useful culinary material whose quality is measured by a fresh-grind-and-wet test rather than by appearance.

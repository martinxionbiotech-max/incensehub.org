**Technical answer:** Wintersweet is the bloom of *Chimonanthus praecox*, a Chinese shrub in the small family Calycanthaceae that opens its flowers in the coldest weeks of the year. The scent is sweet, honeyed, and faintly waxy, and it comes from flowers borne on wood grown the season before. The plant is a garden ornamental first and an aromatic material second, and in incense work it appears far more often as a reconstructed floral accord than as harvested flower. The name is a trap: 蜡梅 means "wax plum," and the plant is not a plum at all.

## What the flower actually is

> **Direct answer:** Wintersweet is a flower, not a wood, resin, or fruit — and specifically a flower built from tepals rather than from separate sepals and petals. Its scent comes from the living bloom, which is why the material behaves like a cut flower in the supply chain: perishable, seasonal, and difficult to standardise.

Most familiar flowers divide their perianth into a green calyx and a coloured corolla. Wintersweet does not. Flora of China records 15–21 tepals — one undifferentiated set of petal-like parts, spirally arranged, yellow, with the inner ones usually carrying purplish-red pigment at the base. That single botanical fact explains a good deal of the flower's look: the bloom reads as a cluster of thick, slightly translucent petals because that is effectively what it is, without the crisp separation of sepal and petal that a rose or a plum shows.

The tepals are also the reason the "wax" comparison stuck. They are thick, slightly translucent, and cool to the touch, closer to the surface of a candle than to the tissue-paper thinness of a plum petal. A flower that looks carved from a soft solid invites a name about wax, and the Chinese name records exactly that impression.

Size is modest. Flowers run 1.5–4 cm across — large enough to be seen on a bare branch in January, small enough that a handful of blooms is a handful rather than a bouquet. The scent is concentrated in that small area, which is part of why the plant is grown near paths and doorways rather than in the middle of a lawn.

## The shrub behind the flower

> **Direct answer:** *Chimonanthus praecox* is a deciduous, occasionally persistent-leaved shrub or small tree recorded at 3–13 m tall. It flowers on wood produced the previous season, generally before the leaves emerge, and it holds its bloom through the coldest part of the year.

The habit is worth knowing because it shapes how the plant is used in a garden and how flowers are picked. A 3–13 m shrub can be trained as a specimen, grown against a wall, or cut back hard and kept low; the flowering wood is always last year's growth, so pruning that removes it removes the coming winter's bloom. That single rule — prune after flowering, never before — is the most common practical mistake made with the plant.

The leaves are opposite and entire, and they appear after the flowers in the normal sequence. Flowering before leafing is an old strategy among early-season trees and shrubs: the blooms are visible and accessible while nothing obscures them, and the plant does not spend resources on foliage before the reproductive event. For a gardener, the effect is a bare grey-brown branch line studded with small yellow flowers, which is a far more striking sight in February than the same flowers would be inside a canopy.

The species sits in Calycanthaceae, a small family with a scattered distribution — east Asia and North America — and a set of shared traits that includes aromatic bark and a floral structure built from tepals rather than differentiated petals. That family placement is the whole answer to the plum question, and it is the one piece of taxonomy a buyer should carry.

## Why it blooms in deep winter

> **Direct answer:** Wintersweet flowers on the previous year's wood, from buds formed well before winter, and it opens them in the coldest window of the year. The sequence is a strategy rather than an accident: pre-formed buds, no leaf canopy, and a scent that lingers in still cold air all point the same way.

**Chain one — the seasonal sequence.** Buds form on the current season's growth → those buds overwinter on the wood → the plant opens them while temperatures are low → the flowers appear before the new leaves → nothing shades or competes with the bloom → the display is concentrated and visible on bare wood. A shrub that flowered in May would have the same flowers, and they would disappear into green.

**Chain two — the scent problem in cold air.** Cold slows evaporation → volatile molecules leave the flower slowly → they stay concentrated in the thin layer of air around the bloom → a passer-by who leans close gets a dense, almost syrupy impression → the scent reads as stronger than its actual output. This is why wintersweet is described as carrying well in cold air while a measured headspace analysis would show a modest release rate. The impression and the measurement are both true.

**Chain three — the garden logic.** Few plants bloom in the depth of winter → a shrub in flower at that moment has no competition for attention → gardeners plant it where it will be encountered on a daily path → the association between the scent and the coldest weeks forms in memory → the flower becomes a seasonal marker as much as an ornamental. That is a cultural mechanism rather than a botanical one, and it explains why the plant carries more emotional weight than its size or rarity would suggest.

The practical consequence for anyone working with the flower is that its season is short and its supply is not scalable. You cannot order wintersweet in July and expect flowers. You can order an accord that smells of it in any month, which is a different product with a different honesty requirement.

## The name: wax, month, and a plum that is not one

> **Direct answer:** 蜡梅 ("wax plum") names the waxy, translucent quality of the tepals; 腊梅 ties the same plant to 腊月, the twelfth lunar month when it blooms. Both spellings are current, both describe one species, and neither implies any relationship to the true plum.

Written Chinese preserves the ambiguity neatly. The character 蜡 refers to wax — the substance, the candle material, the soft translucent solid. The character 腊 refers to the twelfth lunar month and to the cured-meat preparations associated with it. Spoken, the two are indistinguishable. Written, they record two different ways of thinking about the same flower: one visual and textural, one calendrical.

The "梅" component is where readers go wrong. In 蜡梅 it functions as a descriptive convenience — a flower that, at a glance, resembles the blossom of a plum. In 梅花 it is the actual name of the actual plum, *Prunus mume*, in the rose family. The two plants share a season, a character, and a general impression of small flowers on bare wood. They share no close ancestry.

That is not a trivial pedantry for an aromatic material. 梅花 and 蜡梅 smell different, behave differently in a blend, and belong to different supply chains. A formula that calls for one and receives the other will be wrong in a way that no amount of adjustment fixes, because the substitution changes the family of the scent rather than its strength.

## Wintersweet and plum blossom: a disambiguation

> **Direct answer:** 蜡梅 is *Chimonanthus praecox* in Calycanthaceae, with a honeyed, waxy, slightly creamy scent. 梅花 is *Prunus mume* in Rosaceae, with a lighter, almondy, cool floral scent. They bloom in the same months, carry the same character in their names, and are otherwise unrelated.

| Feature | Wintersweet (蜡梅) | Plum blossom (梅花) |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Chimonanthus praecox* | *Prunus mume* |
| Family | Calycanthaceae | Rosaceae |
| Habit | Shrub or small tree, 3–13 m | Small tree, typically much shorter |
| Flower | 15–21 tepals, thick and waxy, yellow with purplish-red inner markings | Five rounded petals, white to pink, thin and papery |
| Scent | Honeyed, waxy, creamy, sweet | Light, cool, almondy, faintly bitter |
| Season | October to March | Late winter into early spring |
| Fruit | Not a stone fruit | A drupe — the plum |
| Cultural frame | Winter garden ornamental, New Year cut branches | One of the 岁寒三友, a scholar's emblem |

The table's last row is the one most often missed. The 岁寒三友 (suìhán sānyǒu, "three friends of the cold season") are pine, bamboo, and plum — and the plum in that trio is 梅花, the true plum, not wintersweet. Wintersweet has its own considerable garden reputation, particularly as a cut branch for New Year displays, but it is not the plant in that particular emblem. Conflating the two is easy, common, and wrong.

For a buyer, the reliable identifier is the species name on the paperwork. For a reader standing in a garden, the separator is the flower itself: thick, waxy, many-petalled yellow blooms with a heavy honeyed scent mean wintersweet; five thin petals with a cool, light fragrance mean plum.

## What it smells like

> **Direct answer:** Sweet and honeyed, with a thick, almost creamy floral body and a waxy undertone. Set against plum blossom it feels heavier, warmer, and less airy — a honey-floral rather than a fresh green one — and in composition it reads as a soft winter bloom rather than a bright spring bouquet.

The word "waxy" in the aroma description is not a chemical statement. It describes a texture in the smell — a certain opacity, a sense of a scent that has body rather than sparkle. Honeyed florals such as osmanthus share part of that quality; so do some honeysuckle accords. What distinguishes wintersweet is the combination: the honey sweetness sits on top of a faintly resinous, almost sappy base, so the flower never reads as purely sugary.

| Stage | Impression | Note for the reader |
|---|---|---|
| Flower on the shrub, cold morning | Dense honey, clean and slightly cool | The classic experience; strongest at close range |
| Flower in a warm room | Sweeter, heavier, faster to fade | Warmth pushes release and shortens the bloom's life |
| Dried flower | Muted honey, hay, a papery edge | The waxy top note is what dries off first |
| Reconstructed accord | Honeyed floral with a creamy body | Usually smoother and more uniform than the flower |
| In a blend, low dose | Rounded sweetness under a woody or resinous centre | Reads as warmth rather than as a distinct floral |

The absence of a sharp green or citrus facet is what makes the flower easy to place. Spring florals often carry a green edge that keeps them lively; wintersweet does not. It sits in the sweet-floral register with osmanthus and honeysuckle rather than in the fresh-floral register with neroli or lily of the valley.

## How the scent behaves, and why that matters commercially

> **Direct answer:** The flower's scent is concentrated, low-volatility, and short-lived once picked — a combination that makes it a poor bulk material and a good memory. Every commercial decision about wintersweet follows from that mismatch between what the nose remembers and what the supply chain can deliver.

Fresh bloom holds its character for a short window. Cut branches in a cool room last days; loose flowers in a warm room fade faster. Drying preserves a honeyed, hay-like echo but strips the waxy top note that makes the scent recognisable. A tincture or an extracted absolute can capture more of the living flower, and it remains a specialist, small-volume operation rather than a commodity stream.

That is the honest shape of the material. It is not scarce in the way agarwood is scarce, because the plant is common in gardens. It is scarce in the way a cut flower is scarce: available in its season, in the quantities a garden produces, and not in the quantities a factory requires.

The mismatch has a predictable result in the market. A fragrance house that wants a wintersweet note will build it rather than buy it, using honeyed floral materials and a creamy base, because a reconstructed accord is consistent, available year-round, and cheaper than a seasonal harvest of small flowers. The natural flower continues to exist as a garden experience and as a niche artisan material. Both are legitimate; they are simply not the same product, and the label should say which one is in the bottle.

## Harvest and handling of the natural flower

> **Direct answer:** Pick flowers at opening, in the cool part of the day, and handle them like cut flowers rather than like a dried herb. The tepals are thin in substance and high in moisture, so they bruise, brown, and lose their top note quickly once detached from the branch.

The practical sequence for a small grower runs roughly like this: choose branches with flowers at or just past opening, cut in the morning while the air is cool, keep the cut ends in water if the flowers are to be used within a day, and strip the blooms gently when they are needed rather than in advance. Flowers picked in the heat of the afternoon have already spent part of their volatile load and will read flatter.

Drying is the difficult step. A thin, moist tepal dried fast in heat goes brown and papery; dried slowly in moving air it keeps a pale colour and a muted honey note. Either way, the waxy top note — the part that makes the flower recognisable — is the first thing lost. That is not a failure of technique but a property of the material: the chemicals that produce the waxy impression are the most volatile fraction, and drying removes them.

Storage follows the same logic. Dried blooms go into a sealed jar away from light, and they are used within a season. They will not improve with age, and a musty jar of last year's flowers is a common disappointment for anyone who treats them like a dried herb with a long shelf life.

## Growing it for scent: placement, pruning, and the home harvest

> **Direct answer:** Plant wintersweet where you will walk past it in winter, give it sun and drainage, and prune immediately after flowering rather than before. The plant is easy to grow; the difficulty is timing, because the flower buds sit on wood that a careless autumn cut would remove.

Placement is the decision that determines whether the shrub earns its space. A wintersweet in the middle of a lawn is a modest winter curiosity. The same plant beside a gate, under a window that opens, or along the walk from a door to a car becomes part of daily life, because the scent is encountered at close range in the season when there is nothing else to smell. The plant wants sun for good flowering and well-drained soil; wet ground in winter is the usual reason a young specimen sulks.

**The pruning chain.** Flowers open on growth made the previous season → that growth carries the buds through summer and autumn → cutting it in autumn or winter removes the coming bloom → the correct window is straight after flowering, before new growth hardens → the new shoots ripen, set buds, and carry the next winter's flowers. This is why the plant punishes tidy autumn gardening and rewards a slightly untidy spring.

The home harvest follows the same calendar. Cut branches when the buds are swelling and the first flowers have opened, take wood that carries a good number of buds rather than the longest stem available, and condition the cut ends in water. Branches cut in bud will continue to open indoors over several days, which extends the experience well beyond what a bowl of loose flowers can offer.

One caution belongs here rather than in the safety section, because it is a garden decision. A shrub that has been sprayed, or one growing beside a road with heavy traffic film on the leaves and flowers, is not a good source for material that will be handled closely or burned. The home harvest is worth doing when you know what has been applied to the plant and what the air around it carries.

## Forms, and what each one is actually good for

> **Direct answer:** Wintersweet reaches users as fresh flowers, as small lots of dried bloom, as a specialist extract or tincture, and — most often — as a reconstructed fragrance accord. Each form carries a different claim, and the accord is the form most people actually encounter without knowing it.

| Form | What it is | Best use | Honest limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh flower | Cut bloom in season | Garden enjoyment; immediate, close-range scent | Days of life; no scalability |
| Dried flower | Air- or low-heat-dried tepals | Potpourri, niche sachets, decorative blends | Loses the waxy top note; browns easily |
| Tincture or extract | Solvent capture of the fresh flower | Perfumery experiments, small batches | Variable, batch-dependent, short-lived |
| Reconstructed accord | A built honeyed-waxy floral | Consistent incense and fragrance work | Not the flower; must be labelled as an accord |
| Scented ornamental | Flowers in gardens, cut branches | Seasonal experience, cultural use | Not a traded material at all |

Two rows in that table carry most of the confusion. The reconstructed accord is what a "wintersweet incense" almost always contains, and saying so is not a criticism — accords are how perfumery has always worked. What is not acceptable is presenting an accord as a natural harvest, because the two carry different expectations about seasonality, variability, and price.

The dried-flower row is the other trap. Gardeners who dry a few blooms and find them disappointing often conclude that the material is weak. The material is fine; drying simply removes the fraction that made it distinctive. A dried wintersweet is a pleasant hay-and-honey note, not a preserved winter garden.

## Natural flower or accord: a decision frame

> **Direct answer:** Choose the natural flower when the season, the locality, and the story are the point. Choose an accord when consistency, repeatability, and year-round supply are the point. Choosing without deciding which of those matters is how disappointing product lines get built.

| Your situation | Recommended form | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Heritage or seasonal gift line | Fresh or dried flower, in season | The scarcity is the offer; a February-only product can say so |
| Year-round incense SKU | Accord | A natural supply cannot support a twelve-month catalogue |
| Perfumery study of the flower | Tincture or extract, plus fresh reference | You need the living smell as a benchmark, not just a rebuild |
| Garden workshop or demonstration | Fresh cut branches | The point is the encounter, not the material yield |
| Blended stick with a winter theme | Accord at low dose | Natural flower would be invisible and uneconomic in a stick |

The decision table also exposes a marketing trap. A brand that sells "wintersweet" year-round is selling an accord, whether or not it says so. That is normal practice. A brand that sells a February-limited natural-flower product is selling something genuinely different, and should charge and label accordingly. The failure mode is a year-round product with a seasonal story attached — the customer buys the story and receives the accord, and the mismatch eventually surfaces.

## A quality screen for flowers and accords

> **Direct answer:** For fresh flowers, judge freshness, colour, and scent at close range. For dried flowers, judge colour retention, absence of mould, and the honesty of the aroma. For accords, judge whether the composition reads as a flower or as a generic sweet floral — and ask what it is built from.

| Criterion | Fresh flower | Dried flower | Accord |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | Clear yellow, inner markings distinct | Pale yellow to cream, not brown | Not applicable |
| Scent | Honeyed, waxy, dense at close range | Muted honey and hay | Honeyed top, creamy body, no harsh solvent edge |
| Condition | Firm tepals, no browning at the edges | Dry, intact, no clumping | Consistent batch to batch |
| Mould | None | None — a musty note is a reject | Not applicable |
| Honesty | Sold in season | Sold as a dried ornamental, not as a scent match | Declared as an accord, not as a natural harvest |

The accord column deserves a note on how to read a fragrance description. Words like "honeyed floral" and "waxy" describe an effect, not an ingredient. A well-built accord will name its character honestly and will not claim a harvest. A poorly built one leans on the flower's reputation while delivering a generic sweet-floral base that could as easily be labelled honeysuckle.

## In a blend: role, dose, and pairing

> **Direct answer:** Wintersweet is an accent, never a base. In 君臣佐使 (jun-chen-zuo-shi) terms it acts as 佐 (zuo, assistant) or 使 (shi, guide): it rounds a composition and lends a seasonal signature, while the chief material supplies the structure. Dosed high, it flattens into a generic sweetness and stops reading as wintersweet at all.

The reason is structural rather than stylistic. A chief material has to project, persist, and define the blend. A honeyed floral with a soft, low-intensity profile does none of those things for long. What it does well is modify: it warms a dry woody composition, softens a resinous edge, or gives a floral blend a winter frame that a spring floral cannot supply.

| Partner | Effect of the pairing | Dose guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood | Creamy base receives the honey; a soft, warm winter accord | Wintersweet as a clear accent over a sandalwood chief |
| Cedar | Dry wood sharpens the floral; good for a cold, clean profile | Keep the floral low; cedar dominates easily |
| Osmanthus | Honeyed florals reinforce each other; risks becoming one-note sweet | Use one as the lead, the other as a shadow |
| Magnolia flower | Adds a waxy, lemon-tinged lift to the honey | Small dose; magnolia is more assertive |
| Daphne | Both are winter flowers with a sweet, almost heady character | Blend with restraint; the two can compete |
| Plum blossom | The natural comparison; lighter and cooler | Use plum for freshness, wintersweet for warmth |

The pairing table makes the point that wintersweet is a *seasonal* material rather than a *structural* one. It tells the nose what time of year it is. That is a real function in composition, and it is the function most often lost when the material is dosed as though it were a rose or a jasmine.

## Where it sits in the floral family

> **Direct answer:** Wintersweet belongs to the sweet-floral group, alongside osmanthus, honeysuckle, and magnolia, and it sits at the warm, creamy end of that group. It is not a fresh floral, not a green floral, and not a white floral in the tuberose or gardenia sense.

| Group | Members | Shared character | Wintersweet's position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sweet honeyed florals | Osmanthus, honeysuckle, wintersweet | Rounded sweetness, low green edge | Warmest and waxiest of the group |
| Waxy white florals | Tuberose, gardenia, champaca | Thick, heavy, indolic | Wintersweet is lighter and less animalic |
| Fresh citrus florals | Neroli, orange blossom | Bright, sharp, clean | Wintersweet has none of the sharpness |
| Cool spring florals | Plum blossom, violet, lily of the valley | Light, airy, almondy | Wintersweet is heavier and slower |
| Resinous florals | Labdanum-floral accords, frankincense with rose | Balsamic, incense-adjacent | Wintersweet can bridge toward this group |

Read across the rows and a working rule appears: wintersweet pairs most naturally with materials that already share its warmth, and it disappears beside anything bright. A citrus-floral composition will bury it. A woody-resinous one will give it a frame and let it be heard.

## In Chinese tradition

> **Direct answer:** Wintersweet is a garden plant with a long cultural presence in central and eastern China, valued for flowering in the weeks when almost nothing else does. Its blooms are cut for New Year displays, its name is bound to the lunar month, and its image belongs to the domestic and garden side of Chinese winter culture rather than to the incense workshop.

The garden role is the primary one, and it is worth describing accurately rather than inflating. A shrub that flowers in January and February, on bare wood, with a scent that carries across a courtyard, is a plant that earns its place in a small garden. It is planted near a window, beside a path, or against a south-facing wall where the winter sun reaches it. The flowers are cut as branches and brought indoors for the New Year period, where they sit in a vase as a seasonal marker rather than as a luxury.

That custom — cut branches for the New Year display, part of the broader tradition of 岁朝清供 (suìzhāo qīnggòng, "pure offerings for the New Year") — is the plant's most durable cultural use. It is domestic, inexpensive, and repeated annually. The flower is not rare and is not treated as rare; its value is timing.

Poetry and painting took the plant up on the same terms. Winter-flowering subjects gave painters a way to represent endurance and quiet, and the yellow bloom on bare branches is a natural subject for that kind of image. The cultural weight is real, and it is the weight of a seasonal emblem rather than of a precious substance.

## The 岁寒三友 misconception

> **Direct answer:** The "three friends of the cold season" are pine, bamboo, and plum — and that plum is 梅花, *Prunus mume*, not wintersweet. Wintersweet is a winter-flowering garden plant with its own reputation, but it is not a member of that trio, and treating it as one misreads both the emblem and the botany.

This is the misconception most worth correcting on this page, because it is repeated in good faith by people who know the plant well. The confusion runs through the shared character 梅, through the shared season, and through the fact that both plants are described in English as "plum" something. The trio's plum is the true plum, the one that fruits, the one in Rosaceae.

The correction matters beyond pedantry. The 岁寒三友 emblem carries specific associations — resilience, integrity, the scholar's character — that attach to the plum in that trio. Wintersweet carries different associations: domestic winter, the New Year, the garden. Merging the two dilutes both, and it produces the kind of writing where a plant is credited with a symbolism it never held.

For an incense writer, the practical version of the correction is simple. If a formula or a description invokes the 岁寒三友, the material being invoked is 梅花. Wintersweet can be present in a winter blend on its own terms, and those terms are garden warmth rather than scholar's emblem.

> **Direct answer:** The botanical identity, the family placement, the flowering period, and the long cultivation as a fragrant ornamental are established. What the materia medica says about the flower is a historical record of traditional use, not clinical evidence, and this page makes no medical claims about it.

The cultivation note is worth spelling out. According to Flora of China the species is grown across most of China and in temperate areas elsewhere, and the same treatment cautions that centuries of planting make parts of its reported native range uncertain. That is a precise statement of a common situation: a plant so widely moved by people that its original range can no longer be read from a map of where it grows now.

The medicinal row is the one to handle carefully. The flower has a place in traditional Chinese medicine with uses described in that literature, and the place itself is a historical fact about the texts. It is not evidence that the flower does anything clinically, and a page about an aromatic material should not borrow the authority of a pharmacopoeia it cannot support.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** Burning flowers or a floral accord produces smoke and particulate matter, and the flower itself is an ornamental and aromatic material rather than a food. Use it in ventilated space, keep the dose small, and keep dried blooms dry and mould-free.

Three specific points follow from the material's nature. First, dried flowers are hygroscopic and mould-prone; a musty batch should be discarded rather than burned, because combustion does not sanitise spoiled plant material. Second, floral dust and pollen are irritants to some people, and handling a quantity of dried blooms in a closed room is not the same as passing a flowering shrub outdoors. Third, any incense smoke deserves respect: ventilation, distance, and duration matter more than the material being burned.

This page is editorial reference material, not medical advice. Households with asthma, other respiratory conditions, or birds and other sensitive animals should apply more caution than the general guidance. Our [safety guide](/safety/) sets out the ventilation and air-quality basics.

## FAQ

**What plant is 蜡梅, exactly?** A shrub or small tree of the species *Chimonanthus praecox*, placed in Calycanthaceae and native to central China. It carries 15–21 thick yellow tepals, blooms from October to March on the previous year's wood, and has a honeyed, waxy scent. It is grown chiefly as a garden ornamental.

**Do 蜡梅 and 梅花 belong to the same plant?** No. The first is *Chimonanthus praecox* in Calycanthaceae; the second is *Prunus mume* in Rosaceae. They share a winter season and the character 梅 in their names, and they are otherwise unrelated — different families, different flowers, different scents, different cultural roles.

**Does wintersweet smell like plum blossom?** Only loosely. Both are light, floral, winter scents, but plum blossom is cool and almondy while wintersweet is warm, honeyed, and waxy. Put side by side, the plum reads thin and fresh and the wintersweet reads thick and sweet.

**Why is wintersweet rarely sold as a dried incense herb?** Because drying removes the volatile fraction that makes the flower distinctive. What survives is a pleasant hay-and-honey note, not the winter garden. The flower is also a garden-scale harvest rather than a crop, so there is no bulk dried-flower trade to speak of.

**Can I use flowers from my garden in incense?** You can use them as a decorative or potpourri element, and you should treat them as a fresh garden material rather than a tested aromatic input. Identify the species first, dry them in moving air, discard anything mouldy, and do not assume that a plant sold as an ornamental is safe to burn in quantity indoors.

**Where does the name 蜡梅 come from?** The 蜡 character refers to wax, and it describes the waxy, translucent substance of the tepals, which look and feel as though they were cut from soft wax. The variant 腊梅 instead points to 腊月, the twelfth lunar month, when the plant is in bloom. Both names describe the same species.

## Summary

Wintersweet (蜡梅) is the flower of *Chimonanthus praecox*, a Calycanthaceae shrub or small tree of central China, 3–13 m tall, flowering from October to March on the previous year's wood with 15–21 yellow tepals marked purplish-red inside. Its scent is sweet, honeyed, and waxy — warmer and heavier than plum blossom, which belongs to an entirely different family. In incense work it is an accent rather than a base, usually supplied as a reconstructed accord because the natural flower is a garden harvest and not a crop. Its garden and New Year associations are long-standing; its medicinal uses are traditional language, not clinical evidence.

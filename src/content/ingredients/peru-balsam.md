> **Direct answer:** Peru balsam (秘鲁香脂, bìlǔ xiāngzhī) is the dark, slow-flowing oleoresin of *Myroxylon balsamum*, a legume tree native to Central America. Perfumery treats it as a sweet base note and fixative, built on a vanilla-cinnamon warmth that no classical Chinese incense text records. Its place in modern Chinese fragrance is an import, and this page claims nothing older than that.

## Key takeaways

- The tree grows in Central America; the label remembers a harbour, not a habitat.
- The material is a balsam in the technical sense — an oleoresin dominated by benzoic and cinnamic esters — which is why it reads sweet rather than dry.
- Its classical Chinese status is nil. The sweet-anchoring job in the historical palette belonged to benzoin (安息香) and storax (苏合香).

## What this material actually is

> **Direct answer:** Peru balsam is a tapped oleoresin, not a distilled oil and not a gum. The distinction decides how it dissolves, how it burns, and how it is dosed.

A tree exudate can be several different things, and the words for them are not interchangeable. A **gum** is a polysaccharide that swells or dissolves in water and carries little scent — gum arabic is the familiar case. A **resin** is the non-volatile fraction: hard or semi-hard, insoluble in water, soluble in alcohol, and inclined to melt rather than evaporate. An **oleoresin** combines resin with a volatile oil, which is why fresh pine oleoresin runs and smells at the same time.

A **balsam** is a narrower category: an oleoresin whose aromatic character comes largely from benzoic or cinnamic acid esters. That chemistry explains the smell of Peru balsam better than any poetic description does. The sweetness is not sugar and not vanilla pods; it is a family of heavy aromatic esters, with benzyl benzoate and benzyl cinnamate the ones most often named in the literature. They are large, slow-moving molecules. They sit on the skin or on a paper strip and keep releasing scent long after the brighter top notes have gone.

That single structural fact — heavy esters, low volatility — produces most of the material's commercial behaviour. It is thick. It is sticky. It darkens with age and exposure. It does not flash off in a burner the way a distilled oil does. A formulator who expects oil-like behaviour will mis-handle it on the first attempt.

## The name is a shipping label, not a habitat

> **Direct answer:** "Peru balsam" describes the port the resin passed through, not the forest it grew in. The tree is Central American, and the trade name has outlived the route that produced it.

Trade names in the resin business are frequently logistical artefacts. Coffee from Java, pepper from Malabar, balsam from Peru: the label records a warehouse or a harbour, and the plant's actual range may sit a thousand kilometres away. Peru balsam follows this pattern exactly. European buyers of the nineteenth century met the resin after it had travelled out through Pacific harbours, so the harbour went on the label, and the label outlived the route by more than a century. Nobody renamed the material when the shipping pattern changed, which is why a Central American tree still answers to a South American country.

There is a second naming layer, and it is Chinese. 秘鲁香脂 is a modern, literal coinage: 秘鲁 for Peru, 香脂 for a fragrant balsam or resinous unguent. It belongs to the vocabulary of imported perfumery materials, not to the vocabulary of the classical incense bench. A reader who encounters 香脂 in a modern product list should not infer a lineage. The classical terms for balsamic aromatics are specific and different — 安息香 for benzoin, 苏合香 for storax, 乳香 for frankincense — and each points at a named material with a documentary history.

What follows from this is a discipline rather than a quibble. A geographic epithet in a material name is a question to ask, never an answer to accept. Ask which species, which plant part, which stage of processing. The label will not tell you.

## Botany: one species complex, two trade names

> **Direct answer:** *Myroxylon balsamum* is a leguminous tree of Central America. Peru balsam and Tolu balsam come from the same species complex, separated by variety rather than by genus, and that closeness is a standing source of trade confusion.

The genus *Myroxylon* sits in Fabaceae, the legume family — a placement worth remembering, because it puts a resinous, tropical timber tree in the same family as beans and acacia. The species is *Myroxylon balsamum*. Older literature and older labels often use *Myroxylon balsamiferum*, and the two names refer to the same plant; modern treatments fold the second into the first. A buyer who sees both names on two documents is not necessarily looking at two materials.

Within the species, the varieties matter commercially. Peru balsam is associated with *Myroxylon balsamum* var. *pereirae*, the form long listed under that narrower name. Tolu balsam is *Myroxylon balsamum* var. *balsamum*. Same species complex, different variety, different product: Tolu is generally the softer, sweeter, more readily pourable of the two, while Peru balsam is darker and heavier. The two are routinely confused in catalogues, and the confusion is understandable — the botanical distance is small even though the trade treats them as separate items.

There is no Flora of China entry for the genus, and that absence is itself informative. *Myroxylon* is a New World plant. It is grown in China, if at all, as an exotic, not as a documented part of the native flora. Any Chinese-language description of the tree is therefore a translation or an agricultural note, not a botanical record with centuries behind it.

## Balsam, resin, gum, resinoid: sorting the categories

> **Direct answer:** The words used for plant exudates describe different physical systems, and each behaves differently in a formula. Treating them as synonyms causes substitution errors that no amount of good aroma can fix.

| Class | Definition | How it behaves | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gum | Water-soluble polysaccharide exudate | Thickens water; carries almost no scent | Acacia gum |
| Resin | The non-volatile part of an exudate | Brittle or glassy; melts; alcohol-soluble, water-insoluble | Mature pine resin |
| Oleoresin | Resin still carrying its volatile oil | Runs, sticks, smells strongly | Fresh pine exudate |
| Balsam | Oleoresin with benzoic or cinnamic esters | Heavy, syrupy, sweetens as it ages | Peru and Tolu balsams |
| Resinoid | Solvent-extracted concentrate | Paste or solid; weighs and doses evenly | Benzoin resinoid |
| Essential oil | The distilled volatile fraction | Thin, mobile, evaporates fast | Frankincense oil |

The table is not pedantry. Each row implies a different solvent, a different melting behaviour, and a different failure mode in production. A balsam that is expected to behave like an essential oil will clog a fine filter and separate out of an alcohol solution. A gum added in the belief that it is a resin will thicken water and contribute nothing to the scent.

Peru balsam sits in the balsam row, and it can also appear as a resinoid. Those are two commercial forms of one material, and the difference between them is standardisation rather than quality. The resinoid is a solvent-extracted concentrate: more uniform, easier to weigh, less inclined to leave a sticky film on equipment. The raw balsam is the whole exudate, with its full natural variation and its full handling difficulty.

## From wound to drum: how the resin is collected

> **Direct answer:** The balsam is obtained by wounding the bark and collecting what the tree exudes, then separating the resin from the collection medium and filtering it. Nothing about the process requires distillation, which is why the material arrives as a balsam rather than as an oil.

The sequence runs in a fixed order, and each step leaves a mark on the finished material.

1. **Wounding.** Collectors cut or strip small patches of bark, exposing the living tissue beneath. The tree responds by exuding oleoresin into the wound — a sealing response, not a gift to the collector.
2. **Collection.** The exudate is thick and slow. Trade accounts describe cloth or rag placed against the wounds to absorb it, with the saturated material gathered later rather than the liquid being poured off.
3. **Separation.** The loaded cloth is worked in hot water so the balsam releases and can be drawn off. This is a physical separation, not a chemical extraction, and it is the step that gives the commercial material its characteristic dark, heavy body.
4. **Cleaning and packing.** The balsam is strained, settled, and filled while still warm enough to flow.

The chain below explains the look of the finished material without invoking any hidden step:

> bark cut → resin bleeds into the wound → the lightest part of the exudate escapes at the wet surface → what remains loses fluid and gains colour → a slow, treacle-like body results, one that has to be warmed or dissolved before it can be weighed.

That is why the raw material is never a clean, clear liquid. It is a partially oxidised, partially evaporated exudate, and its viscosity varies with temperature, age and how much of the volatile fraction survived.

Much of the perfumery trade buys it as a resinoid instead — a solvent extract of the balsam. That step removes some of the heavy waxes and insoluble matter and leaves a concentrate that doses evenly. The spec sheet treats it as another product, though the tree behind it is unchanged.

## The smell, described in perfumery terms

> **Direct answer:** The scent is sweet, balsamic and softly spiced, closer to vanilla and warm cinnamon than to any dry incense resin. It is a base-note smell: heavy, rounded, and slow to leave.

Peru balsam does not open. It arrives already at the bottom of the composition. The first impression is sweetness with a resinous weight behind it, then a spice note that reads soft rather than sharp — cinnamon bark warmed rather than cinnamon powder thrown on heat. Underneath sits a faintly leathery, dried-fruit depth that keeps the sweetness from turning sugary.

Set it beside the classical Chinese resin shelf and the difference is immediate. Frankincense (乳香) reads bright, citrus-resinous, almost terpenic at the top. Benzoin (安息香) reads sweet but dry, with a powdery vanilla facet and a balsamic backbone. Storax (苏合香) reads smoky-sweet, closer to a sweetened leather. Peru balsam goes further into the edible register than any of them. It is the smell of a dessert component, not the smell of a temple.

That character is a Western perfumery value. The gourmand accord — vanilla, tonka, caramel, milky sweetness — belongs to a twentieth-century European and American fragrance language. A material that supplies that register has an obvious use in modern perfumery and no obvious use in a reconstruction of a Song or Ming incense blend. The mismatch is not a defect in either tradition. It is a difference in what each tradition wanted sweetness to do.

## What a fixative does — and what it does not do

> **Direct answer:** A fixative slows the loss of more volatile materials and blends them into a rounder whole. Much of that effect is physical, and part of it is perceptual; the word is used more loosely in the trade than the chemistry justifies.

The physical story is straightforward. A fragrance is a mixture of molecules with different vapor pressures. The light ones leave first. If those molecules are held in a heavy, low-volatility matrix — a resin, a balsam, a viscous base — their escape is slowed, because they must diffuse through the matrix before they can reach the air. The matrix also deposits a film on the surface it is applied to, and that film keeps releasing material for a long time.

Follow the mechanism in order:

> heavy esters, low vapor pressure → a film that stays put on paper or skin → lighter molecules must travel through that film to escape → the evaporation curve stretches and flattens → the drydown reads round rather than abrupt.

The perceptual half matters as much. A sweet, heavy material at the base changes how the whole composition is read: sharp notes seem softer, dry notes seem warmer, and the drydown feels intentional rather than merely faded. Perfumers call this fixation, and the term covers both effects without always distinguishing them.

The editorial point is that "fixative" is not a guarantee. A balsam added at the wrong dose can flatten a composition, mute its top, and leave a sticky, sweet residue that reads as a cheap base. Fixation is a balance, not a virtue. A material that anchors well at one level turns cloying at another.

There is a low-tech way to see the effect for yourself. Prepare two blotter strips: one with the fragrance without the balsam, one with the same formula plus the balsam at the level you intend to use. Smell both when wet, then again after a few hours, then the following day. The useful comparison is not which one smells stronger; it is which one still has a recognisable shape at the later readings, and whether the balsam strip has developed a sticky sweetness that the other lacks. Do this with the real base you will use, because a fixative that works in alcohol can behave differently in a wax or an oil. The test costs two strips and a day, and it answers a question that no supplier sheet can.

## Peru balsam against the sweet-resin shelf

> **Direct answer:** Four materials dominate the sweet-balsamic corner of the shelf, and only two of them belong to the classical Chinese canon. The comparison is decided by botanical source and status before it is decided by scent.

| Material | Peru balsam (秘鲁香脂) | Tolu balsam | Benzoin (安息香) | Storax (苏合香) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant | *M. balsamum* var. *pereirae*, pea family | *M. balsamum* var. *balsamum*, pea family | *Styrax* species, storax family | *Liquidambar orientalis*, sweetgum family |
| Body | Dark, heavy, treacle-like | Lighter and sweeter, pours freely | Hard lump or resinoid | Dark; semi-fluid through solid |
| Scent in trade terms | Vanilla, mild spice, dried fruit | Sweeter than Peru, less leathery | Dry vanilla, powder, balsam | Smoke, leather, sweetness |
| Chinese canon | Not present | Not present | Documented 香材 | Documented 香材 |
| Why it is bought | Modern fixation | Modern fixation | Temple work and base notes | Temple work and base notes |
| Watch for | Allergen declaration | Allergen declaration | Species confusion | Dilution and grade |

The distinction that matters most is not on the aroma line. It is the status line. Benzoin and storax have documentary histories in Chinese incense compounding; Peru and Tolu do not. A blend that substitutes one for the other can smell plausible and be historically wrong, which is exactly the kind of error a reference page should prevent.

The Peru–Tolu pair adds a second trap. They share a species, a family, a continent and a general sweetness, so a supplier can swap one for the other with a straight face. They differ in consistency and in the weight of the sweetness, and a specification that does not name the variety has left the door open.

The wider shelf is mapped in the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/); the two classical neighbours have their own entries, [benzoin](/ingredients/benzoin/) and [storax](/ingredients/styrax-resin/).

## A near neighbour in the same family: copaiba

> **Direct answer:** Copaiba is another legume oleoresin from the Americas, and it is the useful control case for Peru balsam: same family, similar origin story, very different physical behaviour.

Copaiba comes from *Copaifera* species, trees of the Amazon basin that also sit in Fabaceae. The exudate is an oleoresin, tapped from the trunk in a way that would look familiar to anyone who has watched pine tapping. That is where the resemblance to Peru balsam ends.

Copaiba flows. It is thin, mobile and strongly terpenic, with a scent that reads woody-peppery rather than sweet. Peru balsam does not flow; it creeps. The difference is chemical rather than botanical: copaiba carries a large volatile terpene fraction, while Peru balsam is dominated by heavy aromatic esters that barely evaporate at room temperature. Two trees in one family, two completely different handling problems.

For a buyer, the pair is a reminder that botanical kinship predicts very little about commercial behaviour. Family tells you something about the plant. It tells you almost nothing about viscosity, solubility or how the material will behave in a formula. Those properties have to be checked on the material in front of you.

The comparison also sharpens the point about naming. Both materials are New World oleoresins with Portuguese or Spanish colonial names in their trade history. Neither is a classical Chinese 香材. A page that treats "American oleoresin" as a single category would blur two materials that a formulator must keep apart.

## Grading without a grade standard

> **Direct answer:** There is no universal grading scale for Peru balsam. What exists is a set of commercial checks — identity, purity, viscosity, aroma, documentation — that separate a usable drum from a doubtful one.

| Test | Question it answers | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Species check | Is this *pereirae*, Tolu, or a mix of both? | Paperwork names only the trade name |
| Dilution check | Has solvent or carrier been added? | Body too thin; sharp note at the top |
| Body check | Does it hold a heavy, even consistency? | Gels, separates, or refuses to flow |
| Smell check | Is the sweetness clean? | Stale, burnt or chemical edge |
| Paper trail | Can the batch be declared and traced? | No allergen statement; no batch number |
| Packing check | Will the container survive the material? | Resin creep; lid sealed shut |

Viscosity deserves a longer note than it usually gets. A balsam is a non-Newtonian material in practice: it may pour on a warm day and refuse to move in a cool store. Two drums that read the same on a specification sheet can behave completely differently on a winter morning. A buyer who judges consistency from a single sample taken in summer has learned very little.

Purity is the second trap. Peru balsam is expensive enough, and sticky enough, that dilution is easy to hide. A solvent cut shows up as a thinness in the body and a sharpness at the top of the smell. A blend with a cheaper balsam shows up as a loss of the dried-fruit depth. Neither is visible in a photograph, and neither is caught by a casual sniff at the warehouse door.

Documentation is the third. Because the material is a declarable allergen in fragrance labelling, the paperwork is not administrative decoration. It is the part of the product that tells a downstream formulator how much room they have.

## Bench handling: viscosity, colour and dosing

> **Direct answer:** Peru balsam is handled warm, dissolved, or pre-diluted, never weighed cold and never treated as a free-flowing oil. Its colour and its stickiness are the two practical problems, and both are managed rather than solved.

Warm the container gently before weighing. A water bath at hand-warm temperature is enough to loosen the body; direct flame is not, and the material is not worth the risk. Weigh into a container you are willing to sacrifice, because the residue will cling and set.

For alcohol-based work, the balsam dissolves slowly and incompletely. Expect a dark solution, a sediment of insoluble matter, and a filter that clogs if the material was not pre-warmed or pre-strained. A resinoid avoids most of this: it is already the soluble, standardised fraction, and it doses like a paste rather than like tar.

Colour is the second consideration. The material is dark brown to near-black, and it carries that colour into whatever it touches. A clear gel, a white wax, a pale cream — all of them will drift toward amber. For some products that drift is acceptable or even desirable. For others it is a formulation constraint that has to be decided before the fragrance is chosen, not after.

Dosing follows from the fixation discussion. Too little and the base does nothing; too much and the composition turns sweet and heavy, with a sticky drydown. The right level is found by sampling against the actual base, not by transferring a number from another formula. That is an editorial judgement, and it is the opposite of the "percentage from a supplier sheet" habit.

## Reading a specification sheet for a balsam

> **Direct answer:** A specification earns the name only if it lets a buyer answer five things: which plant, which part of it, which physical form, how clean the material is, and what paperwork travels with the batch. A sheet that covers only the paperwork is a sales document.

Most balsam sheets are short, and the short ones tend to be vague. The fields worth insisting on are these:

- **Botanical identity.** The species and, where the trade distinguishes them, the variety. "Peru balsam" alone does not separate *pereirae* from *balsamum*.
- **Plant part and material class.** Trunk oleoresin, not a leaf or seed product; balsam, not an essential oil relabelled as one.
- **Form.** Raw balsam, resinoid, or a dilution. A dilution stated as a percentage of resin is a different purchase from a neat balsam, and the price difference is not a discount.
- **Appearance.** Colour and approximate consistency at a stated temperature. "Dark viscous liquid" without a temperature is close to meaningless, because the same drum reads differently in January and July.
- **Documentation.** Allergen declaration, batch number, and a statement of what the material is cut with, if anything.

The trap is a sheet that lists aroma adjectives and nothing else. Adjectives cannot be checked on receipt, cannot be disputed, and cannot be used to reject a drum. A buyer who accepts an aroma-only sheet has agreed to buy whatever arrives.

The second trap is a certificate of analysis that describes the wrong product. A resinoid certificate says nothing about a raw-balsam purchase, even when one supplier and one tree sit behind both. The form has to match the purchase order before any number on the page means anything.

## Storage: how the material changes in the drum

> **Direct answer:** Peru balsam is stable in the sense that it does not spoil quickly, and unstable in the sense that it thickens, darkens and skins over. Storage is about slowing those changes rather than preventing them.

Three processes run simultaneously in a stored drum. The volatile fraction continues to evaporate from any exposed surface, so the material thickens. Oxidation darkens the colour further and can push the aroma toward a drier, flatter profile. At the surface, a skin or crust forms where the balsam meets air, and that crust is difficult to redissolve once it has set.

The practical responses follow from the mechanisms:

- Keep headspace small and the lid sealed; the surface is where the change happens fastest.
- Store cool and dark, but not cold enough to make the material unworkable when a sample is needed.
- Avoid repeated warming and cooling of the whole container; decant a working quantity instead.
- Expect colour drift over time, and do not use colour alone as a freshness test.

There is a boundary worth stating. A balsam that has darkened is not automatically spoiled, and a balsam that still pours is not automatically fresh. Appearance is a weak signal in both directions. The reliable check is a smell and a small dissolution test against a retained reference sample — which is why keeping a sealed reference from each incoming batch is worth the shelf space.

## Safety framing: sensitizer first, scent second

> **Direct answer:** Peru balsam is a recognised contact sensitizer and a declarable fragrance allergen. Its handling is governed by labelling, purity and use limits, and it is not a material to burn as incense.

Sensitisation is a specific hazard, not a vague warning. A sensitised person reacts to a substance that most people tolerate, and once sensitised, the response can generalise. Peru balsam sits squarely in that category: regulators require it to be named on fragrance labels, and dermatologists have used it for decades as a marker substance in patch testing. That double appearance — on a label and in a clinic — is what makes the trade treat it as a controlled aromatic rather than a free one.

For a formulator, three consequences follow:

- **The declaration is mandatory where the regime applies.** A product containing the material carries the allergen name on the label, and the threshold rules decide when.
- **The use level is a constraint, not a preference.** A beautiful accord that cannot be declared within the permitted level is not a usable accord.
- **Cross-reactivity is a live question.** A person who reacts to one balsam may react to related materials, so a "natural alternative" swap is not automatically a safer one.

Combustion is a separate issue and should not be blurred with the sensitisation one. Burning any aromatic material generates smoke and airborne particles, and a sticky balsam is a poor candidate for the burner in the first place: it chars, smokes, and releases an acrid note rather than a clean aroma. Anyone who burns it is exposed to combustion products first and to the aromatic second.

Read this as editorial material knowledge rather than medical guidance. Anyone with a known fragrance allergy should take the material seriously and seek qualified advice rather than relying on a website.

## The Chinese record: what is absent, and why that matters

> **Direct answer:** Neither 香乘 (Xiang Cheng) nor the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) lists this resin, and the classical 香材 canon has no slot for it. That silence records trade history; it is not a hole in the record waiting for a modern writer to fill.

Classical Chinese incense compounding worked with a defined material set and a defined hierarchy. The 君臣佐使 framework — chief, minister, assistant, guide — assigned each ingredient a role in a blend, and the roles were not interchangeable. A chief material carried the composition. A minister supported it. An assistant moderated or corrected it. A guide carried the aroma outward or fixed the direction of the blend. The system assumed materials with known behaviour and documented standing.

Peru balsam enters that framework from outside. It has no classical role because it was not present to have one. The sweet-anchoring function in the historical palette was served by benzoin and storax, both of which are documented 香材 with established positions. A modern blender who wants a vanilla-cinnamon base has to make a choice: use the classical materials and accept a drier sweetness, or use the modern import and accept that the result is a modern composition.

That is the boundary this page draws. It does not say the material is inferior because it is not classical. It says the two things are different categories, and that a page which blurs them is useless as a reference.

The absence also carries a dating signal. A blend that claims to be a reconstruction of a Ming formula and contains Peru balsam is not a reconstruction. The material was not in the toolkit. That is a test a reader can apply without any laboratory, and it is more reliable than most authenticity claims.

## Matching the material to the job

> **Direct answer:** The right form of Peru balsam depends on the product, not on the material's reputation. Fine fragrance wants a resinoid; heavy home fragrance can use the raw balsam; an incense paste is usually the wrong application altogether.

| Product type | Choose this form | Specify carefully | Likely failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fine fragrance | Resinoid | Extraction solvent, batch-to-batch consistency, allergen paperwork | Too much base; dark tint in a pale concentrate |
| Reed diffuser or candle | Neat balsam, or a resinoid | Flow behaviour, tint, miscibility with the carrier | Blocked wick; sediment; colour shift |
| Modern incense paste | A small proportion of balsam | Its role as a softener, not as fuel | Tacky dough; smouldering burn; harsh smoke |
| Skin-contact product | Low-level resinoid | Declaration and permitted level | Non-compliant label; sensitisation risk |
| Teaching or reference set | Neat balsam | Origin record and a tight container | Leaks; resin creep; ruined labels |

The table is a decision aid, not a set of recipes. Its purpose is to make the first question the right one: which form, for which job, under which constraint. A buyer who starts from "I want Peru balsam" and works forward will usually end up with the wrong physical form.

## Three routes to sweetness, and why they are not interchangeable

> **Direct answer:** Peru balsam, vanilla and tonka all read sweet, but the sweetness comes from different molecules with different behaviour. Swapping one for another changes the drydown, the colour and the labelling.

| Sweet material | What carries the sweetness | How it sits in a blend | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peru balsam (秘鲁香脂) | Benzyl benzoate, benzyl cinnamate and related heavy esters | Sits low, warms slowly, darkens the mix | Declarable allergen; awkward handling |
| Vanilla | Vanillin and related phenolic compounds | Reaches from the base into the heart | Own labelling and cost profile |
| Tonka | Coumarin | Powdery, hay-like, soft | Coumarin is restricted in several markets |
| Benzoin (安息香) | Benzoic esters in a dry resin | Powdery vanilla over a balsamic spine | Species and grade vary widely |

The practical lesson is that "sweet" is a family, not a single note. A formula short of vanilla character is not automatically fixed by adding Peru balsam: the balsam brings weight, spice and a dried-fruit shadow, and it will not supply the milky, edible top that vanillin gives. Reaching for the balsam as a cheap vanilla substitute produces a blend that smells heavy at the bottom and thin at the top.

The second lesson concerns the constraint column. Three of these four materials carry a labelling or restriction issue, and they are not the same issue. A formulator who treats them as interchangeable sweetness units will discover the difference at the label stage, which is the most expensive place to discover anything.

## What this material cannot do

> **Direct answer:** Peru balsam is a base note with a specific sweetness, and it fails at several jobs people ask of it. Knowing the failures is more useful than another paragraph of praise.

- **It is not a top note.** There is no bright opening in the material. Added to a light citrus cologne, it sits underneath and pulls the composition downward.
- **It does not replace vanilla.** The sweetness is spicier and drier than vanillin, and it carries a resinous weight that vanilla does not.
- **It does not burn cleanly as incense.** The body is heavy and sticky; heat chars it before it perfumes the air.
- **It does not fix everything.** A base note can round a composition, but it cannot rescue a formula whose top notes are badly chosen.
- **It does not stay pale.** Any product that must remain white or clear has already excluded the material.

That list is editorial judgement, drawn from how the material behaves rather than from a measurement. It is also the fastest way to decide whether the material belongs in a project at all. If the brief needs a bright conifer top, a dry classical sweetness, or a clean-burning resin, Peru balsam is the wrong answer and no amount of blending will make it the right one.

## Misreadings worth correcting

> **Direct answer:** Five assumptions recur in buyer questions, and each one leads to a different mistake. Correcting them is more useful than repeating the aroma description.

**"Peru balsam comes from Peru."** It does not, in botanical terms. The tree is Central American; the name records a trade route. This is the single most common error, and it is harmless in itself — until someone uses the name as provenance evidence.

**"Balsam means medicine."** Balsams have a long history in pharmacy, and older literature treats them as having therapeutic value. That history is real; the therapeutic claim is not established here, and this page makes none. A material with a pharmaceutical past is not thereby a safe or effective product.

**"It is a Chinese incense material."** It is not, and no classical source says otherwise. The confusion usually comes from the Chinese name, which looks like a traditional term and is not.

**"Natural means gentle."** Peru balsam is the clearest counterexample on this site. It is a plant exudate and a recognised contact allergen at the same time. The two facts do not contradict each other; they describe different properties.

**"Stronger smell means better grade."** A loud, sharp top can indicate a solvent cut or a stale drum. Good balsam is heavy and quiet, and its sweetness sits low rather than shouting.

## Summary

Peru balsam (秘鲁香脂) is the oleoresin of *Myroxylon balsamum*, a Central American legume, traded under a name that remembers Peruvian ports rather than the tree's range. Its chemistry — heavy benzoic and cinnamic esters — gives it the vanilla-cinnamon sweetness that Western perfumery uses as a base note and fixative, and gives it the viscosity that makes it awkward on the bench. No classical Chinese text records it; the old palette anchored its sweets with benzoin and storax. The commercial terms are set less by aroma than by sensitisation, labelling and documentation, which is why a specification sheet matters more here than a smell test.

## FAQ

**What is Peru balsam (秘鲁香脂)?** A heavy, dark exudate tapped from the trunk of *Myroxylon balsamum*, a pea-family tree of Central America. It smells sweet, vanilla-like and softly spiced, and perfumery uses it as a base note and fixative. The Chinese name is a modern translation, not a classical term.

**Is Peru balsam a classical Chinese incense ingredient?** No. Search 香乘 (Xiang Cheng) or the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) and the material does not appear; the classical 香材 canon never absorbed it. Chinese fragrance met it through Western perfumery in the modern period, and this page claims no earlier origin.

**Why is it called "Peru" balsam if the tree grows in Central America?** The name records the shipping route. The resin was consolidated and exported through Peruvian ports, and the commercial label stuck to the drum rather than to the botany. The tree itself is Central American, and the name has never been a range description.

**Are Peru balsam and Tolu balsam the same thing?** They are closely related — two varieties of the same species complex, *Myroxylon balsamum*. Peru balsam is the *pereirae* variety, darker and heavier; Tolu is the *balsamum* variety, softer and sweeter. They are separate trade items and should be specified separately.

**Why does one drum smell sweeter than another?** Age and handling explain most of the difference. As the lighter fraction leaves, the heavy esters that carry the sweetness become proportionally stronger, so an older drum can read sweeter, darker and thicker than a fresh one. Batch variation in the raw exudate does the rest. A difference in sweetness is therefore a stage signal rather than a grade, and the only way to judge it is against a retained reference from a batch you have already accepted.

**Can Peru balsam be burned as incense?** It is a poor candidate. The material is viscous and heavy, it chars rather than vaporises cleanly, and the resulting smoke is acrid. It belongs in perfumery and in resinous bases, not on a charcoal disc. Any burning of aromatic materials produces smoke and particulate matter, which is a separate exposure from the material's sensitising potential.

**What does "contact sensitizer" mean for someone formulating with it?** It means a person who has become sensitised can react to the material on skin contact, and that labelling rules require the substance to be declared. For the formulator, that produces a ceiling on use and a line on the label; it is not a scent descriptor. Read it as editorial material knowledge, not medical guidance.

Two classical neighbours carry most of the comparison: [benzoin](/ingredients/benzoin/) and [storax](/ingredients/styrax-resin/). The gourmand direction leads to [vanilla](/ingredients/vanilla/); a different New World exudate appears on the [copaiba](/ingredients/copaiba/) page, and the [scent guide](/blog/scent-guide/) sets the group in a wider map.

## Evidence, graded by claim

> **Direct answer:** The botanical claims are solid, the perfumery claims are established practice, the historical claim is a documented absence, and the health claims are not established at all. Keeping those four grades apart is the point of this section.

| Point at issue | What supports it | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Which species, and where from? | Taxonomic record for *M. balsamum* | Firm |
| One material or a species complex? | Varietal treatment inside the species | Firm, with a naming history |
| Does the Chinese canon know it? | No entry in 香乘 or the *Bencao Gangmu* | Firm negative |
| Is it safe to handle? | Allergen listings and patch-test reports | Firm as a hazard |
| How is it used in perfumery? | Workshop practice | Practice, not measurement |
| Do the aroma words measure anything? | Sensory trade language | Descriptive only |
| Does it cure anything? | Nothing used on this page | Not established |

Negative evidence is worth a sentence of its own. Saying "Peru balsam is absent from 香乘" is not the same as saying "nobody has checked". The classical corpus is finite, indexed and widely studied; a material that does not appear in it is genuinely absent from the tradition rather than merely undocumented. That is why the claim can be stated flatly.

Sensory language is the weakest row, and it should be labelled as such. "Vanilla-cinnamon" is a comparison, not a chemical measurement. Two competent noses can disagree about the spice note without either being wrong, and a specification sheet that tries to encode aroma in adjectives is a poor substitute for a sample.

> **Direct answer:** Silk tree flower (合欢花, héhuān huā) is the blossom of *Albizia julibrissin* (合欢), a leguminous tree native to Asia and planted widely for its fern-like leaves and pink powder-puff flowers. The flower smells softly sweet and powdery, and the tree is far more famous for its cultural meaning than for any role in the incense workshop — a distinction this page keeps in view throughout.

The table above is deliberately short, because the honest summary of this material is short: a beautiful tree, a gentle flower, a large cultural reputation, and a small aromatic footprint. Most of what follows explains how those four facts fit together, and where a buyer or a formulator is likely to be misled by the reputation into expecting more scent than the flower can give.

## What the plant actually is

> **Direct answer:** *Albizia julibrissin* is a deciduous legume tree with bipinnate, feathery leaves and pink flowers whose show is made almost entirely of long stamens. Flora of China gives a tree to 16 m, flowers from May to July, and flat strap-shaped pods that ripen from August to October.

The botanical description is worth knowing in detail, because the flower's structure explains its scent. Flora of China describes a deciduous tree reaching roughly 16 m with an open crown and angular branchlets. The leaves are bipinnate, built from 4 to 12 pairs of pinnae, and each pinna carries 10 to 30 pairs of small, oblique, linear leaflets measuring about 6–12 mm by 1–4 mm. The pink colour of the "flower" comes chiefly from filaments roughly 2.5 cm long, grouped into terminal panicles. The fruit is a flat, strap-shaped legume about 9–15 cm long and 1.5–2.5 cm wide.

| Character | What Flora of China records | Why it matters for the material |
|---|---|---|
| Habit | Deciduous tree to about 16 m, open crown | A tall, airy tree, not a shrub crop; flowers are borne high and wide |
| Leaves | Bipinnate, 4–12 pinna pairs, leaflets 6–12 × 1–4 mm | The fine leaflets create the ferny silhouette and the night-closing behaviour |
| Flower | Pink, filaments about 2.5 cm, terminal panicles | The "petals" a picker sees are mostly stamens — thin, quick-drying tissue |
| Fruit | Flat legume 9–15 × 1.5–2.5 cm | Sets after flowering; not the aromatic part |
| Phenology | Flowers May–July, fruits August–October | A narrow picking window in the warm season |

The structural point deserves emphasis: a silk tree flower is not a dense, waxy petal mass like a camellia or a gardenia. It is a puff of long, slender filaments. That architecture gives the tree its airy look, and it also sets a hard ceiling on how much aromatic material a single flower can hold.

## The name: collective joy, and leaves that close at night

> **Direct answer:** 合欢 means "collective joy" or "conjoining joy," a name about harmony and reunion rather than scent. The classical alternates 合昏 and 夜合 both point to the same botanical habit — the leaflets fold together after dark.

Two naming strands run through the Chinese record, and they are easy to blur. One is emotional: 合欢 names the tree as a symbol of joy shared, of reunion, of a household or a friendship restored. The other is observational: 合昏 ("dusk-closing") and 夜合 ("night-closing") describe what the foliage does at the end of the day.

That leaf movement is a real phenomenon, not a poetic invention. Many legumes have a swelling at the base of the leaf stalk, the pulvinus, where shifts in cell turgor swing the leaflets into a folded position at night and open them again with light. The tree's own specific epithet, *julibrissin*, is unrelated to this behaviour, but the Chinese common names are direct field notes. A reader who encounters 夜合 in an old text should read it as a description of foliage, not as a claim about fragrance or about a night-blooming flower.

The flower's own name is simply the tree's name plus 花 (flower): 合欢花. There is no separate classical coinage for the blossom, which is itself a small signal. Materials that were traded and worked as incense tend to accumulate their own terminology — grades, part-names, preparation terms. A flower that carries only the generic "flower of the tree" label sits closer to the ornamental and the medicinal than to the perfumer's bench.

## What it smells like

> **Direct answer:** The scent is a light, sweet, powdery floral — closer to a soft cosmetic powder than to a heavy white flower. It reads as a quiet garden note, and it fades rather than develops.

Fresh, the blossom offers a sweetness with a cosmetic, almost almond-powder softness underneath. There is no animal warmth, no citrus lift, no resin. The closest familiar comparison in this encyclopedia is not jasmine, which is far denser and more indolic, but the gentler end of the white-floral range — the airy sweetness of [osmanthus](/ingredients/osmanthus/) without its apricot fleshiness, or the thin honeyed breath of [honeysuckle](/ingredients/honeysuckle/) without its green sap.

| Material | Weight in a blend | Character | Structural role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silk tree flower (合欢花) | Very light | Sweet, powdery, diffuse | Accent or narrative note; cannot anchor |
| [Osmanthus](/ingredients/osmanthus/) | Medium | Apricot-sweet, floral-fruity | Heart note with body |
| [Honeysuckle](/ingredients/honeysuckle/) | Light–medium | Honeyed, green-floral | Fresh heart note |
| [Jasmine](/ingredients/jasmine/) | Heavy | Dense, indolic, animalic edge | Heart that can dominate |
| [Lotus flower](/ingredients/lotus-flower/) | Light | Watery, clean, faintly green | Atmospheric accent |

The comparison is not a ranking — each material does a different job. The point is structural: silk tree flower has the least carrying power of the group. In a formula it behaves like a tint rather than a colour. Used at a small proportion it softens a blend and adds a powdery bloom; pushed higher it contributes volume without proportion, and the blend starts to smell of dried hay.

## Why the scent is modest

> **Direct answer:** The aromatic load of 合欢花 sits in thin, short-lived floral tissue rather than in resin ducts or oil cells, so there is little material to concentrate and much surface from which it can escape. Drying removes water and carries a large share of the volatiles with it.

The mechanism runs in a clear line:

fresh filaments with a high surface-to-mass ratio → water evaporates quickly from the tissue → aromatic molecules leave alongside the water → the dried flower retains mainly a hay-and-honey sweetness → the bright blossom character is gone within the drying step.

A resinous material resists this sequence because its aromatics are locked in a heavy, sticky matrix that gives them up slowly and at higher temperatures. A thin floral has no such matrix. Its scent is a by-product of living tissue, produced and lost continuously. Once the tissue is dead and dry, what remains is the ghost of the fresh note plus the plant's own dried-cell smell.

That is why a perfumer's absolute or a distilled oil would be a different material from the dried flower, and why this page does not treat the dried blossom as a concentrated aromatic. The flower is at its best close to the moment of picking, and every step away from that moment costs aroma. This is not a defect of the material; it is the nature of a delicate floral, and judging it by the standards of a resin will always produce disappointment.

## Where it grows, and the picking window

> **Direct answer:** *Albizia julibrissin* is native across a wide band of Asia and is planted as an ornamental through much of China, where Flora of China records it as widely cultivated in many provinces. The flowers open in the warm months, giving a narrow window for collection.

The tree's natural and planted range is broad. Flora of China lists it as widely cultivated in provinces that include Anhui, Fujian, Gansu, Guizhou, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Shandong, Shanxi, Taiwan, Yunnan, and Zhejiang. It also occurs through central, eastern, and southwestern Asia. In practice this means the flower is not a rare or remote commodity: it is a by-product of a common street and garden tree, gathered where people already grow the species for shade and ornament.

That abundance shapes the supply picture. There is no plantation system dedicated to 合欢花 in the way there is for a commercial resin crop. Material comes from flowering trees in season, picked by hand or gathered from the ground, and the quality varies accordingly. A buyer should expect small-lot, variable material rather than a standardized agricultural commodity, and should judge each lot on its own condition.

The picking window follows the phenology. Flowers appear from May into July across most of the range, and the useful moment is when the panicles are open and dry. Wet weather is the enemy of any thin-petalled flower: moisture at picking invites browning and mould before the material ever reaches a drying rack. The practical rule, stated as a principle rather than as a claim about any particular grower, is to collect in dry conditions, in the cooler part of the day, and to keep the gathered flowers shallow and ventilated rather than heaped.

## Drying and storage: a preservation problem

> **Direct answer:** Handling 合欢花 is entirely about preservation: dry it fast enough to stop mould, gently enough to keep the sweet note, and store it so it does not reabsorb moisture. There is no classical grinding, tapping, or distillation tradition attached to the flower.

The drying chain is the second mechanism this material teaches:

damp flower packed in a basket → heat builds in the mass → enzymes and mould act before the tissue dries → browning and a sour, musty note develop → the lot is aromatic only in the sense that it still smells of something.

The way to interrupt that chain is air, thin layers, and shade. Direct sun is tempting because it dries quickly, but strong sun bleaches colour and drives off the light volatiles that make the flower worth having. Shade with moving air is slower and kinder. The flower should feel papery, not brittle, and should keep a faint pink cast rather than turning uniformly brown.

Storage continues the same logic. Dried floral tissue is hygroscopic; it will pull moisture from humid air and quietly return to the state that mould prefers. A sealed container, kept dark and cool, is the minimum. Clear jars on a sunny shelf are a poor choice for any volatile floral. If the material arrives soft rather than crisp, or if the container smells of dust and old hay when opened, the aroma has already gone and no later intervention will bring it back.

## Fresh versus dried: what actually changes

> **Direct answer:** Fresh 合欢花 is sweeter and more alive; the dried flower is quieter, more hay-like, and far more stable. Neither state is "better" in the abstract — they answer different needs.

| State | Aroma | Handling | Realistic use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh | Sweet, airy, faintly green | Wilts within hours; cannot be stored | Immediate enjoyment; no commercial lot |
| Shade-dried | Hay-sweet, powdery, much weaker | Stable for a season if sealed | Sachets, blending, display |
| Old or badly stored | Flat, dusty, sour | Crumbles; mould risk | None |

The fresh flower has one advantage that no processing can preserve: the living tissue is still producing aroma. Pick a panicle in the morning and the sweetness is there in the air around it, thin but unmistakable. Leave that same panicle on a shelf for a day and most of the impression is gone, not because the flower has "spoiled" but because the volatile fraction has simply left.

What drying buys is time. It fixes the material in a state that can be packed, shipped, and reopened months later, at the cost of most of the top note. This is the ordinary trade every dried floral makes, and it is worth stating plainly because marketing language often implies that a dried flower is a preserved version of the fresh one. It is a reduced version, and a good supplier will describe it as such.

## The 香乘 question: what the silence means

> **Direct answer:** 合欢花 is not a staple of the 香乘 (Xiang Cheng) incense canon, and this page does not invent a classical burning role for it. The absence is a statement about the documentary record, not a verdict on the flower.

This is the point where a lot of plant writing goes wrong. A famous tree with a beautiful flower invites the assumption that it must have been an incense material, and from that assumption a paragraph of invented tradition tends to follow. The record does not support that move. The 香乘 and the related classical incense compilations assemble materials that were traded, priced, graded, and worked into formulas. 合欢花 does not appear among them as a working material in the way agarwood, sandalwood, borneol, or clove do.

What follows from the silence is narrower than it looks. It does not mean the flower was unknown, and it does not mean nobody ever put it in a sachet or a blend. It means the flower was not catalogued as a burning incense material in the canonical literature — and that a writer who claims otherwise is filling a gap with imagination. The distinction matters because it separates a defensible statement ("this flower is documented culturally and in materia medica") from an indefensible one ("this was a classical incense ingredient").

For the reader building a mental map of Chinese aromatic materials, the useful category here is "cultural and medicinal flower with a pleasant scent," not "incense ingredient." Placing it correctly keeps the rest of the map honest.

## Four things sold under overlapping names

> **Direct answer:** The names 合欢 and 夜合 attach to more than one plant, and the bark of the silk tree is a separate materia-medica item from its flower. Checking the botanical name, and the part, settles most confusion before money changes hands.

Confusion around this material is mostly linguistic rather than visual, which makes a table the most efficient corrective.

| Name encountered | Botanical identity | What it is | How to tell it apart | Relevance here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 合欢花 (héhuān huā) | *Albizia julibrissin* | The open flower | Pink filament puff, sweet powdery scent, papery when dried | The subject of this page |
| 合欢皮 (héhuān pí) | *Albizia julibrissin* | The bark | Flat, grey-brown, quilled pieces; no floral scent | A separate materia-medica item, not a flower |
| 夜合花 (yèhé huā) | Often *Magnolia coco* in horticultural usage | A magnolia flower | Fleshy, creamy petals; different family entirely | A naming overlap, not a substitute |
| 含羞草 (hánxiū cǎo) | *Mimosa pudica* | The sensitive plant | Low herb, prickly stems, small pom-pom heads | Same family, same leaf-folding habit, different plant |

The pattern is worth naming: two of the four confusions come from a shared behaviour (leaves that close), and one comes from a shared tree. None comes from a shared scent. A buyer who sorts material by smell alone will not confuse the bark with the flower, but a buyer working from a written order in Chinese can easily receive the wrong part.

The 夜合花 row deserves a caution rather than a verdict. Horticultural Chinese uses 夜合花 for a magnolia, and the same two characters also appear as a classical alias for the silk tree. When a supplier's list says 夜合, ask for the Latin binomial. That single question resolves the ambiguity faster than any photograph.

## Quality: a working grading frame

> **Direct answer:** There is no published commercial grading ladder for 合欢花, so the practical approach is a simple three-tier working frame built on colour, intactness, aroma, and cleanliness. Treat it as an editorial screening tool, not as an industry standard.

| Grade (editorial frame) | Colour | Intactness | Aroma | Foreign matter | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top | Pink to dusky rose retained | Panicles mostly whole | Clear sweet-powdery note, no hay | Stems and dust minimal | Sachet, display, delicate blending |
| Sound | Faded pink to tan | Partly broken | Faint sweetness, slight hay | Some stem and dust | General blending, potpourri |
| Poor | Uniform brown or grey | Crushed, dusty | Musty, sour, or flat | Heavy stem, grit, mould risk | Reject |

Two judgments sit behind the table. First, colour is a proxy, not a goal: a flower that has been dyed or heavily sun-bleached can look better than it smells. Second, aroma outranks appearance at the margin. A slightly faded lot that still smells clean and sweet will outperform a pretty lot that smells of the bottom of a hay barn.

The failure modes are worth listing explicitly, because they are the things a photograph cannot show:

- **Mustiness** — the signature of moisture damage; irreversible.
- **Sourness** — fermentation in a packed basket before drying.
- **Dust** — often grit from ground collection, which also carries a flat, mineral smell.
- **Stem load** — woody stems add weight and almost no aroma.
- **Colour fade without mustiness** — old but clean material; usable, low value.

For a buyer, the useful question is not "is this premium grade?" but "what is this lot actually capable of?" A clean, faintly scented flower is honest material for a sachet. It is not a substitute for a resin, and no grade of it will behave like one.

## Forms, and where each belongs

> **Direct answer:** 合欢花 moves as dried whole flowers, broken flowers, and powder. Distilled oils and absolutes belong to modern perfumery rather than to the classical record, and should not be treated as the same material.

| Form | Preparation | Behaviour | Sensible use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried flower | Shade-dried panicles, lightly cleaned | Slow, faint release; visually attractive | Sachets, display jars, potpourri |
| Broken flower | Hand-rubbed, stems removed | Stronger initial release, faster fade | Blending, sachet refills |
| Powder | Milled dried flower | Immediate aroma, short life, dusts heavily | Incense blends at low proportion |
| Distillate or absolute | Solvent or steam extraction (modern) | Concentrated, different aroma profile | Perfumery; not a classical incense form |

The distinction between the dried flower and an extract is not a technicality. Extraction changes which molecules reach the nose, and it changes the material's economics completely. A distilled product sold under the flower's name is a modern industrial article; the dried blossom in a sachet is the form the traditional record actually describes.

## Using it in a blend

> **Direct answer:** Use 合欢花 as a small accent that softens and sweetens, never as the structural body of a formula. It works best in sachets and low-heat applications, where its delicate volatiles can escape before they scorch.

The third mechanism is about heat:

fine floral tissue close to a glowing ember → the tissue reaches combustion temperature almost at once → floral volatiles pyrolyse before they can distil → the note turns to scorched hay and acrid smoke → the sweetness the flower is valued for never reaches the room.

This is why delicate flowers belong in the cooler parts of an incense practice. In a sachet, the release is driven by ambient warmth and air movement, which is exactly the gentle regime the material needs. In a blended cone or stick, the flower should sit at the edge of the formula — a small percentage that modifies the top of the smoke rather than a visible fraction of the mass. If the dried flower is a large share of the combustible material, the blend will smell of burning vegetation, and the floral contribution will be lost.

There is a second, quieter use that suits the material better than burning: scenting paper, cloth, or a small display jar. The flower's cultural weight and its soft appearance do work that its aroma cannot, and a sachet lets both operate at once.

## Reading an aroma description critically

> **Direct answer:** Descriptions of this flower tend to borrow the vocabulary of heavier materials — "rich," "deep," "long-lasting" — and those words do not survive a direct comparison. A reader should discount any floral description that promises density.

A useful test for any aromatic description is to ask what the material could physically do. A flower built from slender filaments, with no resin ducts and no waxy petal mass, cannot release a heavy, tenacious scent. If a listing calls such a flower "deep," the word is doing marketing work rather than descriptive work.

The same test applies to duration. Thin floral tissue gives up its aroma quickly, and a dried version has already given up most of it. Claims of exceptional longevity for a material of this structure should be read as a signal about the seller, not about the flower.

Three phrases are worth translating when they appear in a listing:

- **"Strong floral fragrance"** — ask for a sample; for this material, expect a soft, close scent rather than a room-filling one.
- **"Wild-picked"** — a true statement about provenance, not a quality grade; wild material varies more, not less.
- **"Hand-dried"** — meaningful if it implies shade and air; meaningless if the lot smells of hay and dust.

The practical significance is simple: judge the lot, not the adjectives. A clean, faintly sweet sample is the best evidence available, and no description can substitute for it.

## The classical record, read carefully

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records 合欢 in its tree section, gives the aliases 合昏 and 夜合, and quotes the line "合欢蠲忿，萱草忘忧." That is a cultural and materia-medica statement about temperament, not a fragrance specification.

The entry sits in 木之二, the second tree section of the materia medica. The aliases it records are the ones discussed above: 合昏 and 夜合, both describing the closing leaves. The quoted line pairs the tree with the daylily: 合欢 dispels 忿 (resentment, anger), 萱草 makes one forget 忧 (sorrow). The pairing is literary and symmetrical — one plant for anger, one for grief — and the line is traditionally traced to Ji Kang's 养生论 (On Nourishing Life), a text about cultivating a settled life rather than a pharmacological manual.

Reading that line as a medical claim would be a category error, and reading it as an incense note would be a different one. What the record establishes is that the tree carried a strong association with calm and reconciliation. That association is why the tree appears in gardens, poems, and family imagery. It is not evidence that the flower was burned, and it is not evidence of any clinical effect.

The materia-medica tradition does treat the tree's parts separately: the bark (合欢皮) and the flower (合欢花) are distinct items with distinct descriptions. That separation is useful to a modern reader for a practical reason — it explains why a supplier's catalogue may list two products from one species, and why the flower is not simply "the aromatic version" of the bark.

## Safety and handling

> **Direct answer:** The safety issues are ordinary ones: smoke from any burning material, dust from dried floral tissue, and mould in badly stored lots. This page makes no medical claims for the flower and does not recommend it for ingestion.

Incense combustion produces smoke and fine particulate matter regardless of how pleasant the raw material smells. Burn in a ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep burning material away from children and pets; the site's [safety guide](/safety/) sets out the general precautions.

Dried flowers add two specific concerns. The first is dust: handling broken flowers and powder releases fine plant matter that can irritate eyes and airways, so work with some airflow and avoid vigorous shaking near the face. The second is mould: a musty lot is not merely lower quality, it is a material that has supported microbial growth, and it should be discarded rather than burned indoors. Anyone with known sensitivity to pollen or plant dust should handle the dried flower cautiously.

Traditional use language appears in older texts and is recorded on this page as tradition. It is not a modern clinical finding, and nothing here should be read as a treatment claim.

## Sourcing checklist

> **Direct answer:** A buyer should confirm the botanical name, the part, the harvest condition, and the storage history — in that order. For a flower with a modest scent, process discipline matters more than any grading label.

The checks, framed as questions to ask before committing to a lot:

1. **Species and part.** Is it *Albizia julibrissin* flower, not bark, not a magnolia, not *Mimosa pudica*?
2. **Colour and condition.** Pink retained, or uniformly brown? Whole panicles, or crushed dust with stems?
3. **Aroma.** Clean and sweet, or hay, dust, and sourness? Smell a sample after rubbing it between the palms; the friction releases what is left.
4. **Moisture.** Crisp and papery, or soft and leathery? Soft material is a mould risk in transit.
5. **Foreign matter.** Stem load, grit, and insects are the usual deductions.
6. **Storage story.** Was it kept sealed and dark, or in an open bag on a shelf?

None of these checks requires a laboratory, and together they answer the only question that matters commercially: is this a clean, faintly scented flower that can do a small job well, or a faded lot being sold on the strength of the tree's reputation?

## FAQ

**What is silk tree flower (合欢花)?** It is the blossom of *Albizia julibrissin*, a legume tree grown across Asia for shade and ornament. The pink, thread-like flowers appear in early summer and carry a soft, sweet, powdery scent.

**Why is the tree called 合欢, "collective joy"?** The name frames the tree as a symbol of harmony and reunion. Its classical aliases 合昏 and 夜合 describe the leaflets folding at dusk, so the naming mixes an emotional meaning with a plain field observation.

**Does 合欢花 smell like jasmine?** No. Jasmine is dense, sweet, and animalic, and it can carry a formula. Silk tree flower is thin, powdery, and diffuse, and it disappears under anything heavier than itself.

**Is it a classical incense ingredient?** Not in the canonical sense. It does not appear as a working material in the 香乘 tradition, and its documented standing is cultural and materia-medica. It can be used as a gentle sachet or accent note, but that is a modern choice rather than a classical pedigree.

**Can the bark be used instead of the flower?** They are different items. 合欢皮 is the bark, recorded separately in the materia-medica tradition, with no floral scent; 合欢花 is the blossom. Substituting one for the other in an aromatic context does not work.

**How can I tell 合欢花 from 夜合花?** Ask for the Latin name. 合欢花 is *Albizia julibrissin*, a legume with a filament-puff flower. 夜合花 in horticultural usage is usually a magnolia, with fleshy petals and a different scent.

## Summary

合欢花 is the flower of *Albizia julibrissin*, a legume tree whose feathery leaves close at night and whose name means collective joy. Its scent is sweet, powdery, and faint; its fame is cultural and materia-medica rather than aromatic. It has no documented place as a staple of the classical incense canon, and the honest way to use it is as a quiet accent in sachets and delicate blends, judged on freshness and cleanliness rather than on the reputation of the tree.

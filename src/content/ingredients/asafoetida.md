**Direct answer:** Asafoetida (阿魏, āwèi) is a gum-resin tapped from the root crown of *Ferula assa-foetida*, a tall umbellifer that grows on dry steppe and mountain ground from Central Asia across the Iranian plateau. Its character is split in two: the cold lump is sulfurous and alliaceous to the point of offence, while a heated pinch turns savory, deep, and faintly resinous. Chinese incense lists keep it among the 香药 (imported aromatics) and treat it as a pinch-scale accent rather than a base material.

## A resin that is judged twice

**Direct answer:** Asafoetida has two distinct smells, and only the second one matters in a blend. The cold resin is harsh and sulfurous; the heated resin is savory and deep. A buyer who smells only the lump is testing the material at its least useful moment.

The raw material arrives as dark, dull lumps with an uneven surface. The smell is not subtle. Sulfur comes first, then garlic, then onion, with a fermented edge underneath and a resinous tail that lingers on the fingers long after the lump is back in its jar. In a tradition built on sweet woods, clean resins, and flowers, this registers as a fault rather than a feature.

That first impression is misleading, and the reason is practical rather than poetic. Asafoetida is almost never used cold. In South Asian cooking the resin — known there as hing — is bloomed in hot fat before it meets the dish, because the raw material would overwhelm the food and the heated material gives it a savory backbone. The same logic holds in a burner or a charcoal disc. The note the material contributes is created by heat, not merely released by it.

What follows from this is an inversion worth stating plainly. Most aromatics are at their best in the jar: citrus peel, rose, jasmine, and the lighter woods all lose definition when they burn. Asafoetida is the reverse. Its cold state is a liability, and its value appears only after the temperature rises. A formulator who judges it by the cold smell will reject a material that would have worked, and a buyer who judges it by the cold smell will pay for the wrong thing.

There is a second consequence, less obvious. Because the useful note is generated on heating, the dose cannot be copied from a cold-smell evaluation. A pinch that seems timid in the jar can dominate a room once lit. The material has to be tested the way it will be used: on heat, in the presence of the other ingredients, at the dose the final formula will carry.

## The transformation, step by step

**Direct answer:** Heat does not add a new note to asafoetida; it changes which fraction of the resin the nose receives. The lightest sulfur compounds leave first, the sharp edge thins, and the heavier savory fraction takes over.

The sequence the nose reports runs like this:

cold resin → heat applied → the most volatile sulfur compounds enter the air first → the garlic-onion edge thins → the heavier resinous-savory fraction dominates → the note reads as allium depth rather than stink

This is a reading of differential volatility, not a laboratory assay, and the page labels it as such. The practical point stands either way: the same lump produces two different impressions depending on how fast and how hot it is heated. A gentle, indirect heat gives the savory transition time to appear. A fierce, direct heat pushes everything off at once and leaves an acrid, burnt-sulfur edge that smells like a kitchen accident.

A second chain explains why the material is so easy to overdose:

too much resin in the blend → the sulfur fraction exceeds what the other ingredients can absorb → the savory note turns back into a raw allium stink → the whole formula smells of garlic rather than of depth

The threshold is low. That is why the classical use is a trace dose, and why the material belongs in the seasoning category rather than the base category. It is not a weaker version of a sweet resin; it is a different tool, with a narrow working range.

One more mechanism matters for anyone building a formula on paper. The transition happens during the burn, not before it. A blend that smells balanced cold can turn sharp in the first minute and mellow in the fifth, because the asafoetida is still changing while the lighter components are already gone. Test burns at two or three time points tell you more than a single impression, and they tell you far more than a jar smell.

## Botany: one genus, two 阿魏s

**Direct answer:** 阿魏 is *Ferula assa-foetida*, a giant Apiaceae of Central Asia and Iran. 新疆阿魏 is *Ferula sinkiangensis*, a Chinese relative from Xinjiang. The two are close enough to share a name layer and different enough that a supplier should say which one is in the bag.

*Ferula assa-foetida* is a big plant by carrot-family standards, flowering on thick stems above dry, open ground. It belongs to the same family as fennel, cumin, carrot, and galbanum, and it shares with those plants a habit of bleeding aromatic exudate when wounded. The range runs across Central Asia and the Iranian plateau into Afghanistan, and the resin has been collected from wild stands for a very long time.

The material is not a bark, a wood, or a leaf. It is a resin, tapped from the living root and the base of the stem, and it reaches the market as a hardened gum rather than as a manufactured extract. That matters for how it should be judged: there is no distillation step to standardize it, no fractionation to concentrate one component, and no blending step unless a seller adds one.

Chinese materia medica also recognizes a native relative. 新疆阿魏, *Ferula sinkiangensis*, grows in Xinjiang and is treated as a regional source of the same kind of resin. The botanical relationship is close; the trade names are close; the chemistry is not identical. A page about 阿魏 that does not say which species it means is a page with a hole in it.

> **A naming caution:** two different plants sit under the 阿魏 name layer. *Ferula assa-foetida* is the resin that arrived through trade from the west; 新疆阿魏 (*Ferula sinkiangensis*) is a Xinjiang native. They are kin, not synonyms. This entry covers the imported classical material and treats the native plant as a regional source with a separate chain of supply.

## The name is a travel document

**Direct answer:** 阿魏 is a transliteration, so the word preserves the material's foreignness instead of describing the plant. That single fact places it among the 香药 — aromatics that arrived through trade — rather than in the native 香材 canon.

Chinese material names usually describe something. 细辛 means "thin and pungent." 沉香 means "sinking fragrance." A loanword does different work: it records a route. 阿魏 is generally traced to a Persian or Central Asian source word for the resin, and the exact philological path is a question for linguists rather than perfumers. What survives in the name is the direction of travel.

That direction matters because the 香药 category had its own prestige and its own supply chain. Imported aromatics arrived through Silk Road trade, were handled by merchants rather than by local collectors, and carried associations with distance and cost. They also brought registers the native palette used sparingly: animalic, medicinal, savory, and sharp. Asafoetida sits at the far end of that range.

The native 香材 tradition was not narrow, but it was coherent. Sweet woods, balsamic resins, floral absolutes, and the citrus-peel notes of the south formed a palette in which the pleasant and the useful overlapped. A material that smelled of garlic and sulfur did not fit that overlap. It entered as a medicine, a fixative, and a curiosity — an aromatic whose job was penetration rather than beauty.

That framing is the most useful thing the name can tell a reader. 阿魏 is not a Chinese plant with a Chinese name that happens to sound foreign. It is a foreign material whose foreignness was preserved in the word, and the preservation is a small piece of evidence about how the incense trade actually worked.

## How the resin leaves the plant

**Direct answer:** Harvest is a wound-and-collect operation: the root and lower stem are cut, the exudate hardens in the air, and the dark gum is lifted, cleaned, and sealed. No still, solvent, or concentrator enters the classical chain.

The chain is short and entirely manual:

root and lower stem cut → milky exudate released → air exposure begins → gum coagulates and darkens → hardened lumps collected → cleaned and sorted → sealed for storage and trade

Because the material is a wound response, its quality begins with the condition of the plant and the care of the cut. A clean cut on a healthy root gives a cleaner exudate; a rough cut on a stressed plant gives a lump full of bark fragments and grit. Nothing later in the chain removes that contamination completely, which is why the appearance of the lump is worth reading even though appearance alone cannot grade the material.

An exudate dries from the outside in. The surface meets the air first, hardens first, and darkens first; the interior stays softer for longer and holds more of the volatile fraction. Break a tear and the fresh face usually smells sharper than the weathered skin. That is a useful sampling trick and also a warning: a seller who lets you smell only the outside is showing you the least informative surface of the material.

Nothing later in the chain refines the gum. What reaches the buyer is hardened resin, ground or dissolved to suit the formula, and any further treatment is a decision made downstream rather than at the point of harvest. For incense work that is an advantage — nothing has been added — and a responsibility, because nothing has been standardized either.

## The forms on the market

**Direct answer:** The trade sells the same name in several forms: whole resin tears, larger lump gum, resin ground without a carrier, and compounded culinary powder cut with a flour carrier. They are not interchangeable, and the difference is dilution.

| Form | What it is | Best use | What to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resin tears | small hardened lumps, unground | incense, 合香, reference samples | species, region, harvest year |
| Lump gum | larger pieces, often with field debris | in-house cleaning and grinding | foreign-matter level, moisture |
| Pure ground resin | resin milled without additives | workshop blending by weight | when it was ground, how stored |
| Compounded powder | resin cut with a carrier such as rice flour | cooking, everyday kitchen use | percentage of actual resin |

The first three are the material. The fourth is a convenience product, and its convenience is exactly the problem for anyone formulating by weight: a recipe that calls for asafoetida and receives compounded powder is a recipe with an unknown dose. The resin percentage is the number that matters, and it is often absent from the label.

Grinding introduces its own issues. A resin that is milled and then stored in a warm place can cake, and the aromatic surface exposed by grinding is larger than the surface of a whole tear. Ground material therefore ages faster in the aromatic sense even when it keeps perfectly well in the physical sense. Grind small batches, store them sealed, and expect the ground material to read differently from the lump it came from.

For incense work the practical preference is whole resin, cleaned and broken as needed. It is slower to use and easier to trust. The one exception is a workshop that has already validated a ground lot and can document its storage history; in that case the convenience is real and the risk is managed.

## What "quality" means at a pinch

**Direct answer:** Because asafoetida is dosed in traces, quality is potency and cleanliness rather than size or shine. A modest, clean resin that still transforms under heat is worth more than a large, handsome lump that smells flat.

No formal grading standard for incense-grade asafoetida exists in the sources consulted for this page, and this encyclopedia does not invent one. What can be offered is a screening table: what a signal suggests, and what it cannot prove.

| Signal | What it suggests | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Dark, uneven tears with a dull surface | slow drying, minimal polishing, some field debris | nothing about species or resin content |
| Sharp sulfur on a freshly broken face | volatiles still retained inside the lump | how the material will behave at dose |
| Flat, dusty, or weakly resinous smell | long storage, heat exposure, or an old lot | that the material is adulterated |
| Grit or sand inside the lump | collection without cleaning | a safety verdict on the whole lot |
| Uniform pale powder sold as resin | a compounded product cut with a carrier | the actual resin percentage |
| Soft, tacky lumps that clump in the jar | moisture uptake during storage | that the resin was poor when packed |

Read the table as a set of questions rather than a scorecard. A dark lump with field debris can be excellent once cleaned; a clean, uniform lump can be old and flat. The signals narrow the conversation, and the conversation is what protects a buyer.

The potency question is the one that resists visual grading. A resin can look right and smell weak, or look rough and smell fierce. The only reliable check available to a small buyer is behavioral: break the lump, smell the fresh face, then heat a very small fragment on a burner or a hot plate and smell again. The difference between the cold and heated impressions is the material's usable range. A lump that barely changes has little left to give.

Editorial judgment follows from this: for incense use, buy small quantities of a material you have tested, and treat size, gloss, and uniformity as packaging rather than as evidence. The material is cheap enough per gram at trace doses that a bad kilo is a waste of money and a good hundred grams is a year of work.

## Testing a lot before it enters a formula

**Direct answer:** A lot is tested in the order it will be used: cold, then heated, then heated beside its intended partners. Each step answers a different question, and skipping the third is the most common mistake.

The sequence below is a bench routine, not a laboratory method. It assumes nothing beyond a burner, a knife, and small samples of the materials the blend will carry.

1. **Fix the identity first.** Species, plant part, and trade form. A label reading only "asafoetida" has not answered the question that decides everything downstream, because the powder and the resin are different materials at different dilutions.
2. **Break the lump; do not sniff the skin.** The weathered surface has already lost its most volatile fraction. The fresh face is where the resin still holds what the burner will release.
3. **Heat a fragment indirectly.** A low, gentle source gives the transition time to happen. Direct flame drives everything off at once and tells you about combustion, not about the resin.
4. **Listen for three phases.** Opening sulfur, middle savor, tail resin. A lot that never leaves the first phase has little useful range; a lot that jumps straight to the tail is old or heat-damaged.
5. **Repeat beside the partners.** Alone, the resin can read balanced. Next to sandalwood it may vanish; next to frankincense it may turn sour. The blend is the only honest test.
6. **Find the garlic line.** Raise the dose in small steps until the room smells of garlic rather than depth. The working dose sits below that line, and the line is closer than most formulators expect.
7. **Retest after storage.** Ground resin drifts faster than whole tears. A lot that passed months ago is not automatically the lot you have now.

| Test | What it answers | What it cannot answer |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh-face smell, cold | whether volatiles are still present | species, or behaviour at dose |
| Gentle heat, alone | whether the savory transition happens | how the blend will read |
| Heat beside partners | masking, clash, or support | long-term storage stability |
| Dose ladder | the upper bound before garlic dominates | intrinsic quality of the resin |
| Retest after storage | aromatic drift since purchase | condition at harvest |

The table is deliberately modest. None of these tests grades a resin the way a lab would grade an essential oil, and none of them substitutes for knowing the supplier. What they do is convert a vague impression into observations that can be repeated, compared, and argued about.

One more habit is worth building. Keep a small reference sample of every lot that worked, sealed and labelled, and smell it beside each new purchase. Memory for smells is poor and suggestible; a physical reference is not. A workshop that keeps three or four such samples can detect a drift in its supply long before a customer does.

## Where it sits in a 合香 formula

**Direct answer:** In the 合香 frame, asafoetida is neither a chief note nor a base. It is a trace modifier, closer to a seasoning than to an ingredient, and its job is savory depth plus a hold that sweet resins cannot supply.

The classical framework of 君臣佐使 (chief, minister, assistant, guide) assigns roles rather than quantities. The chief note defines the composition; the minister supports it; the assistant modifies, balances, or corrects; the guide carries the blend and shapes how it opens. A material can be excellent and still belong in the assistant role, and asafoetida is the clearest case in this encyclopedia.

Its contribution is not a smell that a reader would call beautiful in isolation. It is a shift in the surrounding blend: a savory undertone that makes sweet woods read deeper, a faint animalic weight that keeps a floral composition from turning sugary, and a persistence that helps the heart of the blend hold together. Traditional perfumery language calls this a fixative effect — a traditional belief rather than a measured claim, but one with a long record behind it.

The register map below places the material against the other families this site covers:

| Register | Materials that usually occupy it | What asafoetida does there |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet-woody | Sandalwood, agarwood | Adds a savory floor under the sweetness, in traces |
| Balsamic-resinous | Frankincense, benzoin, galbanum | Cuts the clean resinous line with a sulfurous edge |
| Floral | Jasmine, champaca, rose | Adds weight and a faint animalic shadow |
| Green | Galbanum, violet leaf | Clashes unless the dose is very low |
| Savory-allium | 阿魏 alone, in this encyclopedia | Defines the register; no substitute within the canon |

The map is editorial, not a rulebook. Its purpose is to make one point concrete: asafoetida is not a general-purpose intensifier. It is a specific register with a narrow window, and the blends that use it well are the ones that wanted that register in the first place.

## What it does to its neighbours

**Direct answer:** Asafoetida changes the materials around it more than it changes itself. In traces it adds a savory floor and a holding weight; past that line it converts every nearby note into garlic.

A trace material is usually described by what it contributes. Asafoetida is better described by what it removes: sugar from a sweet wood, cleanliness from a balsamic line, air from a heavy floral. That removal is the effect. The resin is doing its job when a blend smells deeper and slightly less pretty than it did without it.

The interaction map below is editorial, drawn from the register logic used elsewhere on this site rather than from a formal trial:

| Neighbour | What the resin adds | Where it goes wrong | Working rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sweet woods (sandalwood, agarwood) | a savory floor; less sugar | a garlic shadow over the wood | stay below the point where the wood disappears |
| Clean balsamics (frankincense) | contrast; a sulfurous cut | a sour, dirty line | keep the resin at the edge, not the centre |
| Florals (jasmine, rose) | weight; a faint animalic shadow | a decay note | use only where a heavy floral is intended |
| Green resins (galbanum) | nothing it does not already have | a sharp, metallic collision | choose one of the two, not both |
| Bitter roots (costus, calamus) | doubles the medicinal register | the blend reads as a prescription | decide which register the formula wants |
| Animalics (musk, ambergris) | reinforced depth | heaviness; loss of air | lower the animalic when adding this resin |

The mechanism behind the last column is straightforward. Asafoetida does not sit in the blend as a separate note; it raises the floor under everything else. Raise the floor far enough and the peaks disappear. That is why a material dosed at a fraction of a percent can change the identity of a formula, and why the failure mode is not "too much asafoetida" but "no longer able to smell the sandalwood."

The chain runs like this:

resin loaded past the working threshold → sulfur volatiles saturate the airstream → the nose stops resolving the sweet components → the blend collapses into one allium note → the formula reads as cooking rather than incense

The practical conclusion is uncomfortable for anyone who likes the material. The correct dose is usually lower than the one that smells interesting on the spoon. Judge the blend, not the material, and judge it after the first minute rather than at the moment of lighting.

An editorial note on "fixative": the word is often used as if it named a measurable physical effect. Here it names a traditional perfumery judgment — the blend holds together longer and reads as one object rather than several. It does not mean the resin has been tested for fixative performance, and this page does not claim that.

## Four confusions worth clearing up

**Direct answer:** Most mistakes about asafoetida come from four conflations: resin with garlic, 阿魏 with 新疆阿魏, compounded powder with resin, and raw strength with blend strength. Each has a simple correction.

| Confusion | What is actually true | Why it persists |
|---|---|---|
| Asafoetida is a kind of garlic | It is a *Ferula* resin; the garlic note comes from sulfur compounds, not from an *Allium* plant | The nose files sulfur under garlic, and the nose is fast |
| 阿魏 and 新疆阿魏 are the same | Two related species under one name layer, with different ranges | Trade names travel faster than taxonomy |
| The powder sold in shops is the resin | Compounded powder is resin cut with a carrier, often a flour | Most cooks meet the material in powder form |
| A strong cold smell means a strong material | Raw intensity is not blend performance; heat rewrites the impression | Cold evaluation is the easiest test to run |

The first confusion is the most consequential for formulators. A material that is assumed to be "garlic-like" gets filed next to culinary alliums and judged by the same expectations. Asafoetida is not an allium and does not behave like one: it has no allicin chemistry and no fresh-chopped texture, and its note is a resin's note, delivered slowly from a solid matrix.

The second is a labeling problem with a safety dimension. Two species under one name means the buyer cannot know which species is in the bag unless the supplier says so. That is not a reason to avoid the material; it is a reason to ask a question that has a checkable answer.

## Reading the classical record without overreading it

**Direct answer:** The classical record documents asafoetida as an imported aromatic and as a materia-medica substance. It does not document a modern clinical effect, and the safe reading keeps the aromatic claim while dropping the therapeutic one.

阿魏 appears in the 本草綱目, in the 木部 section, and it appears in the incense literature as one of the 香药. Those are two different kinds of record. The incense record is about behavior in a blend: pungency, penetration, fixative hold. The materia-medica record is about a system of correspondences and uses that belongs to its own intellectual world.

The descriptive language in the medical record — warming, penetrating, dispersing — is a category language, not a diagnosis. It tells a reader how the material was classified, not what it does in a body. This page keeps that distinction explicit and does not translate traditional categories into modern medical claims.

| Layer | Status | What it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established | *Ferula assa-foetida*, Apiaceae; *F. sinkiangensis* as the native relative |
| Aromatic use | Historically documented | 香药 status and 合香 appearance |
| Traditional medicinal use | Traditional belief | 本草綱目 木部, read as a historical record |
| Modern clinical efficacy | Not established | no claim made on this page |

A reader who wants the aromatic material can take the first two rows and stop. A reader interested in the history of materia medica can take the third row as history. Nobody should take the fourth row as available, because it is not.

## Storage and the odor problem

**Direct answer:** Asafoetida must be stored sealed because its odor migrates. A jar that is merely closed will perfume everything on the shelf beside it, and the contamination runs one way.

The practical regime is glass, a tight lid, a second barrier such as a sealed bag, and a shelf away from absorbent materials. Paper, cardboard, and loose tea will take the smell within days. A workshop that stores resins together should give this one its own container and its own corner; the cost of doing so is trivial compared with the cost of a batch of clean frankincense that now smells of sulfur.

Temperature and moisture matter for a different reason. The resin softens when warm, and a soft lump takes up moisture and dust more readily than a hard one. A cool, dark cupboard is enough. Refrigeration is not necessary and introduces a condensation cycle every time the jar is opened.

The storage problem has an aromatic dimension that is easy to miss: the material loses its sharp top over time, and the loss is not always a decline. An old, mellowed lump can be easier to dose because the harsh edge has already gone. What it cannot do is recover the transition. A resin that no longer changes when heated has lost the property that made it useful, and no storage method restores it.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The hazard in asafoetida incense is the smoke, not a special toxicity of the resin. Burning plant material produces fine particulate matter, and this material's smoke carries a sulfurous edge that most people find sharp.

Burn in a ventilated space, keep the burner away from curtains and children, and never leave a lit charcoal disc unattended. The dose should stay small for aromatic reasons as well as safety ones; a large piece produces a harsh, choking smoke and no better scent. Anyone with a respiratory condition should be cautious around any incense smoke, not only this one.

Do not ingest the resin as a medicine, do not take essential oil of asafoetida internally, and do not apply a concentrated resin to skin. The medicinal language in the classical sources is read here as traditional belief; the page makes no clinical claim. Nothing here is medical advice; it is editorial reference material. See our [safety guide](/safety/) for the site's general combustion guidance.

## The editorial view: the anti-perfume

**Direct answer:** Asafoetida is the anti-perfume of the classical palette: a material whose cold smell is a defect and whose value is created by heat. Its presence in the 香药 canon shows that Chinese incense was not only a sweet-smelling project.

Three judgments follow, and they are editorial rather than documentary.

- **The inversion is the material's identity.** Most aromatics are pleasant in the jar and degrade on the burner. 阿魏 is useless in the jar and useful on the burner. A page that describes only the garlic smell has described the material's packaging, not its behavior. The useful description is the transition.
- **The canon was wider than "pleasant."** The 香药 category is often flattened into luxury goods with sweet smells. 阿魏's place in it is evidence against that flattening: the tradition made room for a material that smelled of sulfur and onions because it did something no sweet resin could do. The register existed; the trade carried it; the formulators used it in traces.
- **The naming layer is a sourcing problem, not a curiosity.** Because 阿魏 is a loanword and 新疆阿魏 is a native relative, the name covers at least two species. A buyer who asks "is this 阿魏?" has asked a question about a word. A buyer who asks "which *Ferula*, from where, and is the resin cut?" has asked a question about a material.

Our reading is narrower than the marketing: this is the pungent outlier of the classical 香药, a savory resin whose worth is manufactured by heat, and whose name covers more than one species. It is not a perfume. It is a seasoning for perfume — which explains both why it is hard to use and why it stayed in the repertoire.

## Summary

The material is a root-derived gum-resin from *Ferula assa-foetida*, a tall umbellifer of the Central Asian steppe and the Iranian plateau; 新疆阿魏 (*Ferula sinkiangensis*) is its named native relative. Cold, it reads as sulfur and garlic; hot, it reads as savory depth, and the blend only ever meets the hot version. Chinese incense keeps it among the 香药 as a trace accent and fixative. Four practical points carry the entry: the heat-driven inversion, the species question, the carrier hidden in culinary powder, and the storage discipline its migrating odor demands.

## FAQ

**What is asafoetida (阿魏)?** A gum-resin collected from the root crown of *Ferula assa-foetida*, an umbellifer of the Central Asian steppe and the Iranian plateau. Cold, it is sharply sulfurous and garlicky; heated, it turns savory and deep. Chinese records list it as an imported aromatic and as a materia-medica substance.

**Why does asafoetida smell of garlic if it is not a garlic?** Sulfur compounds in the resin — disulfides among them — produce the note; the plant has no *Allium* chemistry. The nose files sulfur under garlic because that is the closest familiar match. The plant is a *Ferula* in the carrot family, and its behavior in a blend is a resin's behavior, not a bulb's.

**Is 阿魏 the same as 新疆阿魏?** No. The first is the imported resin of *Ferula assa-foetida*; the second is *Ferula sinkiangensis*, a Xinjiang native sold as a regional source. Related species, shared name layer, different plants — a supplier should be able to say which one is in the bag.

**Does compounded culinary powder work in incense?** It can be burned, but it is not the same material. Compounded powder is resin cut with a carrier such as rice flour, so a formula written for resin will receive an unknown fraction of resin and an unplanned amount of starch. For incense work, buy whole resin or pure ground resin and dose by weight.

**How much asafoetida belongs in a blend?** There is no universal number, and this page will not invent one. The working rule is that the material should be present as a trace — enough to deepen the blend, not enough to name itself. Test on heat at increasing doses, starting far below what seems necessary, because the savory note and the garlic note sit very close together.

Two GBIF records anchor the taxonomy: [Ferula assa-foetida on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/8113074) for the accepted species and [Ferula sinkiangensis (新疆阿魏) on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/3636475) for the Chinese relative. **Status:** established.

### Traditional Chinese use
The 香药 status comes from the historical incense record; the 本草綱目 places the material in its 木部. Aromatic use is documented history. The medicinal vocabulary attached to it belongs to the same historical record and is read here as traditional belief, not as clinical evidence.

### Authenticity and disambiguation
Separating 阿魏 from 新疆阿魏 is a screening judgment built on taxonomy, not a lab assay. Three questions do most of the work: which species, which region, which trade form.

### Safety
Any incense fire produces smoke and fine particles. Ventilate the room. This is general combustion guidance, not a toxicological review of this resin.

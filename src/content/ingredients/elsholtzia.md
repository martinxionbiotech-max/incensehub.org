> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia (香薷, xiāngrú) is the dried, above-ground herb of a mint-family annual, most often *Elsholtzia ciliata*, and it brings a minty-spicy, faintly sweet green lift to Chinese incense. The classical texts file it under the fragrant herbs (芳草) and list it among the 香乘 aromatics, but its place inside a blend is as a brightening top note, never as the burned anchor.

## Key takeaways

A handful of fixed points anchor everything that follows. The plant is *Elsholtzia ciliata*, a member of the mint family, and the material in use is its above-ground herb — leaves together with flowering tops — gathered and dried. The Chinese name, 香薷 (xiāngrú), shows up in the older texts with the alternate writings 香柔, 香茸, and 蜜蜂草. The scent lives in the mint register but warms it: herbal and spicy first, then a faint sweetness, with a camphor-like lift riding on top. In the classical record it is a fragrant herb (芳草) and a listed fragrant in the 香乘 — never a wood or a resin.

## What the name actually records

> **Direct answer:** The characters 香薷 are a smell claim, not a borrowed label. The *Bencao Gangmu* notes that 薷 was once written 葇 and that the plant took its name because its smell is fragrant and its leaves are soft — 其氣香，其葉柔，故以名之.

Li Shizhen's *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), inside the fragrant-herb section (草之三), traces the name and lists the variants 香柔 ("fragrant-soft"), 香茸 ("fragrant-downy"), and 蜜蜂草 ("honeybee herb"). The gloss it gives — "其氣香，其葉柔，故以名之" — boils down to two traits: fragrant smell, soft leaves.

What sits behind the etymology is a classification decision, and it matters more than the wordplay. Li Shizhen took the plant out of the vegetable section and re-filed it among the fragrant herbs (芳草), a move that treats aroma, rather than edibility, as the plant's defining feature. The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) then records 香薷 in its roster of aromatics. A reader can watch the plant migrate across sources — from potherb to 芳草 to listed fragrant — and that migration is itself the evidence of where the herb belongs.

## A soft annual explains both the brightness and the fade

> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia is a soft, leafy annual rather than a woody perennial, and that single structural fact accounts for both its vivid opening and its quick disappearance. Annuals spend their fragrant effort on light leaf oils that peak in one season.

The herb's behaviour as a scent material follows from its life history. A mint-family annual keeps its fragrance in tiny glands on the leaf surface, filled with light terpenoid oils — small carbon-and-hydrogen molecules, several carrying oxygen. The causal chain governing everything downstream runs: **small, light oil molecules → a low boiling point → fast release the instant warmth touches the leaf → a vivid burst of green scent → rapid exhaustion of the oil.** The very lightness that makes the opening bright is what makes it brief.

That mechanism slots the herb into a 合香 (blended incense) formula. In the classical chief–minister–assistant–guide arrangement, a material like 香薷 occupies the assistant or guide layer: it opens the top of the scent and lifts the heavier resins underneath, but it never carries the base. The same lightness also degrades the note in storage, where the chain runs: **age plus warmth plus exposure to air → slow oxidation of the leaf oils → loss of the spicy-green top → a flat, hay-like residue.** Freshness, in short, is not a courtesy for this ingredient — it is the entire specification.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Elsholtzia ciliata* is an annual mint-family herb, and Flora of China fixes its shape: erect plants 30–50 cm tall, purplish flowers from July to October, fruiting through to January. The genus holds several East Asian fragrant herbs, with *E. ciliata* the one a reader meets most.

Flora of China records the species as **erect herbs 30–50 cm tall**, the stems glabrous or pilose and turning purple-brown with age. The leaves are **ovate to elliptic-lanceolate, 3–9 × 1–4 cm**, with a serrate margin and an acuminate tip. The flowers are **purplish corollas** about 4.5 mm long, packed into dense spikes 2–7 cm, and the plant sets yellow-brown nutlets. Flowering runs **July–October**, fruiting October–January. The reference points are [Elsholtzia ciliata in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200019619) and the [GBIF record](https://www.gbif.org/species/2927075).

Both *E. ciliata* and the related *E. splendens* are gathered for their volatile oils, so the genus name alone underspecifies the material. For a buyer, the species written on the label is the thing to pin down, not just "elsholtzia."

## A common herb, not a rarity

> **Direct answer:** *Elsholtzia ciliata* spans East and Southeast Asia, and Flora of China records it in every Chinese province except Qinghai and Xinjiang, from sea level to 3,400 m. It is a widely gathered herb, not a regional or scarce one.

The species holds a broad native and adventive range. Flora of China places it in **all provinces except Qinghai and Xinjiang**, across habitats from **hills, waste ground, and sunny terraces to riverbanks and forest edges at 0–3,400 m**, and extends the range through Cambodia, India, Japan, Laos, Malaysia, Mongolia, Myanmar, Russia, Thailand, and Vietnam, with introductions into Europe and North America.

That breadth has a direct commercial meaning. Because the plant colonizes disturbed, open ground across many regions, it is gathered rather than farmed to any meaningful degree, which keeps it cheap and plentiful. A buyer should read 香薷 as a common green fragrant, not a collector's item — the scarcity premium attached to aged agarwood or wild sandalwood does not apply to a weedy annual mint. The trade-off is variability: with wild gathering spread over so many regions, the note's intensity drifts from lot to lot, which is why aroma-on-warming, not origin, is the practical check.

## The aroma, in detail

> **Direct answer:** 香薷 reads as a clean green fragrant — minty and herbal, with a spicy edge, a hint of sweetness, and a camphor-like lift — that sits between mint and perilla but warms the register instead of cooling it. It freshens a blend rather than leads it.

香薷 occupies a specific corner of the mint-family register. It is minty and herbal up front, with a spicy undertone, a touch of sweetness, and a camphor-adjacent lift that stops it collapsing into plain mint. Set against its nearest relatives, the differences sharpen: it is not as sharply cooling as true mint (薄荷, *Mentha*), and not as anise-leaning or shiso-like as perilla (紫苏, *Perilla frutescens*). That warmer, softer cast is the herb's signature.

What this means in practice is that elsholtzia is a *brightener*, not a *leader*. A heavy sandalwood or agarwood blend gains a green, airy top when a little 香薷 is added, because the herb's oils lift off early and clear the nose for the deeper notes underneath. The trade-off is the same physics viewed from the other side: a note this volatile also leaves early, so its contribution lives and dies in the opening minutes of a burn unless the formula is tuned to hold it.

Elsholtzia is minty-spicy and slightly sweet, agastache is minty-anise, and perilla is shiso-like and anise-leaning.

The mint family contributes a dense cluster of green aromatics to Chinese incense, and three sit close enough that a label reading only "fragrant herb" tells a buyer almost nothing:

Three species, three distinct notes:

## Why it is used in incense

> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia enters a 合香 blend as a fresh green fragrant — a minty-spicy top or mid note that lifts heavier woods and resins without leading the scent. Its value is freshness and lift, not sweetness or depth.

香薷's classical role is as a *fresh green fragrant*: a minty-spicy note that brightens a blend and lifts the heavier woods and resins without ever stepping in front of them. The historical record supports this framing — the *Bencao Gangmu* groups it among the 芳草, and the *Xiangcheng* lists it as an fragrant — but neither source treats it as a burned base or a fixative. It is a material you reach for when a blend has gone heavy and needs air, not one you build a scent around.

A useful image: 香薷 does for a blend what a squeeze of fresh leaf does for a rich dish — it cuts the density, opens the top, then steps back. In practical terms it is a supporting top or mid note, and its contribution is almost entirely about the opening. Anyone expecting it to anchor a long burn has misread its chemistry; the base must come from elsewhere.

## Processing: drying, not tapping

> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia is used as the dried aerial herb — leaves and flowering tops — gathered and dried to hold the volatile oils. Processing is careful drying, not resin-tapping or distillation, and the goal is to keep the minty-spicy note from escaping before it reaches the blend.

A leafy mint-family herb like this one is handled by drying, not by any resin-tapping or distillation step. The part gathered is the above-ground herb — leaves plus flowering tops, which is where the oil glands sit — and it is dried so that the volatile oils carrying the minty-spicy note stay put. The practical concern is volatility: the oils are heat- and age-sensitive, so the drying temperature and the storage conditions decide how much of the fresh green character survives. The chain is direct: **over-hot or prolonged drying → premature vaporization of the light oils → a herb that arrives already flat → a blend that opens dull.**

This sets the herb apart from the two other material families it is often confused with. A resin such as frankincense is tapped and hardened, locking its scent into a stable solid; a distilled oil concentrates a single fraction into a sealed liquid. Elsholtzia, by contrast, is a whole-herb material whose aroma rides on oils that are free to leave. The wider herb-handling logic sits within [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/).

## What determines quality

> **Direct answer:** Quality for 香薷 turns on freshness and aroma intensity — a clean, well-dried herb that still smells minty-spicy and slightly sweet when gently warmed. Faded, musty, or heat-damaged material has already lost the volatile note that is the entire point of the ingredient.

The quality factors, in rough order of importance:

1. **Scent strength** — the whole value lives in the minty-spicy, faintly sweet green note, so a vivid clean smell always beats a faded one.
2. **How fresh it is** — the volatile oils decline with age and bad storage, so an old or poorly kept lot reads flat no matter how it looked at harvest.
3. **Physical condition** — clean, well-dried material, with no mold, no surplus stem, and no foreign bits mixed in.
4. **Which species** — *Elsholtzia ciliata* and its relatives, not a vague "mint-family herb," so the buyer knows what green note is really in hand.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder for 香薷 the way there is for graded agarwood. The practical signal is the aroma on gentle warming, which should read minty-spicy and slightly sweet, not musty or faded. A batch that needs heat before it gives up any scent is a batch that has already spent itself.

## The chemistry behind the minty-spicy note

> **Direct answer:** The minty-spicy character of elsholtzia comes from a family of light, oxygen-bearing terpenoids stored in the leaf glands — the same chemical territory that gives mint its coolness and camphor its lift, which is why the note reads "camphor-adjacent" without being either mint or camphor.

Leafy mints share a chemical toolkit, and elsholtzia draws from it selectively. The note is built on small terpenoid molecules — carvone-like, menthone-like, and camphor-like compounds in varying ratios — that differ from one mint-family plant to the next by proportion rather than by presence. The result is that all three greens (elsholtzia, agastache, perilla) smell related, but none is a copy of the others.

The "camphor-adjacent lift" in the description is a proportion judgment, not a claim that the herb contains camphor specifically. What the nose reads as a lift is the shared oxygenated-monoterpene register, and the herb's particular balance of those compounds is what lands it between mint and perilla. The practical point is sensory, not chemical: a buyer should trust the aroma description (minty, spicy, faintly sweet, camphor-like) over any attempt to pin the note to a single compound.

## What elsholtzia pairs well with

> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia is a supporting green note, and it pairs best with materials that need a fresh lift — heavy woods, resins, and spice-forward bases. It is a brightener, so it belongs where a blend has gone dense or dark.

The pairing logic follows directly from the note's lightness. Materials that are heavy, sweet, or resinous gain the most from a green top, while materials that are already bright gain little. A short guide:

- **With sandalwood or agarwood** — a small amount of elsholtzia opens the top and clears the nose before the wood's depth arrives.
- **With spice-forward blends** — the minty-spicy register supports cinnamon, clove, and the warming spices without adding sweetness.
- **With other greens** — elsholtzia, agastache, and perilla can be layered, but the species must be specified, since they overlap and one can swallow another.

These are compositional conventions of practice, not measured rules; a blender should treat them as starting points and adjust by smell.

## How the note unfolds across a burn

> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia is a textbook top note: it opens a burn with a quick green flash and is largely gone by the time the mid and base notes arrive. Understanding that timing is the difference between using it well and wasting it.

A burn is a sequence, not a single smell. When heat touches a blend, the lightest oils leave first, then the middle-weight compounds, then the heaviest. Elsholtzia sits at the light end, so the sequence it produces is: **first heat → the minty-spicy top vaporizes within moments → a bright green opening → the note fades as heavier woods and resins rise → the blend settles into its base.**

This timing has a practical consequence for anyone compounding. If the herb is added in hope of a lasting green character, it will disappoint, because its contribution is front-loaded. If it is added as an opener — a brief lift that clears the nose before sandalwood or agarwood takes over — it does exactly the job intended. The material is not weak; it is simply early.

## Sourcing elsholtzia: the freshness problem in practice

> **Direct answer:** Because the entire value of elsholtzia rides on volatile oils that fade, the supply chain is working against the buyer at every step. Sourcing is less about finding a rare grade and more about finding a lot that has not already gone stale.

The freshness problem is structural, not incidental. A leafy annual is harvested in a burst, dried, packed, shipped, and stored — and at every one of those stages the light oils that carry the minty-spicy note are oxidizing away. The chain runs: **harvest → drying (some loss) → storage (slow loss) → shipping (more loss) → shelf (continued loss) → a buyer receives whatever freshness survived the whole journey.**

What this means for sourcing is that the buyer's leverage is small but real. Three things are controllable: buying from a source that moves stock quickly, buying in a quantity that gets used before it fades, and checking aroma on arrival rather than trusting a label. There is no fixed shelf life published for the material, so the practical rule is to treat every lot as perishable and verify it by smell.

The practical takeaway for a buyer is modest: a common annual gathered in season is a low-risk choice, and there is no CITES-style protection or scarcity premium attached to it. A supplier who claims otherwise — treating a weedy mint as a rare, precious harvest — is telling the buyer more about their marketing than about the plant.

## Reading a supplier's description

> **Direct answer:** The words a seller wraps around elsholtzia can either inform or mislead, and a few phrases are worth reading with skepticism. "Premium," "rare," and "hand-picked wild" often mean more about pricing than about the herb in the bag.

The herb is common and cheap, so a description that leans on scarcity language should raise a flag. Terms like "premium grade," "rare wild harvest," or "artisanal hand-picked" promise a distinction the material does not really have — there is no grading ladder, no rarity tier, and no meaningful premium lot for a weedy annual mint. The honest description names the species, states the form and the pack date, and lets the aroma speak for itself.

The buyer's defence is simple: ignore the adjectives, check the species and the smell. A bag of elsholtzia that is correctly named, recently dried, and still fragrant is a good bag, whatever poetic language the seller attached to it; a flat, faded bag is a poor one, no matter how "premium" the label claims it to be.

## A note on the genus name

> **Direct answer:** The scientific name is an identification label, not an aroma description, and it is worth reading that way. *Elsholtzia* commemorates a seventeenth-century Prussian physician and botanist, Johann Sigismund Elsholtz, while the species epithet *ciliata* points to the fine marginal hairs on the leaves.

The Latin name does a single job — it pins the plant to one species so that a buyer on any continent is discussing the same herb. The genus honours Elsholtz in its Latinised spelling, and the epithet records a physical feature, the leaf's hairy fringe. Neither part of the name says anything about how the herb smells or how it burns, which is exactly the point: the name identifies, and the nose evaluates.

## Summary

Elsholtzia (香薷) is the dried aerial herb of *Elsholtzia ciliata*, an annual mint-family plant of East and Southeast Asia. Its minty-spicy, faintly sweet green note earned it a place among the 芳草 in the *Bencao Gangmu* (which derives the name from "fragrant smell, soft leaves") and a listing in the Ming *Xiangcheng*. It works as a supporting top note whose value is freshness and species clarity, it is distinct from the related agastache and perilla, and its volatile oils are both the reason it brightens a blend and the reason it fades. Its medicinal uses are traditional language, not clinical evidence.

## FAQ

### Q: What plant does 香薷 come from?
香薷 is the dried aerial herb of *Elsholtzia ciliata*, an annual in the mint family that Flora of China records as an erect plant 30–50 cm tall, flowering purple from July to October. It grows wild across much of East and Southeast Asia rather than being a cultivated specialty, and the classical texts record it as 香薷 alongside the names 香柔 and 香茸.

### Q: Where does the name 香薷 come from?
The name is a description. The *Bencao Gangmu* explains that 薷 was earlier written 葇 and that the herb earned the name because its smell is fragrant and its leaves soft — the phrase is 其氣香，其葉柔，故以名之. The alternates 香柔 and 香茸 restate the same two traits, and 蜜蜂草 ("honeybee herb") points to the same sweet, green register.

### Q: How would you describe the scent of elsholtzia?
Minty and herbal first, with a spicy edge, a faint sweetness, and a camphor-like lift, sitting between mint and perilla but warmer than either. It is not as sharply cooling as mint and not as anise-leaning as perilla, and in a blend it reads as a clean green note that freshens the opening rather than leading it.

### Q: Is elsholtzia the same as agastache or perilla?
No — all three are mint-family greens but belong to different genera. Elsholtzia (香薷, *Elsholtzia ciliata*) is minty-spicy and faintly sweet; agastache (藿香, *Agastache rugosa*) is minty-anise; perilla (紫苏, *Perilla frutescens*) leans anise with a shiso character. They overlap in register, which is exactly why the species name has to be specified.

### Q: Does 香薷 appear in the classical incense record?
It does. The *Bencao Gangmu* files it among the fragrant herbs (芳草), and the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) lists it among its aromatics. The documented role is as an fragrant herb that adds a fresh green note to blends, not as a self-sufficient burned material or a fixative.

### Q: What is the point of the herb in a blend?
Its job is lift, not depth. Because its oils are light and volatile, 香薷 opens a blend with a bright green top and then steps back as the heavier woods and resins beneath it take over. It is a supporting note by chemistry, not by convention.

### Q: How do you tell a good lot from a spent one?
By aroma on gentle warming. A good lot smells minty-spicy and slightly sweet; a spent lot smells flat, musty, or hay-like, because the volatile oils have oxidized away with age or poor storage. There is no standardized grading ladder, so the nose is the instrument.

### Q: How should dried elsholtzia be stored?
Cool, dark, and airtight. The oils oxidize faster under warmth, light, and moving air, so the practical setup is a sealed container kept out of sunlight in a cool spot, plus buying only what will be used within a season or two. There is no published shelf life, so treat the herb as perishable rather than shelf-stable.

### Q: Does elsholtzia need a base to burn properly?
Yes, in the sense that it is a top note, not a self-supporting burn. Its oils are light and flash out quickly, so it relies on a heavier wood or resin underneath to carry the burn and give the blend body. On its own it would give a brief green puff and little else.

### Q: Can elsholtzia be confused with other mint-family herbs?
It can, and this is its main sourcing risk rather than adulteration. Agastache and perilla share the green register, so a label that says only "mint-family herb" or "fragrant herb" has not identified the material. The scientific name *Elsholtzia ciliata* is the fix, and then the remaining judgment is freshness.

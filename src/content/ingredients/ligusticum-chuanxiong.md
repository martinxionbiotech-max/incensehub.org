> **Direct answer:** 川芎 (chuānxiōng) is the dried underground stem of *Ligusticum chuanxiong*, a carrot-family plant farmed in Sichuan. Break a piece and the smell arrives at once — lovage, pepper, warm earth, and a bitter tail. Chinese pharmacy treats it as a major root; the incense bench treats it as a quiet structural material. The identification that decides whether a purchase is sound is not the Latin name but the separation from 藁本.

## What the material is

> **Direct answer:** A rhizome, not a taproot: a swollen stem that sits at or just under the soil line, stores the plant's oil, and carries the buds for the next season. That anatomy explains the weight of a good piece, the slowness of its drying, and the way it behaves when ground.

*Ligusticum chuanxiong* is a perennial umbellifer grown as a field crop. Above the soil it is unremarkable — finely divided leaves, flat heads of tiny white flowers, the general outline shared by carrots, parsley, and celery. The harvestable part sits below: a short, knotted stem that thickens at the nodes, throws out feeding roots, and in spring pushes up new growth from buds on its surface.

Cut one open and the difference from a carrot is plain. The interior is solid and slightly spongy, with a ring of vascular tissue and a pith that may hollow as it loses water. Aromatic oil is held in cavities scattered through that tissue, which is why a broken face smells far stronger than an untouched skin. The lumpy exterior, marked by node scars, is where the buds sat; graders read those scars as one of the visual signatures of the drug.

Solidity drives the handling. Water has to migrate out through tissue rather than evaporate off a thin leaf, so a rhizome dries slowly, and it dries unevenly because nodes and internodes are not equally dense. Push the heat and the outside seals while the centre stays damp. Slice too early and the discs cup, then split along the vascular ring. Most of the practical skill with this material is a negotiation with that unevenness.

Cultivation adds a second kind of uniformity. Fields are normally established from planting stock rather than seed, so a block of plants behaves as one crop and quality tracks soil, water, and lifting date more than genetic variation. That is part of why the Sichuan reputation holds: it describes a regional growing system with repeated practice behind it, not a wild population that anyone can gather.

## Nose first: the lovage signature

> **Direct answer:** The scent is a phthalide impression — celery-leaf and lovage at the front, pepper and warm earth behind, bitterness at the end. It is a root smell rather than a flower smell, and it reads as medicinal to most noses because the same compounds turn up in both medicine and soup.

Phthalides are lactone compounds with a distinctive green-celery lift. Ligustilide is the member most often named in this root, and it is also the most useful single indicator of freshness: it evaporates, it oxidises, and its presence is what a nose registers as "live" material rather than stored material.

Behind the volatiles sits a fraction that does not travel in smoke. Phenolic acids, ferulic acid among them, are heavier and largely stay in the residue. That split matters because the smell of the jar and the smell of the ember are not the same event: heat distils some compounds, decomposes others, and sends a simplified mixture into the room.

| Fraction | Examples | What heat does |
|---|---|---|
| Volatile phthalides | Ligustilide and its relatives | Distil early and carry most of the aroma |
| Minor terpenes | Various monoterpenes | Add sharpness and lift at the opening |
| Phenolic acids | Ferulic acid | Mostly remain in the ash and residue |
| Structural tissue | Cellulose, lignin | Chars, glows, and generates smoke |

The practical result is a middle note. Phthalides leave faster than a resin but slower than anise or citrus, so they arrive after the bright opening and before the heavy base has fully opened. A composition without a middle can feel like two separate smells in the same room; this root is one way to join them.

Bitterness is the other half of the signature. In smoke it reads as depth to one nose and as harshness to another, and the difference is mostly dose. At a trace it gives a blend a grown-up, rooty weight. Pushed harder it takes over and the room smells like a dispensary. No fixed threshold exists — the formula, the format, and the size of the room all move it — but the direction is reliable.

## Where it sits in a blend

> **Direct answer:** It is a bridge, not a lead. Chuanxiong gives an earthy spine that holds sweet or woody material together and keeps a composition from reading flat. It cannot carry a formula on its own, and it should not be asked to.

A useful way to think about a blend is by three jobs: brightness, body, and the connection between them. This root does the connection. Its pepper cuts through materials that would otherwise cloy, and its earthiness gives resins something to stand on. Used well it disappears into the whole; used badly it is the only thing anyone notices.

The mechanism is volatility timing. Phthalides occupy the middle of the range, so they show up as the top notes thin and before the base has warmed into itself. That timing, more than any romantic idea about "harmony", is what makes the material structural.

| Situation | What it contributes | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet floral heart | An earthy counterweight | Too much reads as medicine |
| Resinous base | A herbal edge under the resin | Muddies a delicate resin |
| Woody core | A bridge from wood to spice | Fights sandalwood's creaminess |
| Medicinal-style formula | Reinforces the root character | Becomes monotonous |
| Bright citrus opening | Little, and often a clash | Usually the wrong material |

A working rule follows: add it late, in small steps, and judge the air rather than the powder. A jar that smells right can burn entirely differently, because heat selects which compounds actually travel. The only honest test is a small burn in the room where the finished product will be used.

There is a physical side too. Dense tissue grinds to a powder that packs tightly, which can stiffen an extruded stick and slow its draw. In a loose blend the same powder adds body. Neither effect is a defect, but both mean the root should be treated as a formulation ingredient with mechanical consequences, not only as a scent.

## Two binomials, one drug

> **Direct answer:** Two names circulate for the same cultivated material. Chinese and older European sources use *Ligusticum chuanxiong*; several modern databases place it under *Conioselinum anthriscoides*. The plant did not change — the labels did — and 川芎 remains the field that stays stable across documents.

Umbellifer taxonomy drifts for understandable reasons. Genera in this family are separated by fruit anatomy that photographs badly, and molecular work has moved species across genus lines. The result for a buyer is mundane: either binomial can appear on a certificate, a flora entry, or a supplier's sheet, and sometimes both appear on the same page.

Paperwork mismatches cost money. A quality officer comparing a purchase order with a spec sheet can reject a correct consignment because the names differ, or accept a wrong one because they match. The remedy is to stop treating the binomial as the whole identity and to require the Chinese drug name beside it.

| Paperwork | Name likely to appear | Field worth checking |
|---|---|---|
| Pharmacopoeia-style monograph | 川芎 | Drug name, part, processing state |
| Flora of China | *Ligusticum* genus treatment | Genus placement and near relatives |
| Global biodiversity databases | *Conioselinum anthriscoides* | Accepted name and synonymy |
| Supplier specification | Either, sometimes both | Agreement with the delivered lot |
| Classical commentary | 芎藭, with 川芎 as the Shu form | Provenance claim behind the name |

Picking a favourite binomial solves nothing; demanding the pair does. A sheet that gives one Latin name and no drug name is thin, and thin paperwork is the door through which substitution walks.

One editorial point deserves to be stated plainly: synonymy is not ambiguity. The two labels describe one farmed drug, and a supplier who treats the synonymy as permission to ship a different root has misunderstood the situation. The naming problem is a documentation problem. It is not a licence.

## Reading "Sichuan" as a claim

> **Direct answer:** 川芎 translates as Sichuan lovage, and the *Bencao Gangmu* gives the rule in a single line: 出蜀中者，為川芎 — what comes from Shu bears the name. Provenance is built into the word itself, which is unusual, and useful, for anyone reading a label.

The older group name was 芎藭. In 草之三 of the *Bencao Gangmu*, 川芎 appears as the Shu form, and later compendia repeated the phrase. Read plainly, it says that material from that region is the one entitled to the name — geography doing the work of an identity claim.

The logic belongs to 道地 (dào dì), the convention that a drug raised in its historic producing district is the reference version. That is a quality convention rather than a laboratory finding: it compresses generations of observation about soil, climate, and handling into a rule of thumb. For this root the convention points to Sichuan, and the name has carried the claim for centuries.

| Wording on a label | What it asserts | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| 川芎 | The drug name, with Sichuan implied | Whether this lot was grown there |
| 川产 | Produced in Sichuan | District, season, year |
| 道地 | Raised in the traditional district | Any measured quality parameter |
| *Ligusticum chuanxiong* | Botanical identity | Origin entirely |

A name is a claim to be checked, not a certificate. Checking is documentary — a stated district, a harvest year, a supplier who can describe the chain — plus sensory confirmation that the root still has the expected punch. What the name cannot do is prove itself.

Here a distinction is worth holding. "Sichuan" in the name has real content: it tells you which regional system produced the reference material and which practices are associated with it. It does not tell you where a particular sack came from. Treating the convention as a guarantee is one mistake; dismissing it as marketing is the opposite mistake, and both lose information.

## 川芎 against 藁本

> **Direct answer:** These are two drugs, not two spellings. 川芎 is *Ligusticum chuanxiong*, the Sichuan rhizome; 藁本 is *Ligusticum sinense*, the Chinese lovage root. The *Bencao Gangmu* gives them separate adjacent entries, and the differences that matter at the bench are part, nose, and role. A shared genus is not permission to swap.

The confusion is easy to understand. Both are umbellifers of the lovage group, both are dried underground parts, both smell aromatic, and taxonomy has shuffled the names around both of them. A buyer without a reference sample can accept one for the other, and a photograph will not settle it.

| Feature | 川芎 (chuanxiong) | 藁本 (gaoben) |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Ligusticum chuanxiong* | *Ligusticum sinense* |
| Part traded | Rhizome, knotted and compact | Root and rhizome, longer and more fibrous |
| Nose | Rounder, warmer, sweet under the pepper | Sharper, more resinous, camphor-like |
| Classical entry | 芎藭, with 川芎 as the Shu form | Its own adjacent entry in 草之三 |
| Incense role | Supporting middle | Appears in some formula contexts |
| Substitution risk | High — same group, similar look | High — same group, similar look |

Screening is sensory and documentary at the same time. Smell both: chuanxiong reads rounder, with a sweetness beneath the pungency; gaoben reads sharper, with a camphoraceous lift that catches the back of the nose. Look at the piece: chuanxiong is a knotted rhizome with visible bud scars; gaoben tends to be longer and more root-like. Then read the paperwork, where the drug name should be written rather than implied by a genus.

Why does the distinction matter to a reader? Not because of pharmacology — this page makes no medical claims — but because a formula's identity depends on it. A blend that names 川芎 and receives 藁本 has a different aromatic spine, and once the powder is in the mix the error is invisible. Naming the drug is the only defence that survives grinding.

## From field to powder

> **Direct answer:** Lift, clean, dry, then slice or mill. Drying is the operation that decides the lot, because the rhizome is dense and gives up water slowly; a badly dried batch develops a sour, mouldy note that no blend can hide.

The sequence is short and unforgiving.

1. **Lifting.** The rhizome is dug once the top has died back, when the underground store is at its fullest.
2. **Cleaning.** Soil is worked out of the knots. Grit trapped in a node is both a defect and a wear problem for the mill.
3. **Drying.** Whole pieces are dried slowly and turned so air reaches every face, until the interior is firm rather than spongy.
4. **Sorting.** Sound pieces are graded by size and condition; slicing produces discs that can be inspected and dried further.
5. **Milling.** Powder is made to the particle size the formula needs, ideally close to the point of use.

Step three is where most failures begin. Dry too fast and the surface seals; the middle stays damp and the lot turns sour in the sack over the following weeks. Dry too slowly in humid weather and mould gains a foothold. The right pace lets water migrate out while staying ahead of spoilage, and it is judged by feel and experience rather than by a fixed schedule.

Traditional pharmacy adds steps that belong to medicine rather than to incense: slicing, wine-frying, steaming with other materials. Those treatments change the nose — wine-frying softens the pepper and adds a fermented note — and they are recorded here as traditional practice, not as recommendations for a burner. Anyone who wants the root's own character should begin with the plain dried rhizome.

Grinding is the last chance to spoil the material. A fine powder releases its volatiles in a rush and can read sharp; a coarse powder gives them up slowly and unevenly. For incense a middle-coarse grind, one that passes a standard sieve but keeps some texture, usually meters the aroma across the burn better than a flour-fine powder that dumps everything at the front.
## Forms, and what storage costs you

> **Direct answer:** Three forms reach the bench: whole rhizome, transverse slices, and powder. Whole and sliced material is for keeping and inspecting; powder is for blending. The volatile fraction is what decays, so every storage decision is a decision about air, heat, and light.

| Form | Best use | Storage behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Whole rhizome | Long keeping, identity checks | Slowest loss; wants dry, dark, sealed conditions |
| Sliced discs | Inspection, small-batch grinding | Faster loss; more surface exposed |
| Powder | Blending | Fastest loss; short rotation, sealed jars |

The whole piece is the reference form because dense tissue limits diffusion. Slicing multiplies exposed surface, and milling multiplies it again. A workshop that uses this root as a supporting note is better served by keeping whole rhizomes and grinding what it needs than by buying powder for convenience.

Failures show up in the nose before they show in the eye. A root that has taken on moisture smells sour before anything visible grows; a root kept warm loses its brightness before it darkens. Cool, dark, dry, and sealed is the entire rule, and the container matters less than the habit of closing it.

Insect pressure is real with aromatic roots, and it is one reason whole material gets inspected rather than trusted. Holes, fine dust at the bottom of a bag, and webbing all signal activity. Freezing can stop an active infestation, but it does not restore the aroma the insects have already eaten.

## Receiving a lot: checks and variation

> **Direct answer:** Judge aroma first, then the physical piece, then the paperwork. A clean, loud, pungent smell from a firm, well-dried rhizome with no mould and a stated Sichuan origin is the working standard; everything else is either a defect or a question that needs an answer.

| Check | Good sign | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Aroma on breaking | Sharp, clean, herbal-spicy, warming | Flat, sour, or musty |
| Surface | Firm, dry, knotted with visible nodes | Soft spots, grey bloom, insect holes |
| Cross-section | Dense, pale, oily sheen | Hollow pith, dark ring, wet centre |
| Foreign matter | No soil, stems, or other roots | Grit, stem fragments, lookalike roots |
| Feel in the sack | Dry and cool | Clammy, warm, clumping |
| Documents | Drug name, binomial, origin, year | A name alone, or a genus alone |

The order is deliberate. Aroma is the fastest read on whether the volatile fraction survived; the physical checks catch handling and storage failures; documents catch substitution. None of the three replaces the others.

One misreading is worth correcting: a very loud, almost acrid smell is not automatically high quality. It can mean a fresh root with a heavy volatile load, or a root stored badly enough to develop sharp off-notes. The useful question is whether the smell is *clean* — lovage and pepper, or solvent and dust. Loud and clean is good. Loud and dirty is a rejection.

Grading itself is editorial. No universal commercial scale exists for a small buyer to cite, and published monographs describe identity and processing rather than grades. What a buyer can do is define working grades against the checks above, apply them consistently, and keep a retained sample from every lot so next season's comparison is against a real reference rather than a memory.

Two sacks that pass the same identity check can still behave differently, because four ordinary variables move between deliveries.

**Moisture.** A slightly damp lot smells muted and turns sour in storage. An over-dried lot is brittle, dusty, and already losing volatiles. The feel in the hand — cool and dry against clammy or bone-dry — is a better guide than appearance.

**Piece size.** Whole rhizomes dry and keep differently from slices, and slices differently from powder. A lot of thin slices has far more exposed surface than a lot of whole pieces, so it fades faster and needs a shorter rotation. When a supplier silently changes form between orders, the blend changes even though the material did not.

**Age.** This root does not spoil dramatically; it fades. Old material still looks like a root, still passes a glance, still smells of something — but the brightness has gone and the bitterness sits forward. Retained samples are the only reliable defence, because memory is a poor instrument for a slow decline.

**Cleaning.** Knots trap soil, and mills that skip washing leave grit that settles as sediment in the powder. Grit is not an aroma problem, but it is a quality signal: a supplier who tolerates visible soil is usually tolerating other shortcuts.

| Variable | Fast check | Effect on the blend |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | Feel, clumping, warmth of the sack | Muted aroma, sour note, mould risk |
| Piece size | Visual, sieve test | Release rate and shelf life |
| Age | Retained-sample comparison | Lost brightness, bitter dominance |
| Cleaning | Sediment in water, visual | Grit, dust, inconsistent powder |

The habit worth building is a two-line note at receipt, tied to the lot number. After a year those notes describe a supplier more accurately than any single evaluation, and they cost almost nothing to keep.

## The other umbellifer roots

> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong belongs to a small group of aromatic umbellifer roots that share a family but not a register. 当归 is sweeter and softer, 白芷 is sharper and more camphoraceous, 藁本 is the close relative with the harder edge. Placing this root among them is the quickest way to understand what it adds.

| Root | Species | Register | Usual job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 川芎 | *Ligusticum chuanxiong* | Peppery, herbal, warm, bitter | Earthy middle, structural bridge |
| 当归 | *Angelica sinensis* | Sweet, musky, soft, warming | Rounds and sweetens a heart |
| 白芷 | *Angelica dahurica* | Sharp, camphoraceous, dry | Lift and penetration at low dose |
| 藁本 | *Ligusticum sinense* | Sharper, resinous, camphor-like | Related but distinct; not a swap |

The differences are not cosmetic. 当归's sweetness comes from a different balance of volatiles and a softer resinous body, so it reads as warm musk rather than pungent root. 白芷 carries coumarin-type notes that give a dry, penetrating sharpness, and it over-doses easily because that sharpness travels well in smoke. Chuanxiong sits between them: less sweet than 当归, less sharp than 白芷, with a bitterness neither carries to the same degree.

A formulator uses that map to assign jobs. Softening a harsh blend points to 当归. Pushing a note through a heavy base calls for a trace of 白芷. Holding a savoury middle without announcing itself is the chuanxiong job. Asking this root to do the other two produces a blend that smells like a pharmacy rather than a composition.

The classical pairing of 川芎 with 当归 in 四物汤 (Sì Wù Tāng) is worth knowing as a historical fact about how the two roots were understood together — one moving, one nourishing, in the traditional reading. That framing belongs to pharmacy, not to incense, and nothing here extends it into a therapeutic claim. What transfers to the bench is the sensory observation: the two complement each other, and a blend using both needs less of either.

## What heat does to a dense root

> **Direct answer:** A dense aromatic root does not burn like a leaf or a resin. It smoulders, holds heat in its mass, and releases volatiles over a longer window, which is why chuanxiong reads as a middle note with a long tail rather than a quick opening.

Follow the chain. Heat reaches the particle → the outer layer warms first → phthalides vaporise and migrate outward → vapour meets the ember and partly combusts → the remainder rides the smoke. Because the particle is dense, heat penetrates slowly, so release is stretched over time. That is the physical basis of its middle-note behaviour.

Smoke from a root is also heavier than smoke from a flower. Particles are larger, tars more abundant, and the room feels thicker as a result. That is not a defect in itself; it is a reason to keep the dose modest and the ventilation adequate.

Bitterness follows the same path. Some bitter compounds are less volatile than the aromatics, so they arrive later and linger in the after-smoke. A blend that smells balanced in the first minute can turn bitter in the last third, and the correction is to reduce the root rather than to sweeten on top of it.

## Dosing a supporting note

> **Direct answer:** A supporting note is judged by what happens when it is removed, not by how it smells on its own. Chuanxiong is dosed by subtraction: start at the edge of perception, then take it away and listen to whether the blend falls apart. If nothing changes, the material is decoration.

This is where most formulation errors occur. A maker smells the powder, likes it, and adds more; the finished stick then reads as a single root note with everything else buried. The instinct is understandable, because the raw material is impressive. It is also the wrong test, since the raw material is never what the burner delivers.

| What you notice | What it usually means | First move |
|---|---|---|
| The blend feels flat in the middle | No bridging material | Add a trace, then re-test |
| The root is all you can smell | Dose too high, or top notes too weak | Cut the root before boosting the top |
| Smoke turns bitter late | Bitter fraction outlasting the aromatics | Reduce the root, lengthen the base |
| The room feels closed | Heavy particulate load | Lower the dose; improve ventilation |
| Nothing changes when removed | The note was never doing work | Leave it out |

The boundary is worth stating. Chuanxiong cannot supply brightness, it cannot supply sweetness, and it cannot fix a blend whose base is wrong. It can only thicken the middle. A maker who asks for more than that will keep increasing the dose until the formula is unbalanced, and the failure will look like a bad root rather than a bad request.

## The celery-seed question

> **Direct answer:** Celery seed and celery leaf carry phthalide notes of their own, and the family resemblance to chuanxiong is real. They are still different materials: one is a kitchen spice with a green, bright lift, the other a dense medicinal root with bitterness and weight. The resemblance explains the confusion; it does not justify the swap.

This is a common shortcut in workshops where the root is hard to buy. The reasoning sounds sensible — same family, same aromatic family, both smell of celery — and the first sniff seems to confirm it. The second sniff usually does not. Celery seed is lighter, greener, and more one-dimensional; it arrives quickly and leaves quickly. Chuanxiong is slower, denser, and carries a bitter floor that celery does not.

The substitution also changes the mechanics of a stick. Celery seed is a small, oily fruit; the root is a solid starch-and-fibre mass. Swapping one for the other alters how the mix packs, how fast it draws, and how much smoke the formula produces.

| Attribute | Chuanxiong rhizome | Celery seed |
|---|---|---|
| Plant part | Underground stem | Fruit |
| Aromatic weight | Dense, slow, bitter base | Light, green, quick top |
| Behaviour in a stick | Packs tight, slows the draw | Packs loosely, burns faster |
| Best use | Structural middle | Bright culinary accent |
| Substitution verdict | Not interchangeable | Not interchangeable |

There is an editorial judgment here. A maker working outside the Chinese palette may legitimately prefer celery seed for a savoury note, and that is a defensible choice on its own terms. What is not defensible is labelling the result as chuanxiong, or assuming the two can be swapped gram for gram. The materials belong to different jobs even when they share a molecule.

## A burn test you can repeat

> **Direct answer:** Test the material alone, in the format you will actually sell, in the room where it will be used. Compare against a blank of the same base without the root. The difference between the two is the only measurement that matters.

A repeatable test does not need instruments. It needs a control and a consistent method.

1. **Make a blank.** The same binder and base, without the root, formed the same way and dried the same way.
2. **Make a single-variable sample.** The same base with the root added at your intended dose, nothing else changed.
3. **Burn both in the same room, at the same distance, with the same ventilation.** Note the first minute, the middle, and the last third separately.
4. **Record the difference, not the impression.** "The blank thins while the sample holds" is useful. "The sample smells nicer" is not.
5. **Repeat on a different day.** One burn tells you about one burn. Two tell you about the material.

What the test can answer: whether the root is doing structural work, whether the dose is in the useful window, and whether the bitterness arrives late enough to be a problem. What it cannot answer: whether the lot is authentic, whether it was stored well before it reached you, or whether a supplier's paperwork is honest. Those are separate checks, and no burn substitutes for them.

## What changes with the format

> **Direct answer:** The same root behaves differently in a stick, a cone, and a loose pile. Density, airflow, and binder decide how much of the oil reaches the room, so a dose that works in one format can fail in another.

A pressed stick is the most controlled case. The mass is tight, air reaches it mainly through the surface, and the ember creeps along the binder. Chuanxiong's dense powder slows that creep, which is why a stick carrying it can feel longer-burning and heavier than the recipe alone would suggest. The catch is that a tight stick also holds heat, and the bitter fraction gets a longer window to appear.

A cone burns hotter at the tip and cools toward the base. The root's volatiles come off early and strongly, then the smoke thins; the shape exaggerates the opening and shortens the middle. Loose material is the opposite: air moves freely, the temperature is lower and less even, and the aroma arrives in gusts rather than a line. Judging a root from a loose burn and then dosing it into a pressed stick is a common way to over-add.

| Format | Airflow | What the root does | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressed stick | Low, surface-led | Slows the draw, lengthens the tail | Reduce dose slightly; expect late bitterness |
| Cone | High at the tip | Front-loads the volatiles | Judge the middle separately from the opening |
| Loose pile | Free and uneven | Gusty, lower temperature | Increase dose only after a second burn |

So the format is not packaging. It is part of the recipe, and a root tested in one shape has not been tested in another.

## After the powder meets the mix

> **Direct answer:** Once ground and blended, the root stops behaving as a separate material. Its volatiles migrate into neighbouring powders, its particles change how the mix packs, and the smell of the jar becomes a composite rather than a sum. A bench trial that ignores this will misjudge the dose.

The first change is physical. Fine particles coat coarser ones, so a small quantity of root powder can sit on the surface of the whole blend and dominate what the nose meets when the lid comes off. That surface smell is not a prediction of the burn, because the burn reaches inside the particles rather than across their coating.

The second change is chemical. Volatiles leave the root and partition into whatever else is in the jar — a porous wood, a dry flower, a resin with its own oil phase. Over the first days the blend redistributes its aroma, which is why a freshly mixed batch and the same batch a week later are different objects. The root may appear to soften, not because it has faded, but because it has spread.

The third change is mechanical. Dense root powder fills the gaps between lighter particles, so the mix packs tighter and draws more slowly. In a stick this can be useful; in a loose blend it can smother airflow.

| Stage | What changes | What to judge |
|---|---|---|
| Freshly mixed | Surface coating dominates the jar smell | Nothing yet; the sample is not representative |
| After resting | Volatiles redistribute through the mix | Whether the root is still doing structural work |
| In the burn | Heat selects and decomposes | The middle of the stick, not the opening |
| After storage | Light fractions leave first | Whether the blend has thinned or merely softened |

So a blend trial needs a rest period and a written record of it. Without both, the same formula can appear to improve and then fail in production for reasons that were never about the root at all.

## Documentation that survives a dispute

> **Direct answer:** A usable specification names the drug, the species and its synonym, the part, the origin, the harvest year, and the processing state. Anything less leaves room for a substitution that no sensory check will catch once the material is ground.

| Field | Why it earns its place |
|---|---|
| Drug name (川芎) | The stable identifier across Chinese sources |
| Binomial and synonym | Reconciles pharmacopoeia and database naming |
| Part used (rhizome) | Excludes root-only or aerial material |
| Origin, district if known | Tests the provenance claim in the name |
| Harvest year | Separates current stock from old stock |
| Processing state | Predicts aroma and burn behaviour |
| Foreign-matter statement | Flags cleaning quality |

A specification is not a certificate of analysis and does not need to be. Its job is to make the lot describable, so that a disagreement can be settled against a document rather than against memory. A supplier who cannot describe material in these terms is not necessarily selling bad root; they are simply giving the buyer nothing to check.

The field most often skipped is processing state. Wine-fried and plain dried chuanxiong are different materials in the nose, and a sheet that says only "chuanxiong" has not told you which one is in the bag. For incense the plain dried form is the usual starting point; for anything sold as a traditional preparation, the processing should be on the page.

## The classical record, and the limits of it

> **Direct answer:** 川芎 is documented in Chinese pharmacy as an aromatic root with a long clinical tradition, and this page reports that record as traditional knowledge rather than as evidence of effect. The verifiable layers are botanical, sensory, and documentary. The therapeutic layer is not tested here.

The *Bencao Gangmu* entry supplies the name, the provenance logic, and the root's place in the pharmacy of its time. Later monographs add processing methods and quality language. All of that is historical documentation, and it is genuinely useful for identification and for understanding why the material was traded.

What the record does not supply is a modern clinical assessment. Traditional use is not the same thing as demonstrated effect, and a page about incense has no business converting one into the other. The honest formulation is that the root has a documented history, a distinctive chemistry, and a place in the aromatic repertoire — and that its medical uses sit outside this page.

| Layer | Status |
|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established: *Ligusticum chuanxiong*, Apiaceae |
| Synonymy | Established: *Conioselinum anthriscoides* in some databases |
| Classical record | Documented in the *Bencao Gangmu*, 草之三 |
| Aroma chemistry | Established: phthalide-rich, ligustilide dominant |
| Incense role | Secondary and supporting; not a leading 香材 |
| Therapeutic claims | Not established here, and not made |

This separation is not pedantry. It guards against two opposite errors: treating a traditional root as a proven remedy, and dismissing a well-documented material because its medical claims are unproven. The material can be interesting, useful in a blend, and honestly described without either overreach.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The risks are smoke, dust, and concentration. Burning chuanxiong produces particulate matter like any botanical incense; the powder is a respiratory irritant when disturbed; and the volatile fraction is strong enough that a small room needs air.

- **Smoke and particulates.** Burn in a ventilated space and keep sessions short. A fragrant room is not a clean-air measurement.
- **Powder handling.** Fine root powder goes airborne easily. Decant gently, wear a mask when milling or blending in quantity, and wipe surfaces with a damp cloth rather than a dry brush.
- **Skin contact.** Volatiles can irritate sensitive skin. Wash after handling and avoid prolonged contact with the powder.
- **Concentration.** This is a strong aromatic. More is not better, and a heavy dose can make a room feel closed even with a window open.
- **Household exposure.** Children, pets, and anyone with respiratory sensitivity should not be in the room during a heavy burn.

The [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case for incense smoke. The root-specific addition is dust: most workshop exposure comes from handling powder, not from the stick.

## FAQ

**What am I buying when I order 川芎?** The dried underground stem of *Ligusticum chuanxiong*, grown in Sichuan and filed in some databases as *Conioselinum anthriscoides*. Expect a peppery, herbal, warming nose with a bitter tail. In Chinese pharmacy it ranks as a major root; on the incense bench it is a secondary one.

**How is it different from 藁本?** They are siblings, not synonyms. 川芎 comes from *Ligusticum chuanxiong* and is a compact knotted rhizome; 藁本 comes from *Ligusticum sinense* and runs longer, more fibrous, and sharper on the nose. The *Bencao Gangmu* keeps them in adjacent but separate entries, and so should a purchase order.

**Does the name guarantee Sichuan origin?** No. 出蜀中者，為川芎 records a 道地 convention — the district whose material set the reference — and a convention is not a lot certificate. Ask for the growing district and harvest year if the claim matters to you.

**Can celery seed stand in for it?** For a green, savoury lift, celery seed can be the better tool. As a direct replacement, it fails: a fruit is not a rhizome, the release is quicker, the bitterness is missing, and the stick packs differently.

**Is a stronger smell always better?** No. Intensity can come from freshness or from damage, and the two smell different once you ask whether the note is clean. Lovage and pepper with a warm tail is the good version; solvent, dust, or a sour edge is the warning.

**Would a formula ever be built on this root?** Not sensibly. It is a middle-weight structural material — useful for joining other notes, incapable of carrying a composition alone, and best kept at the edge of perception.

## Summary

Take the plant first: a carrot-family perennial, farmed rather than gathered, whose knotted underground stem is the traded drug. Take the name second: 川芎 means the lovage of Shu, a provenance convention rather than a laboratory certificate. Take the nose third: phthalide chemistry, celery and lovage up front, pepper and warm earth behind, bitterness at the close. Put together, those three facts explain the material's position — a heavyweight in the pharmacopoeia, a middleweight at the incense bench, and never the note a formula is built around. The two checks that decide a purchase are the synonymy, which is paperwork rather than ambiguity, and the separation from 藁本, which is a different drug with a harder edge.

### Authenticity and disambiguation
**What the record shows:** classical entries and modern taxonomy both keep 川芎 and 藁本 apart, on species, on part used, and on smell. **Status:** sufficient for practical screening at receipt.

### Safety
**What the record shows:** any burning botanical yields smoke and particulates, so ventilation comes first; handling powder adds an inhalation route that the stick does not. **Status:** established.

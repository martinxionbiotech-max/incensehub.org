**Direct answer:** Castoreum (海狸香, hǎilíxiāng) is the scent secretion of beavers, produced in a pair of castor sacs at the base of the tail and used historically in European perfumery as an animalic fixative. It belongs to Western perfumery, not to the Chinese incense canon, and the glandular material is not a lawful modern supply — what the fragrance trade sells under the name today is a synthetic reconstruction.

This page sits in an incense encyclopedia because readers keep meeting castoreum in two places: old perfume formulas that call for a "castoreum tincture", and modern ingredient lists that use the word as a note name. Both encounters need the same three facts before anything else makes sense. The material is animal-derived, its historical home was the perfumer's organ rather than the incense burner, and its Chinese name is a modern description rather than an inherited term.

What follows treats castoreum the way a materials reference should treat any obsolete material: it explains what the substance was, why it behaved the way it did in a formula, how the trade replaced it, and where the boundary sits between the historical record and the marketing that borrows from it. It does not explain how to obtain the animal material, because in most jurisdictions there is no legitimate route to do so.

## Key facts

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Material class | Animal secretion — not a plant resin, gum, wood or flower |
| Producing organ | Paired castor sacs in the pelvic region at the tail base; present in both sexes |
| Source animals | *Castor fiber* (Eurasian beaver) and *Castor canadensis* (North American beaver) |
| Historical role | European perfumery fixative, roughly the 18th to mid-20th century |
| Typical accords | Leather, tobacco, amber, heavy floral bases |
| Chinese canon status | Absent from classical incense compendia; 海狸香 is a modern descriptive name |
| Modern commercial reality | Synthetic reconstruction; glandular material effectively unavailable |
| Legal frame | National and regional wildlife protection, not a CITES listing for the genus |
| Aroma register | Animalic, leathery, smoky-sweet, urinous when concentrated |
| Physical behaviour | Waxy solid or alcohol tincture; not a combustible incense material |

Two rows in that table decide the shape of the whole article. The material is animal-derived, which puts it under wildlife law rather than under the agricultural and forestry rules that govern plant resins. And it is a tincture material, which means it was never handled like a chip of agarwood or a spoon of powder — a distinction that most short summaries flatten into "animal fixative" and then move past.

## What castoreum physically is

**Direct answer:** Castoreum is a waxy, strongly scented secretion that accumulates in a pair of castor sacs flanking the cloaca at the base of a beaver's tail, and both males and females produce it.

The anatomy matters more than it might seem. The castor sacs are discrete, paired structures with their own ducts, sitting between the pelvis and the tail base. They are not the anal glands, and the secretion is not urine, though a great many popular descriptions blur the three together. A beaver marks territory by dragging and pressing this material onto scent mounds — piles of mud and vegetation at the water's edge — and the secretion carries individual and colony information in the way that scent marks do across mammals.

The fresh secretion is soft and pale to reddish-brown, and it darkens and hardens as it dries. That physical history is why the historical trade unit was the dried sac rather than a bottled liquid: the material travels better as a solid, and the perfumer could re-soften or extract it at the point of use. Anyone who has handled old perfumery raw materials will recognise the pattern — the same logic applied to civet paste and to ambergris, both of which also moved as solids before they moved as tinctures.

The chemistry is complex in the way animal secretions usually are: volatile phenolics, ketones and alcohols carried in a waxy, resinous matrix. The phenolics are what the nose reads as smoky, tarry and faintly medicinal; the waxy fraction is what makes the material persist. That combination explains nearly everything castoreum did in a formula, and it also explains why the synthetic reconstruction is built around phenols and leathery bases rather than around a single "castoreum molecule".

The chain from animal behaviour to perfumer's bottle runs like this: a beaver marks territory → secretion accumulates and oxidises in the castor sacs → the dried sacs are collected → alcohol draws the volatile fraction into a tincture → the low-volatility phenolics and waxes give the tincture its long, heavy tail on skin. Each arrow is a physical step, and each one changes the material. What arrived at the perfumer's bench was not "beaver scent" in the abstract; it was a specific extract of a specific dried gland.

## The name, and a translation problem worth naming

**Direct answer:** 海狸香 reads literally as "beaver fragrance", but it is a modern descriptive material name rather than a classical Chinese term, and modern zoological Chinese more often calls the animal 河狸 (hélí).

The word 海狸 is compounded from 海 (sea) and 狸 (a small wild mammal, used loosely for civets, raccoon dogs and similar animals). That is a description written by people encountering beaver material through trade, not a name inherited from a canon. Modern Chinese zoology prefers 河狸 — "river beaver" — for *Castor fiber*, while 海狸香 survives as the conventional name of the perfumery material. The split is a small thing with a large diagnostic value: a material whose animal has one name and whose product has another is usually a material that arrived as an object of commerce.

| Term | Literal reading | Status | What it signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 海狸香 | beaver fragrance | Modern trade and translation term | A Western material described in Chinese, not inherited |
| 河狸 | river beaver | Modern zoological name | The living animal, *Castor fiber* |
| 麝香 | musk fragrance | Classical, attested in materia medica | A material inside the Chinese canon |
| 灵猫香 | civet fragrance | Materia-medica term for civet | Animal fixative with Chinese standing |
| 龙涎香 | dragon-spittle fragrance | Classical trade name for ambergris | An import that was fully assimilated into the canon |

Read the right-hand column as a scale of assimilation. Ambergris arrived from the sea and was given a mythic name, a medical role and a place in recipes; it became Chinese. Castoreum arrived as a perfumery material in a century that already had synthetic chemistry waiting in the wings, and it never made that journey. The name stayed descriptive because the material stayed foreign.

This is also where a common error starts. Because 海狸香 follows the same two-character pattern as 麝香 and 灵猫香, a reader can assume the three are peers. They are not. Sharing a naming pattern is not evidence of shared provenance, and the pattern itself was available to anyone coining a new term in the twentieth century.

## Why it belonged to the perfumer, not the censer

**Direct answer:** Castoreum was used as an alcohol tincture in European perfumery, where it deepened leather and tobacco accords; it was never a material that anyone placed on hot coals, and burning it produces acrid, burnt-hair notes rather than the effect perfumers valued.

The distinction is physical, not cultural. A tincture is a dilute alcohol solution applied to skin, fabric or blotter. The alcohol flashes off, the volatile fraction lifts, and the heavy residue stays close to the surface and releases slowly over hours. That slow release is the entire point. Heat the same material on charcoal and you skip the controlled evaporation stage: the waxes and proteins scorch, the phenolics overwhelm, and the result reads as burnt rather than animalic.

So the historical use case was a base note in a composed fragrance, not a fumigant. In the classical European leather tradition, castoreum sat underneath the dry, tarry, smoky materials that gave tanned leather its smell, and it supplied the warmth that made those accords feel worn rather than chemical. Tobacco accords used it the same way — a sweet-animalic undertow beneath dried leaf. The same function appears in heavy floral bases, where a small amount of an animalic material keeps a large bouquet from smelling like soap.

Trace the mechanism and the reason becomes obvious: the tincture is applied → body heat volatilises the light fraction → the phenolic and waxy residue stays on skin → it releases for hours → the fragrance's base holds its shape. Remove the skin and the body heat, and you remove the mechanism. A material whose function depends on slow evaporation at 32 °C cannot be evaluated by setting it alight at 400 °C.

That is why the page treats "castoreum incense" as a category error rather than a recipe variant. The phrase combines a tincture material with a combustion format, and the combination does not preserve the material's behaviour. If a modern blend is described as containing castoreum, the practical question is not "how does it burn" but "which reconstruction was used, and at what dose".

The leather connection is worth spelling out because it is often repeated without explanation. Tanned leather smells of the process as much as of the animal: tannins, smoke from curing fires, oils used to soften the hide, and the slow oxidation of fats. A perfumer building a leather accord is therefore assembling a memory of a workshop, and castoreum supplied the part of that memory that came from the animal itself — warm, slightly fecal, slightly tarry. The same logic explains its place in tobacco accords, where the cured leaf carries a similarly composite smell of smoke, sweetness and controlled decay.

## How the historical trade handled the material

**Direct answer:** Castoreum moved as dried castor sacs, graded by animal origin and condition, and the perfumer made the tincture at the bench rather than buying a ready-made extract.

The trade unit was the dried sac, sometimes still paired, packed and shipped whole. Trade documents from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries treat it as a commodity with grades rather than as a pharmaceutical: size, fatness, colour, freedom from dirt and the strength of the smell were the working criteria, and a buyer judged them by hand and nose at the point of sale. There was no analytical standard to appeal to, which is why the historical literature on quality is descriptive rather than numerical.

Adulteration was a known problem, as it was for every expensive animal material. The common forms were stretching with cheaper fatty or waxy matter, mixing sacs from different sources, and selling material that had dried badly or lost its volatile fraction. A buyer's defence was provenance and sensory check — the same two defences that traders in musk and civet relied on, and for the same reason: the material's value sat in a smell that could be imitated but not verified by inspection alone.

At the bench, the dried sac was macerated in alcohol for weeks, then filtered and left to age. The perfumer worked by ratio and by nose, adjusting the strength of the tincture to the composition rather than to a fixed specification. That is a very different production model from the modern one, where a reconstruction arrives with a batch number and a declared composition, and it explains why historical formulas give proportions in parts rather than in percentages.

The trade did not fade because perfumers stopped wanting the effect. It faded because the supply became legally and ethically untenable at roughly the same moment that synthetic chemistry made a substitute commercially viable. Those two changes arrived together, and the industry took the path that was both cheaper and defensible.

## What "fixative" actually means here

**Direct answer:** A fixative is a low-volatility material that slows the overall evaporation of a composition and adds a persistent base; it does not chemically lock other molecules in place.

The word invites a wrong picture — glue, or a cage, or a preservative that stops a fragrance from degrading. The physical reality is closer to traffic management. A perfume is a mixture of molecules with a wide range of vapour pressures. The light ones leave in minutes; the heavy ones leave over hours. Add a large quantity of very heavy material and the mixture's average evaporation rate falls, the early bloom becomes less abrupt, and the dry-down lasts longer. The top notes are still there; they are simply leaving from a mixture that is, on average, less eager to evaporate.

Castoreum performed that role with an unusual combination of properties: it was heavy, it was persistent, and it had a smell strong enough to matter at low concentration. The last point is easy to underestimate. A fixative that is heavy but odourless changes the physics without changing the scent; castoreum changed both, which is why it appears in formulas as a structural material and as an effect at the same time.

There is a boundary worth stating. Fixation is not preservation. A tincture that has oxidised, or a resin that has lost its volatiles, cannot be rescued by adding a heavier material on top. And a fixative does not make a bad composition good — it extends whatever is already there, including the faults. In practice, the material is a multiplier on the base, not a correction for the whole.

## The animal-fixative family, compared properly

**Direct answer:** Musk, civet, castoreum and ambergris are four animal materials with different organs, different chemistries and different legal positions, and only the first two have any standing in the Chinese incense tradition.

| Material | Origin | Aroma register | Legal position | Chinese canon | Modern substitute |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Musk (麝香) | Preputial gland secretion of *Moschus* deer | Warm, sweet, skin-like, slightly powdery | CITES Appendix I or II depending on population | Classical, attested in materia medica | Synthetic macrocyclic musks |
| Civet (灵猫香) | Gland secretion of civets (*Viverra* and relatives) | Sharp, fecal, animalic, ammoniacal when fresh | CITES Appendix III for some populations | Materia-medica term, used as an animal accent | Synthetic civet bases |
| Castoreum (海狸香) | Castor sac secretion of beavers | Leathery, smoky, tarry-sweet, urinous when concentrated | National and regional protection; no CITES listing for the genus | Absent | Synthetic leather-castoreum accords |
| Ambergris (龙涎香) | Biliary mass formed in sperm whales, beach-cast | Salty, sweet, marine, faintly fecal | Trade restricted in many jurisdictions; beach-cast only | Classical trade name, fully assimilated | Ambroxan-type synthetics |

The table makes the outlier obvious. Three of the four have a Chinese textual life; castoreum does not. Three are regulated through international instruments or explicit national bans; castoreum's beaver is protected through a patchwork of national and regional wildlife law. And the aromas are not interchangeable even within the family: musk is skin-warm and sweet, civet is sharp and fecal, castoreum is tarry and leathery, ambergris is saline and sweet. Treating "animalic" as a single note is like treating "green" as a single note — it names a direction, not a material.

There is a further distinction that matters to anyone reading old formulas. Ambergris is a pathological secretion that washes ashore and can be collected without killing the animal; musk and civet historically involved the animal directly; castoreum involved killing or handling the animal for the sacs. The ethical weight is not identical across the family, and a page that lumps them together as "cruel animal materials" is being imprecise in a way that weakens its own case.

## Does castoreum belong in the Chinese canon? A three-part test

**Direct answer:** Castoreum fails every reasonable test of Chinese canonical standing — no textual attestation in the incense compendia, no plausible trade-route history as a Chinese incense material, and no inherited naming form.

Claims of classical standing are cheap and easy to test. Three checks settle most of them.

The first is textual attestation. The Ming-dynasty incense compendium 香乘 (Xiangcheng), compiled by 周嘉胄, gathers the materials and recipes of the Chinese incense tradition, and the materia-medica literature — the 本草 line of texts — records the aromatics that entered Chinese medicine. Musk and civet appear in that record with measured units, preparation notes and recipe roles. Castoreum does not appear with any of them.

The second is trade-route plausibility. A material enters a tradition through a route that leaves traces: a source region, a port, a commodity name, a tax record, a substitute that appears when supply fails. The beaver's range runs across northern Eurasia and North America, far from the maritime and overland aromatic trade that fed Chinese incense practice. There is no Chinese commodity history of beaver castor to match the well-documented flow of resins from the south and west.

The third is naming form. Inherited Chinese material names tend to be short, old and sometimes opaque — 麝香, 乳香, 龙涎香. Names coined to describe an unfamiliar import are longer, more literal and more explanatory: 海狸香 is exactly that shape.

| Test | What a genuine classical material would show | Castoreum's result |
|---|---|---|
| Textual attestation | Recipe or materia-medica entry with preparation notes | No entry in the incense compendia |
| Trade history | Source region, commodity name, substitute patterns | No Chinese castor trade record |
| Naming form | Short inherited term, sometimes opaque | Descriptive modern compound |
| Recipe role | A defined function in classical blends | No classical role |

Absence of evidence is not proof of absence, and this page does not claim that no beaver material ever reached China. The claim is narrower and stronger: castoreum has no place in the canon, no inherited name and no recipe tradition, so presenting it as a Chinese incense material is a provenance error rather than a matter of interpretation.

## The legal picture: protection without a single listing

**Direct answer:** Beaver protection is set by national and regional wildlife law rather than by a CITES appendix, so the legal status of castoreum varies by jurisdiction even though the commercial conclusion is the same nearly everywhere.

Readers who know the musk and civet entries will expect a CITES appendix number. There isn't one for the beaver genus, and that absence can be misread as permission. It is not. *Castor fiber* is protected under wildlife legislation across much of Europe and Asia, and the species has been the subject of reintroduction and management programmes for decades; *Castor canadensis* is managed as a game and fur species in North America under provincial and state rules. Those regimes differ in purpose, season and permit structure, and none of them establishes a lawful international trade in castor sacs for perfumery.

The conservation story is more layered than a single status label suggests. The Eurasian beaver was reduced to a handful of relict populations by the early twentieth century and has since recovered across much of its former range through protection and reintroduction, to the point that it is now managed as an ecosystem engineer and, in some places, as a nuisance species. At the same time, particular subspecies and isolated populations remain small and vulnerable. Global recovery and local fragility coexist, which is exactly why the legal instruments are national.

The beaver's own history explains the shape of that law. European beaver populations were driven close to extinction by centuries of trapping for fur, meat and castoreum, and the recovery came from deliberate protection, natural recolonisation and, in places, the return of a species that had been absent for generations. The animal is now valued for a different reason: beaver dams and canals create wetlands, slow water in catchments and support species that depend on wet ground. That ecological role is why reintroduction programmes continue, and it is also why the animal is managed rather than simply left alone — the same dam that creates a wetland can flood farmland or block a culvert.

A material reference should be honest about that complexity. "Protected" does not mean "rare everywhere"; in parts of Europe the beaver is common enough to require management, and in parts of Asia particular populations remain precarious. What protection does mean, consistently, is that the animal is not a free resource. The legal status removes the material from ordinary commerce regardless of how many beavers are in a given river, which is the point that matters for anyone reading a product label.

Follow the commercial chain: protected or tightly managed populations → no lawful large-scale supply of castor sacs → the trade substitutes a synthetic → the word survives on labels without the material behind it. The substitution is not a recent marketing convenience; it is the predictable result of a supply that law and biology closed off.

What that means for a reader is simple. A seller offering "natural castoreum" is either mislabelling a reconstruction or describing an unlawful supply, and neither possibility requires laboratory testing to evaluate. The claim itself is the problem.

For a buyer who wants to be thorough rather than reassured, the documentary test has three parts. Ask which legal regime the seller believes applies to the source animal and in which country the material was produced. Ask for the declared trade name and, where a reconstruction is being sold, the supplier's own description of the material class. Ask whether the seller distinguishes between a note name and an ingredient — a supplier who uses the two interchangeably has not thought about the question, and that is information in itself. None of these questions requires a laboratory, and all of them are cheaper than a disputed shipment.

## Reading a modern label that says castoreum

**Direct answer:** On a contemporary fragrance or flavour label, "castoreum" almost always names a synthetic reconstruction, and the same word can also appear as a historical note name with no material claim behind it at all.

| Label wording | Most likely meaning | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| "Castoreum" in a note pyramid | A reconstruction used for the leather-animalic effect | Nothing material; it is a descriptive note name |
| "Castoreum accord" | A perfumer's built base, synthetic | The allergen declaration, if any |
| "Castoreum absolute" | Historically an animal-derived extract | A red flag on a modern label; ask for the origin |
| "Castoreum tincture" | Historically an alcohol infusion of the dried sac | Same red flag; not a lawful modern material in most markets |
| "Castoreum flavour" | A flavour reconstruction, not glandular tissue | The flavour-ingredient declaration |

The naming trap is that one word covers a historical animal material, a modern synthetic accord and a purely descriptive note. A note pyramid is not an ingredient list; it is a description of what the composition smells like. When a fragrance says "castoreum, leather, tobacco", the honest reading is "this smells of the leather-tobacco-castoreum idea", not "this contains beaver secretion".

For buyers in the flavour and fragrance trades, the check is documentary rather than sensory. Ask for the material's declared name, its CAS or supplier reference where applicable, and confirmation that it is a reconstruction. A supplier who cannot answer those questions is not necessarily selling something unlawful, but the uncertainty is itself a reason to stop.

## Why the name outlived the material

**Direct answer:** Perfumery keeps material names as descriptors long after the material itself has gone, so "castoreum" now names a smell family rather than a substance on a shelf.

This is not unique to castoreum. "Musk" survives as a note name across a trade that has not used deer musk for decades; "ambergris" survives in fragrance copy for compositions built on synthetic amber materials; "civet" survives as an adjective for a style of animalic sharpness. The vocabulary of perfumery is a layer of historical references that the industry keeps because it communicates an effect efficiently, and because a familiar word sells better than a chemical name.

The consequence for a reader is a habit worth building: separate the note name from the ingredient list. A note name is a promise about smell. An ingredient declaration is a statement about content. The two are written by different people for different purposes, and they are allowed to disagree — a fragrance can honestly say "castoreum, leather, tobacco" while containing none of the three as materials.

The habit matters most with animalic names, because those names carry an ethical charge that the modern materials do not. A buyer who reacts to "castoreum" as though it meant a beaver was harmed is reacting to a word that, in the contemporary trade, usually describes a reconstruction. The reverse error is worse: assuming that because the name is old, the material must be natural. It is old vocabulary attached to new chemistry, and the only way to tell which is which is to ask what the supplier actually sells.

## If an old formula calls for castoreum

**Direct answer:** Read the formula for the function the material performed, then substitute for that function; reproducing the animal material is neither lawful nor necessary to keep the composition's shape.

Old formulas are instructions written for a supply system that no longer exists. Treating them as literal shopping lists produces either an impossible search or a bad substitution — someone drops in a leather aroma chemical and wonders why the base collapsed. The useful question is what castoreum was doing in that particular formula.

| Function in the formula | Practical substitute | What you keep | What you give up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm animalic base | Synthetic castoreum accord | Leathery warmth and tenacity | The specific phenolic sharpness of the glandular extract |
| Leather-tobacco depth | Birch tar with labdanum and a touch of styrax | Smoke, tar, amber weight | The skin-like animalic note |
| Fixative for a citrus top | Benzoin or labdanum | Sweetness and holding power | The tarry edge |
| Heavy floral underside | A small dose of a leather base | Roundness under the bouquet | The urinous facet, which most modern briefs do not want |

Two judgments sit behind that table. The first is that reconstruction is not replication: a synthetic accord can reproduce the direction of an effect without reproducing its chemistry, and a formula built on the substitute will smell like a modern reading of the original rather than the original itself. The second is that the original may not be worth reproducing. Some historical accords depend on materials whose animalic sharpness reads as stale or dirty to a contemporary nose, and a faithful reconstruction can be a worse composition than a considered adaptation.

Where a formula is being published rather than made, the responsible move is to print the historical material with a note that it is no longer available and to give the substitution actually used. That preserves the record and keeps the recipe usable.

## Castor oil is not castoreum

**Direct answer:** Castor oil is a pressed seed oil from the castor bean plant *Ricinus communis*, a completely different material from a beaver secretion, and the two share nothing but a fragment of vocabulary.

The confusion is common enough to be worth a section. Castor oil is a triglyceride oil pressed from the seeds of a spurge-family plant; it is used as a lubricant, a cosmetic base and an industrial feedstock. Castoreum is a mammalian glandular secretion. One is a plant oil sold by the litre, the other is an animal material that the trade replaced a century ago. There is no substitution, no safety equivalence and no shared chemistry.

The words diverge in the middle: *castor* in castor oil attaches to the plant and its seed, while *castoreum* attaches to the animal and its sac. A recipe that calls for castor oil and gets castoreum, or the reverse, fails at the level of what the material is. The mistake usually appears in casual writing rather than in trade documents, but it is the kind of error that makes a whole paragraph untrustworthy once a reader spots it.

## The accord, layer by layer

**Direct answer:** Castoreum works almost entirely in the base of a composition, where its leathery phenolics and waxy persistence support tobacco, woods and amber; used higher up the pyramid it muddies the opening.

| Layer | What castoreum contributes | Natural partners | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | Nothing useful | — | Blurs citrus and green notes into a stale haze |
| Heart | Depth under heavy florals | Rose, jasmine, orange flower | Turns a bouquet heavy and slightly dirty |
| Base | Leather, smoke, animalic warmth | Birch tar, labdanum, vetiver, tobacco, oakmoss | Reads urinous or stale when overdosed |
| Fixative | Slower dry-down, rounder transition | Benzoin, tonka, sandalwood | Extends faults as readily as virtues |

The dosing logic follows from the same chemistry. The material is loud at low concentration and unpleasant above it, so the working range is narrow: enough to register as warmth, not enough to register as a note. Perfumers describe this as a material that "disappears" into a formula at the right dose and ruins it at twice that dose, which is why old formulas often specify it as a trace ingredient rather than a measured component.

For a modern incense maker reading this page for practical reasons, the translation is worth spelling out. Castoreum has no role in a burnable stick or cone. If a brief asks for a leathery-smoky incense, the work is done with birch tar, labdanum, styrax or a resinous base — materials that tolerate combustion and behave predictably in a binder. Borrowing the castoreum name for the brief is fine; borrowing its chemistry is not possible and not needed.

## What the historical record supports, and what it does not

**Direct answer:** The glandular origin, the European perfumery use and the modern substitution are documented; medicinal and aphrodisiac reputations attached to castoreum are traditional belief, not established fact.

| Claim | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Secretion of beaver castor sacs | Established | Anatomy and zoological record |
| Used in European perfumery as a fixative | Historically documented | Perfumery literature and trade practice |
| Used in Chinese incense | Not supported | Absent from the compendia and trade record |
| Medicinal value | Traditional belief | Historical materia medica and folk practice, not established clinically |
| Aphrodisiac reputation | Traditional belief, unverified | Folklore attached to strong-smelling animal materials |
| A lawful modern supply exists | Not established | Protection regimes and trade substitution |

The medicinal row deserves a plain statement. Castoreum appears in older European and Near Eastern medical writing with a range of attributed properties, and those attributions belong to the history of medicine rather than to pharmacology. This page does not repeat them as advice, does not convert them into benefits, and does not treat "used for centuries" as evidence of anything except that people used it.

The same discipline applies to the aroma claims. "Leathery, smoky, sweet" is a description of a perceptual register, not a chemical assay, and it is stable across sources because it describes an effect that perfumers were trained to recognise. That is a modest kind of knowledge, and it is the kind this page can honestly carry.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The safety issues here are combustion smoke, alcohol-based tinctures and the absence of any legitimate medicinal use — not the material's historical reputation.

Incense and any smoking material produce particulate matter; burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep flames and hot surfaces away from children and pets. Historical tinctures were alcoholic, which makes old bottles a fire risk as well as a curiosity; do not open and burn them, and dispose of them through appropriate household hazardous waste channels. Nothing on this page is medical advice, and no claim is made that castoreum treats or prevents any condition. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general handling notes that apply to all aromatic materials.

## Summary

Castoreum is a beaver secretion that spent roughly two centuries as a European perfumery fixative for leather and tobacco accords, and then lost its supply to wildlife protection and its function to synthetic chemistry. It never entered the Chinese incense canon, its Chinese name is a modern description, and the material sold under the name today is a reconstruction. The useful knowledge is not the folklore but the structure: an animal material with a specific organ, a specific physical behaviour, a specific legal position and a specific substitute.

- **Provenance:** Western perfumery, not Chinese incense; the name 海狸香 is descriptive and modern.
- **Physics:** a tincture material that works by slow evaporation on skin, not a combustible incense ingredient.
- **Law:** national and regional protection rather than a CITES appendix, with the same commercial result.
- **Practice:** substitute for the function — leathery warmth, tenacity, a heavy base — not for the molecule.

## FAQ

**Is castoreum a Chinese incense material?** No. It has no entry in the Ming-dynasty incense compendium 香乘, no Chinese trade history as an incense material and no inherited name. 海狸香 is a modern descriptive term coined to name a Western perfumery material, which is why it follows the naming pattern of a description rather than the pattern of an inherited material name.

**What does castoreum smell like?** Animalic and leathery first, with a smoky-tarry sweetness underneath and a faint urinous edge when concentrated. Perfumers used it as a base material rather than a leading note, so the smell in isolation is harsher than the effect in a composition — at working dilution it reads as warm leather and tobacco rather than as a glandular secretion.

**Is castoreum still used today?** Only as a reconstruction. The glandular material has no lawful large-scale supply, and modern fragrance and flavour materials sold as castoreum are synthetic accords built to reproduce the leathery-animalic effect. A label that names castoreum is describing a smell, not declaring an animal ingredient.

**Is castor oil the same thing as castoreum?** No. Castor oil is pressed from the seeds of *Ricinus communis*, a plant in the spurge family, and is a completely different material with different chemistry and different uses. The shared letters are a linguistic coincidence in trade vocabulary, not a family relationship.

**Why do old perfume formulas mention castoreum?** Because it was a standard base material in European perfumery until the mid-twentieth century, particularly in leather and tobacco accords. Those formulas describe a supply system that no longer exists; the material is now read for the function it performed and replaced with a reconstruction or with other leathery base materials.

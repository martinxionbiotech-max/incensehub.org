> **Direct answer:** Tree peony bark (牡丹皮, mǔdānpí) is the dried outer layer of *Paeonia suffruticosa* roots — a woody peony in the family Paeoniaceae. Smell it and you get dry root, clean earth, and a floral trace so faint it disappears if you look for it. The material's record is medicinal, not aromatic, and it is not the flower 牡丹: one tree, two products, two markets.

## One tree, three products

**Direct answer:** The peony group supplies at least three commercial identities — the flower 牡丹, the root bark 牡丹皮, and the herbaceous peony root 芍药 — and they are not interchangeable. Two come from the tree peony; the third comes from a different species.

| Product | Chinese | Source | Part | Register |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tree peony flower | 牡丹 | *Paeonia suffruticosa* | Blossom | Ornamental, fragrant |
| Tree peony bark | 牡丹皮 | *Paeonia suffruticosa* | Root bark | Materia medica, quiet |
| Herbaceous peony root | 芍药 | *Paeonia lactiflora* | Root | Materia medica, different species |

The first two are the same plant. That is the fact that causes most of the confusion. A reader who knows that 牡丹 is a famous flower may assume that anything called 牡丹 carries the flower's fragrance. The bark does not. It is a root tissue with a faint, earthy smell, and it belongs to a different market, a different preparation chain, and a different vocabulary.

The third product is a different species altogether. 芍药 is the herbaceous peony, a soft-stemmed perennial that dies back each winter, and its root is a separate materia-medica material. The names are distinct in Chinese, but English "peony" flattens them into one word. A buyer working in English has to hold the distinction deliberately, because the language will not do it for them.

A useful rule follows. "Peony" is not a specification. "牡丹皮" is. The Chinese name names the plant and the part in three characters, and that is exactly the information a buyer needs. The English common name names a genus and leaves the part to context.

The three products also differ in harvest. The flower is picked or simply enjoyed on the plant; the bark is dug and stripped; the herbaceous root is lifted and dried. Each harvest has its own season and its own labour, and none of them substitutes for another. A flower harvest does not produce bark, and a root harvest does not produce flowers.

The commercial lesson is blunt: when a plant yields multiple products, the part-name is the reliable identifier. A buyer who orders "peony" has ordered a category. A buyer who orders 牡丹皮 has ordered a part of a named plant. The difference in precision is the difference between a purchase order and a wish.

## Two markets on one plant

**Direct answer:** The flower and the bark belong to different markets with different selection pressures, different harvests, and different quality languages. The flower is an ornamental and a fragrance subject; the bark is a bulk medicinal material. The same tree supports both, but not with the same logic.

The ornamental market selects for flower form, colour, size, and bloom season. A garden tree peony is judged by how it looks in April or May, and a plant with a mediocre flower is a disappointment even if its root is excellent. The medicinal market selects for root mass, bark thickness, and the chemical profile that pharmacognosy associates with the drug. A plant with a spectacular flower may be unremarkable as a root source, and a plain single-flowered plant may be the better producer.

That difference in selection explains why the classical preference for the "red single-petaled mountain form" is not a contradiction of the ornamental tradition. The garden forms were selected for double flowers and unusual colours; the medicinal form was selected for the root. The two traditions ran on the same species but on different criteria, and the classical note preserves the medicinal side of that split.

The markets also differ in volume. A flower is sold by the plant, the stem, or the bloom; a bark is sold by weight, in sacks, to a processor. The flower trade can support a high price for a single exceptional specimen; the bark trade runs on consistency across a batch. A buyer who comes from the ornamental world and expects the bark to be priced like a rare cultivar is looking at the wrong market.

The halo effect is the commercial risk. The fame of 牡丹 as the "king of flowers" (花王) lends prestige to anything that carries the name, and a seller can lean on that prestige without making a false claim. The buyer may pay for the association rather than for the bark. The defence is the part-name and the quality checks, not scepticism about the flower's fame.

## Botany and the cultivar problem

**Direct answer:** *Paeonia suffruticosa* is a woody shrub peony, not the soft herbaceous peony of the border. It has persistent woody stems, large divided leaves, and large showy flowers, and it has been cultivated in China for so long that its wild ancestry is a botanical discussion in its own right.

The tree peony is a slow, long-lived plant. Its stems stay above ground through winter, and the plant builds a woody framework over years rather than dying back to the crown. The leaves are large and deeply divided, and the flowers are among the most celebrated in Chinese horticulture. The plant is grown for the flower first and for the root second, and the two uses have produced different selection pressures.

That history complicates the botany. Garden tree peonies are a complex of forms, and the name *P. suffruticosa* is used in horticulture and pharmacognosy for a group that has been shaped by centuries of cultivation and hybridization. Wild relatives such as *P. rockii* and *P. ostii* are part of the story, and botanists do not all draw the species boundary in the same place. For a material page, the practical point is that the cultivated tree peony is the source, and that the name on the label should be treated as a cultivated identity rather than a claim about a single wild population.

The root is woody and branched. The bark sits on the outside of the root, over a paler inner tissue, and that arrangement is what the classical phrase "肉白皮丹" describes: white flesh inside, red bark outside. The bark is the material; the inner wood is not. A harvest therefore produces a bark product and a woody residue, and the skill is in separating them cleanly.

Root harvest is a different operation from flower harvest. It is done for the underground part, it disturbs the plant, and it is managed on a different cycle from the ornamental trade. A garden may be grown for decades for its flowers and never be harvested for bark. A medicinal plantation is managed for roots. The same species can serve both, but not in the same season or the same way.

The cultivar question matters for sourcing because the classical text expresses a preference for a particular form. That preference is a historical sourcing note, not a claim that only one cultivar exists today. Modern material may come from named cultivars or from mixed plantings, and the buyer's real question is whether the material is the root bark of the correct species, cleanly separated and properly dried.

## The name as a specification

**Direct answer:** 牡丹皮 is a three-character specification: 牡丹 names the tree peony, 皮 names the bark, and 丹 carries the old association with red. The name tells you the plant and the part, which is more than the English common name does.

The character 丹 originally names cinnabar, and by extension a deep red. In 牡丹皮 it points to the reddish outer surface of the bark, the surface that gives the material its classical description. The character 皮 means skin, peel, or bark. Put together, the name is almost a label on a specimen jar: this is the bark of the tree peony.

Chinese material names often work this way. 花 is flower, 根 is root, 子 is seed, 叶 is leaf, 皮 is bark. The part-character is not decoration; it is the disambiguator. When the same plant yields a fragrant flower and a medicinal bark, the part-character is what keeps the two apart in the market.

The classical phrase "肉白皮丹" reinforces the same structure. The root has white flesh and a red bark. The material is the red outer layer, stripped from the white inner tissue. A piece that is mostly white is not the bark; a piece that is mostly red is. The name and the description agree, and the buyer can check one against the other.

The English trade name "moutan cortex" preserves the same logic in Latin. Cortex means bark, and moutan is the transliteration of 牡丹. The Latin name is more precise than "tree peony bark" because it names the drug rather than the plant, but it is also less accessible. A buyer who sees "moutan cortex" on a specification should read it as the root bark, not as the flower or the whole root.

What the name cannot do is guarantee species. 牡丹皮 names a part of a plant, and the plant name is a cultivated complex. A package can carry the right name and the wrong material. That is why the naming section is a starting point, not a substitute for the quality checks later on.

### Root bark, not stem bark

The 皮 in 牡丹皮 means bark, but it does not mean any bark on the plant. The material is the bark of the root, and the classical description makes that explicit: "根似芍藥，肉白皮丹" places the description in the root. A stem bark would be a different tissue, with a different structure and a different chemistry, and it would not be the drug.

The distinction is easy to lose in English. "Tree peony bark" could be read as the bark of the tree peony, without specifying which organ. The Chinese name is tighter because the plant name 牡丹 is conventionally associated with the root in materia-medica usage, and the classical entry describes the root directly. A buyer who reads only the English phrase may not realise that the organ is part of the specification.

The anatomy reinforces the point. Root bark sits over a soft inner root tissue; stem bark sits over wood. The two have different thicknesses, different surface patterns, and different drying behaviour. A batch that mixes them is not a blend of equivalents; it is a batch with a variable component that the buyer did not ask for.

For inspection, the root origin is usually visible in the curvature and the inner surface. A root bark tends to curve around the root's cylinder, and its inner surface shows the impression of the tissue it covered. A stem bark has a different curvature and a different inner texture. The visual check is not a laboratory test, but it is a useful screen, and it begins with knowing which organ the name specifies.

The practical instruction is to write "root bark" in the specification, not just "bark". The extra word is the difference between a specification and an assumption.

## What the Bencao Gangmu records

**Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) treats the tree peony in 草之三, and its description of the root is compressed into four characters: "肉白皮丹", a white interior inside a red bark. It then adds a sourcing preference — "惟山中單葉花紅者，根皮入藥爲佳" — for the bark of the red single-petaled mountain form.

The two phrases are worth reading closely. The first is a description of the root's anatomy: a pale inner tissue surrounded by a red outer layer. The second is a judgment about which form was preferred. Neither is a formula, a dose, or a clinical claim. They are the observations of a pharmacognosist describing a material and its best source.

The context is the materia medica, not the incense manual. The *Bencao Gangmu* is organized around medicinal substances, and its interest in 牡丹 is in the root bark as a drug. That is the register of the entry. A reader who lifts the name out of that context and treats it as an aromatic pedigree has changed the subject.

Read as a sourcing note, the preference has an internal logic. A single-petaled flower stands closer to the wild type than a doubled garden cultivar; a mountain habitat suggests slower growth and denser tissue; a red flower was the classical marker of the preferred type. The text does not spell out the reasoning in modern terms, and this page does not invent one. The note is recorded as a historical preference, not as a botanical law.

What the record does not contain is equally important. There is no classical incense formula built on 牡丹皮, no instruction to burn it, and no description of its smoke. The material's classical identity is medicinal. That is the fact the page is built on.

The record also does not establish modern clinical efficacy. A classical text is evidence of historical use, not of controlled trials. The page quotes the text as history and labels the medicinal claims as traditional. It does not translate them into advice.

## Aroma: why the bark is quiet

**Direct answer:** 牡丹皮 smells of dry root, earth, and bark, with a faint floral whisper that is easy to miss. The quietness is not a defect. It is what a root bark smells like, and it is the opposite of the flower that made the tree famous.

The first impression is dusty and woody, closer to a dry root than to a perfume. There is an earthiness that recalls clean soil, a faint bitterness in the back of the nose, and sometimes a dry floral note that appears only after the piece is broken. The overall intensity is low. A bowl of bark pieces on a table will not scent a room the way a bowl of petals would.

The reason is anatomical and chemical. A flower petal is a thin, metabolically active tissue whose volatiles are produced to attract pollinators; the scent compounds are light, volatile, and released at ordinary temperatures. A root bark is a dense, protective tissue. Its chemistry is dominated by phenolic and tannin-type constituents, and its volatile fraction is small. The result is a material that holds its scent rather than broadcasting it.

Paeonol, the phenolic compound associated with moutan cortex in pharmacognosy, is part of that chemistry. It is not a perfume in the way a floral terpene is; it is a solid aromatic constituent of the bark. Its presence helps explain why the material has a medicinal, slightly camphoraceous reputation rather than a sweet floral one. The page names it as a chemical marker, not as a promise of any effect.

Grinding changes the intensity but not the character. A powdered bark has more surface area, so the earthy note is stronger and the faint floral note is easier to detect. The powder still does not smell like the flower. What is released is the bark's own chemistry, not a stored version of the blossom's perfume.

Heat produces the same lesson. A piece of bark warmed on a surface releases a dry, rooty smell; a piece burned produces smoke and char, not a floral plume. The floral note, such a small part of the profile to begin with, does not survive combustion. A reader who expects the flower's fragrance from the bark will be disappointed at every stage.

The comparison with orris root is instructive precisely because it is an exception. Orris is a rhizome with a strong, violet-like scent that develops over years of storage; it is a root that behaves like a perfume material. 牡丹皮 is the opposite case: a root bark whose value is medicinal and whose scent is a whisper. The exception proves the rule, and the rule is that roots are usually quiet.

### Flower and bark side by side

**Direct answer:** Put a petal and a piece of bark side by side and the difference is immediate: the petal is thin, soft, and scented; the bark is dense, hard, and faint. The comparison matters because the names are so close that a buyer can transfer the wrong expectation from one to the other.

| Attribute | Flower 牡丹 | Bark 牡丹皮 |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue | Thin petal | Dense root bark |
| Feel | Soft, cool, flexible | Hard, dry, brittle |
| Break | Tears | Snaps |
| Smell | Sweet, floral, projecting | Rooty, earthy, faint |
| Colour | Cultivar-dependent | Reddish-brown outside, pale inside |
| Use | Ornamental, fragrant | Materia medica |

The petal is built to be seen and smelled. It is thin, metabolically active, and loaded with the volatile compounds that attract pollinators. The bark is built to protect and store. It is dense, lignified, and chemically dominated by constituents that are not designed to evaporate. The two tissues are doing different jobs for the same plant, and their sensory profiles follow from those jobs.

The hand test is a useful screen. A material that is soft, flexible, and strongly floral is not 牡丹皮, whatever the label says. A material that is hard, brittle, and faintly rooty is consistent with the bark. The test does not prove species, but it catches the gross substitution of flower material for bark.

The expectation problem is commercial. A buyer who knows 牡丹 as a luxurious flower may expect a luxurious scent from anything with the name. The bark is not luxurious in that way. It is quiet, dry, and utilitarian, and its value is in a different register. The comparison makes that clear without disparaging either product.

## Why it is not an incense lead

**Direct answer:** 牡丹皮 does not lead an incense formula. Its record is medicinal, its smell is faint, and its dense bark makes a poor burn. A modern blender might add a pinch for an earthy note, but that is an experiment rather than a tradition.

The burn mechanics are against it. Bark is dense and lignified, with little resin or volatile oil to carry a flame. When it is heated, the outer surface chars before the interior dries, and the result is incomplete combustion: smoke, soot, and an acrid edge rather than a clean aromatic plume. The material does not smoulder gracefully the way a resin or a soft wood does.

The chain is easy to follow. Dense bark → slow heat penetration → the surface pyrolyses while the interior is still wet → combustion is incomplete → the smoke carries char and tarry products → the room smells of burning rather than of 牡丹. A blender who wants to use the bark has to solve that problem with other materials, and once the other materials are doing the work, the bark is no longer the point.

There is also the question of what the bark would contribute if it burned well. Its aroma is rooty and earthy, not floral. In a blend, that register can add weight or a sense of ground, but it cannot supply the flower note that the name suggests. A formula that wants a peony-like floral top note needs a different material; the bark will not deliver it.

A modern experimenter could include a small amount of powdered bark in a loose blend, where the powder burns faster and the earthy note might register before the char. That is a legitimate experiment, and it is not a classical use. The page records the distinction because a modern experiment presented as tradition is a small lie that becomes a large one when it is repeated.

The practical alternative depends on the effect wanted. For an earthy root note, a different root or wood may be more suitable; for a floral note, the flower or another floral material is the obvious choice. 牡丹皮 sits in an awkward middle: too faint for a floral role, too dense for a clean burn, and too valuable in its own medicinal market to be treated as a cheap filler.

That is not a criticism of the material. It is a description of its proper use. A material can be excellent in one register and poor in another, and the honest page says which is which. The medicinal register is the one where 牡丹皮 has a long record.

## Processing: the part-separation step

**Direct answer:** Processing is a separation, not a transformation: the root is dug, washed, and stripped of its bark, and the bark is dried. There is no fermentation, no roasting, and no aromatic curing step, because the product is a dried medicinal bark.

The first step is the harvest. The root is lifted, and the bark is separated from the inner woody tissue while the root is still fresh enough to work. The classical description gives the target: white flesh inside, red bark outside. The stripping is done to preserve the bark as intact as possible, because a clean piece is easier to inspect and easier to store.

Drying is the critical step. Fresh bark carries a lot of moisture, and the outer surface loses water faster than the inner surface. That gradient has consequences: the outer layer shrinks first, the piece curls, and the bark can lift away from the inner tissue. If the drying is too fast, the surface can case-harden while the interior stays damp; if it is too slow, mould has time to grow. The practical aim is even drying, not rapid drying.

The mechanism is worth stating plainly. Fresh bark → moisture gradient during drying → differential shrinkage → curling and possible case-hardening → uneven pieces and trapped moisture → a batch that looks dry but is not. A good dryer manages the gradient; a careless one creates a batch that fails in storage.

After drying, the bark is sorted and stored. The storage enemy is moisture, followed by insects and light. A dry, dark, airtight container is the default. The material should not be stored next to strong-smelling substances, because a dried bark can pick up odours from its surroundings even though its own scent is faint.

The processing goal is preservation, not flavour development. That distinguishes 牡丹皮 from tea, from resin, and from a roasted nut. There is no chemical transformation designed to create aroma. The material is valued for what it already contains, and the processor's job is to keep it intact.

## Storage and shelf life

**Direct answer:** Dried 牡丹皮 keeps well if it is kept dry, dark, and sealed. Moisture is the main enemy, insects are the second, and strong odours are the third. A batch that has been properly dried and properly stored keeps well; a batch that was damp when it went into the bag will fail in storage.

The storage logic follows from the material. The bark is hygroscopic: it takes up moisture from humid air, and the moisture softens the tissue and creates conditions for mould. A sealed container with a dry interior is the default. Opening the container in a humid room, or storing it in a paper bag in a damp cupboard, undoes the drying work.

Insects are the second risk. Dried bark is a food source for some storage pests, and an infestation can spread through a batch. Inspection at intake, a clean container, and periodic checking are the practical defences. A batch that has produced dust or webbing should be discarded rather than salvaged.

Odour pickup is the third risk, and it is easy to overlook because the bark's own scent is faint. A dried bark stored beside a strong resin, a spice, or a perfumed product can absorb the neighbour's smell. The result is a batch that no longer smells like itself. Store it separately, in a container that seals.

The shelf-life question has no single number. It depends on the initial dryness, the storage conditions, and the form. Whole pieces keep longer than powder because the surface area is smaller and the interior is protected. A powder is convenient for blending and vulnerable to moisture and odour at the same time.

The sensory check before use is the same as at purchase. Smell the material: a clean, faint, rooty note is right; a musty, sour, or perfume-like note is wrong. Look at the pieces: dry, brittle, and free-running is right; soft, clumping, or discoloured is wrong. The nose and the hand are enough for a screen, and a laboratory is the arbiter when identity is in doubt.

## Grading and adulteration checks

**Direct answer:** Grade 牡丹皮 on part identity, outer and inner surface, cleanliness, dryness, and aroma. Size is cosmetic; part identity is the real grade. A large piece of the wrong part is still the wrong part.

The outer surface should be reddish-brown to dark brown, and the inner surface should be pale. The contrast is the classical "肉白皮丹" seen in a single piece. A piece that is uniformly pale may be inner wood rather than bark; a piece that is uniformly dark may be over-dried or stained. The bark layer should be present and reasonably thick, not a thin shaving that is mostly residue.

| Check | Good sign | Failure mode | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Part | Clear bark layer over pale inner surface | Mostly white wood, little bark | The bark is the material |
| Outer surface | Reddish-brown, dry, clean | Black, stained, mouldy | Storage and handling |
| Inner surface | Pale, dry | Damp, discoloured | Drying quality |
| Thickness | Substantial bark layer | Paper-thin shavings | Yield and grade |
| Cleanliness | No sand, no fleshy residue | Grit, soft tissue | Preparation quality |
| Aroma | Faint rooty, earthy | Musty, sour, chemical | Freshness and storage |
| Moisture | Brittle, free-running | Soft, clumping | Mould risk |

Cleanliness is a real issue because root bark comes from soil. Sand, grit, and fragments of the inner tissue are common. A batch that feels gritty or that leaves dust on the hand has not been properly cleaned. A batch with soft, fleshy residue has not been properly stripped.

Adulteration is harder to see. Other *Paeonia* root barks, and barks from unrelated woody roots, can resemble the material after drying. A visual screen can catch gross substitutions; a laboratory identity test is the reliable check. The buyer's visual inspection is a filter, not a proof.

Colour grading deserves a caution. A dark outer surface can be normal for the material, and a pale surface can be normal for the inner side. A seller who grades by colour alone is grading appearance, not identity. The useful questions are about part, species, cleanliness, and dryness, in that order.

Size grading is the least meaningful criterion. Large pieces are easier to display and slower to dry, but size does not predict quality. A uniform, well-stripped, well-dried lot of moderate pieces is more useful than a mixed lot of showpieces.

## A buyer's decision table

**Direct answer:** Most sourcing mistakes happen before the quality check, at the moment a label is read too generously. The table below pairs common label language with the question that actually settles it.

| Label says | It may mean | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Peony root" | Any *Paeonia* root, any part | Which species, and is it bark or whole root? |
| "牡丹皮" | Tree-peony root bark, if the seller is precise | Is the botanical name *P. suffruticosa* on the lot? |
| "Moutan cortex" | The pharmacognostic drug name | Which lot, which year, and what analysis? |
| "Wild mountain bark" | A style claim, possibly a sourcing claim | Where was it collected, and is there documentation? |
| "Red bark" | A description of the outer surface | Is the inner surface pale and the bark layer intact? |
| "Premium grade" | A seller's own scale | What are the criteria, and who set them? |
| "Organic" | A certification claim | Which certifier, and does it cover this lot? |

The pattern is that the label names a category and the buyer needs a specification. "Peony root" is a category; "牡丹皮, *Paeonia suffruticosa*, root bark, with a stated lot number" is a specification. "Premium" is a judgment; a criterion is a fact. The questions in the third column are short, and they move the conversation from marketing to sourcing.

The table is not an accusation of dishonesty. Most suppliers use the vocabulary their market uses, and a Chinese wholesaler who writes 牡丹皮 is being precise in their own language. The problem is translation: English "peony root" is broader than 牡丹皮, and a buyer who works only in English loses the part distinction that the Chinese name preserves. The fix is to keep the Chinese name in the specification.

## What a good specification looks like

**Direct answer:** A workable specification names the material, the species, the part, the form, the crop year, the cleanliness requirement, and the packing. It is written before the price is discussed, because a price without a specification is a price for an unknown.

The material name should appear in both languages where possible: 牡丹皮 and moutan cortex. The botanical name should follow: *Paeonia suffruticosa*. The part should be explicit: root bark, not root, not flower. These three fields prevent the category errors that no later inspection can repair.

Form is the next field. Whole pieces, broken pieces, and powder are different products with different storage behaviour and different inspection options. A buyer who needs to verify the bark layer should buy pieces, not powder. A buyer who needs a uniform blend may accept powder, but should ask for the lot to be milled from inspected pieces rather than from mixed scrap.

Crop year and lot are the traceability fields. "Current crop" is better than no date; a lot number is better than both. The crop year tells the buyer how old the material is, and the lot number gives a handle if a problem appears later. Neither field guarantees quality, but their absence makes quality impossible to investigate.

Cleanliness and dryness should be stated as requirements rather than as adjectives. "Clean, free of sand and fleshy residue, dry and brittle" is a requirement; "premium" is not. Packing matters for the same reason: a sealed liner and a clean outer sack protect the material, while an open bag invites moisture and odours.

A specification is not a guarantee. It is a basis for rejection, which is more useful. The buyer who writes the spec first can compare what arrives against a document; the buyer who negotiates price first has nothing to compare against except a memory of the sample.

## Inspecting a sample

**Direct answer:** A sample inspection takes a few minutes and answers most of the questions a laboratory would answer later. Look at the piece, break it, smell it, feel it, and compare it with a retained reference. If any step fails, stop; the later steps cannot repair the earlier ones.

Start with the look. A piece of 牡丹皮 should show a reddish-brown outer surface and a pale inner surface, with a visible bark layer between them. The contrast is the classical "肉白皮丹" in miniature. A piece that is pale all through is probably inner wood; a piece that is dark all through may be stained or over-dried. Set aside the pieces that do not show the structure, and judge the lot by the proportion that does.

Then break one piece. Dry bark should snap cleanly, with a hard, brittle fracture. A piece that bends, feels leathery, or tears rather than snaps is holding moisture. That is a storage failure, and it is the single most useful thing a buyer can detect by hand.

Smell the broken surface. The note should be faint, rooty, and clean. A musty or sour smell means mould or poor storage; a perfume-like smell may mean the batch has picked up odours from a neighbour in the warehouse. A strong, sharp, chemical smell is not the natural profile of this bark and deserves a question.

Feel the surface and the break. Grit or sand on the outside is a cleaning failure. Soft, fleshy residue on the inner side is a stripping failure. A dust-covered piece may have been handled roughly, and a lot that is mostly dust has lost the structural evidence a buyer needs.

The last step is comparison. Keep a small reference sample from a lot that was verified, and compare the new sample against it under the same light. Memory is unreliable across weeks; a physical reference is not. The reference does not need to be expensive. It needs to be documented, and it needs to be kept dry.

## Telling the peonies apart

**Direct answer:** The English word "peony" covers a flower, a root bark, and a different species' root, and the four common confusions are easy to separate once the part and the species are named.

| Name | Plant | Part | Aroma | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 牡丹 | *Paeonia suffruticosa* | Flower | Sweet, floral, prominent | Ornamental, fragrant |
| 牡丹皮 | *Paeonia suffruticosa* | Root bark | Rooty, earthy, faint | Materia medica |
| 芍药 | *Paeonia lactiflora* | Root | Earthy, starchy | Materia medica |
| Orris root | *Iris* species | Rhizome | Violet-like, powdery | Perfumery |

The first two are the same plant, different parts. The third is a different species with a similar English name. The fourth is not a peony at all; it is an iris rhizome that happens to be a famous rooty perfume material. The table is short because the distinctions are simple once they are stated.

The flower is the fragrant part. That is the sentence to remember. The bark is the medicinal part. The herbaceous root is a different medicinal part from a different plant. Orris is a different family and a different aromatic register.

A buyer who is offered "peony root" should ask which plant and which part. If the answer is *P. suffruticosa* root bark, the material is 牡丹皮. If the answer is *P. lactiflora* root, the material is 芍药. If the answer is "peony flower", the material is neither. The questions are short, and they prevent most of the category errors in this corner of the trade.

## Safety and handling

**Direct answer:** 牡丹皮 is a materia-medica material, not a culinary herb. It should not be eaten casually, dosed at home, or treated as safe because it is plant-based. Its medicinal use belongs with a qualified practitioner, and its smoke belongs in a ventilated room.

The first safety point is category. A dried root bark that appears in a pharmacopoeia is not a spice. It has a medicinal history, and medicinal materials carry dose and interaction questions that food materials do not. This page does not provide a dose, a preparation, or an indication, and a reader should not infer one from the classical quotations.

The second point is storage. Damp bark can grow mould, and mouldy material should be discarded rather than cleaned and used. Insects can infest a stored batch; a sealed container and periodic inspection are the ordinary defences. Keep the material out of reach of children and pets, who may chew or scatter it.

The third point is combustion. If the bark is burned as part of an experimental blend, it produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, use a heat-resistant holder, keep the material away from flammables, and never leave a burning blend unattended. A faint scent in the jar does not mean a faint smoke in the room. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general rules.

The fourth point is the boundary around traditional belief. The classical record is a historical fact; it is not a modern safety or efficacy assessment. A material can be traditional and still be inappropriate for a particular person. The page states the boundary rather than pretending it does not exist.

## Tradition and evidence

**Direct answer:** The plant identity and the classical record are established. The old medicinal claims are traditional belief, not modern clinical evidence. The page keeps those categories apart and makes no medical claim.

The documented facts are these: *Paeonia suffruticosa* is a woody peony of the family Paeoniaceae; its root bark is 牡丹皮; the *Bencao Gangmu* records the tree under 牡丹 and describes the root as white flesh with red bark; the classical text prefers the red single-petaled mountain form. Those facts are enough to identify the material and to explain its place in the materia medica.

The traditional belief is a different layer. The classical texts discuss the bark in their own framework, and that framework is not the framework of modern clinical research. Quoting the text as history is legitimate; turning the quotation into a health claim is not. The page uses the text for naming, anatomy, and sourcing, and it stops there.

The incense question is separate again. There is no classical incense record for 牡丹皮, and the material's aroma is faint. A modern blender can experiment, but the experiment should be labelled as modern. The page does not invent a censer tradition for a root bark that never had one.

What follows from this is a modest conclusion. 牡丹皮 is a well-documented medicinal bark with a quiet scent and a clear part-name. Its value is in the part-specification, the classical record, and the quality of the dried bark. It is not a floral perfume, not an incense staple, and not a substitute for the flower that shares its tree.

## FAQ

**What is tree peony bark (牡丹皮)?** The dried root-bark layer of the tree peony, *Paeonia suffruticosa* (Paeoniaceae). The trade name is moutan cortex; the material is faintly rooty rather than floral, and its classical standing is medicinal.

**Is 牡丹皮 the same as 牡丹?** No. They come from the same tree, but 牡丹 is the flower and 牡丹皮 is the root bark. The flower is the fragrant, ornamental part; the bark is the quiet, medicinal part. The shared plant name does not make them interchangeable.

**Is 牡丹皮 the same as 芍药?** No. One is tree-peony root bark; the other is the root of *Paeonia lactiflora*, the herbaceous peony. They are different species and separate drugs, and the English word "peony" hides the difference.

**Why is it called 牡丹皮?** The name is a specification: 牡丹 names the tree peony, 皮 names the bark, and 丹 evokes the reddish outer surface. The *Bencao Gangmu* describes the root as "肉白皮丹" — white flesh, red bark — which is exactly the structure the name encodes.

**Does 牡丹皮 belong in incense?** Not as a classical material. Its standing is medicinal, its scent is faint, and its dense bark burns poorly. A modern blender may experiment with a pinch for an earthy note, but that is a modern experiment, not a tradition.

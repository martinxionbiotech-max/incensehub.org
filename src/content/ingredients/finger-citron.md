> **Direct answer:** Finger citron — 佛手 (fóshǒu), "Buddha's hand" — is the fingered form of the citron, *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis*, in the rue family (Rutaceae). Its fruit divides into thick, oil-rich segments instead of closing into a solid rind, and it is used as a display fragrance: a 清供 offering fruit that scents a room by evaporation and is never burned. The name is a trap for buyers, because 佛手柑 in Chinese names the bergamot, a different citrus.

## Key facts

> **Direct answer:** The essentials are these: a citron variety, a rind that splits into fingers, almost no pulp, a bright citrus-floral scent, and a display use that never involves burning.

- **Chinese name:** 佛手 (fóshǒu), "Buddha's hand", taken from the shape of the fruit.
- **Lookalike name:** 佛手柑 is not this fruit; in Chinese it names the bergamot.
- **Botany:** a variety of the citron — *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis* — in the rue family.
- **Structure:** thick rind divided into finger-like segments, with pulp almost entirely absent.
- **Scent:** bright citrus lift over a soft floral sweetness, never sharp or sour.
- **Use:** displayed whole for room fragrance, or candied and dried as food.
- **Not a burning material:** absent from the classical compendium 香乘.
- **Grading:** none published; the buyer judges freshness, rind and scent.

## What the name actually says

> **Direct answer:** 佛手 means "Buddha's hand", a name taken from the shape of the fruit rather than from any religious use. The same characters appear in 佛手柑, which in Chinese names the bergamot — so the name that looks most specific is the one most likely to mislead.

Chinese fruit names often describe a form, and this one does it precisely: the segments curve outward like fingers, and the whole fruit reads as a hand. The name is a visual description that became a cultural one, and the fruit's role in New Year display follows from that reading — a hand-shaped fruit carrying an auspicious gesture into the house.

The trouble begins one character later. 佛手柑 adds 柑, the character for a citrus fruit, and the resulting word does not mean "the citrus called Buddha's hand". It names the bergamot. An English reader who sees "finger citron" and a Chinese reader who sees 佛手柑 are looking at two different plants, and neither label alone resolves which one is meant.

English does not escape the problem, it only relocates it. "Finger citron" describes the shape and the genus at once, which is more informative than "Buddha's hand" but still stops short of a species. "Buddha's hand" describes only the shape. Neither phrase tells a buyer whether the fruit in the crate is the citron variety or something else with divided segments, and no English common name is obliged to.

The asymmetry is worth noticing because it shapes how the confusion travels. A Chinese reader can see the characters and misread them; an English reader can see a translation and never learn that the original word carried a different plant. Translation launders the ambiguity into something that looks precise. That is why the binomial belongs on the paperwork in both languages, and why a label that offers only a common name has not finished the job.

That is the naming trap in its plainest form, and it is worth stating as a rule rather than an anecdote. When a Chinese plant name is built from a shape, treat the shape as a description and the species as the fact. The description can be borrowed by a different plant; the binomial cannot.

## Three citrus that get mixed up

> **Direct answer:** 佛手, 香橼 and 佛手柑 are three different things in Chinese, and only two of them are closely related. 佛手 is the fingered citron, 香橼 is the plain citron, and 佛手柑 is the bergamot.

| Name | Species | Fruit form | Primary use | The risk in the name |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 佛手 (finger citron) | *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis* | Split into finger-like segments | 清供 display and room fragrance | Read as "Buddha's hand", then assumed to be the bergamot |
| 香橼 (citron) | *Citrus medica* | A closed, thick-rinded fruit | Fragrant rind, display, some processing | Treated as a separate species when it is the parent |
| 佛手柑 (bergamot) | *Citrus bergamia* | A small, round, bitter citrus | Essential oil, flavouring | Assumed to be the finger citron because of the characters |

Read the second column and the confusion dissolves. 佛手 and 香橼 share a species; one is the variety with split carpels, the other is the ordinary form. 佛手柑 is not in that family group at all — it is a distinct citrus grown primarily for its oil, which is why its name in English is "bergamot" and not "finger citron".

The practical consequence is a specification problem. A purchase order that says "finger citron" in English is reasonably safe. A purchase order that says 佛手柑 in Chinese is asking for bergamot, whatever the English column says. Bilingual paperwork needs the binomial precisely because the two languages do not divide these fruits the same way.

For the parent species, see the [citron entry](/ingredients/citron/); for the oil crop that borrows the name, see [bergamot](/ingredients/bergamot/).

## The botany of a fruit that split

> **Direct answer:** The finger citron is a variety of *Citrus medica*, one of the oldest cultivated citrus, native to the Himalayan foothills and long grown across China. Its distinguishing trait is developmental: the carpels fail to fuse, so the fruit opens into separate segments instead of closing.

In an ordinary citrus the carpels — the segments that would become the juicy vesicles — fuse into a single body inside a continuous rind. In this variety they do not complete that fusion. The result is a fruit made of diverging sections, each carrying its own stretch of rind, and the whole structure open to the air in a way a closed fruit is not.

Flora of China treats the fingered form within *Citrus medica* and records the varietal name *sarcodactylis*. GBIF lists that variety name as a synonym of *Citrus medica*, which is the taxonomic way of saying the same thing: this is a form of the citron, not a separate species. [Flora of China's *Citrus medica* treatment](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200012432) and the [GBIF record for *Citrus medica*](https://www.gbif.org/species/3190157) are the references used here.

One more botanical point follows from the variety status. A named form is maintained by vegetative propagation — cuttings or grafts — rather than by seed, because seed-grown plants do not reliably reproduce a shape. The fingers are a trait that has to be kept, not a trait that reappears on its own. That is why the fruit on a table in one region looks consistent with the fruit from the same stock in another, and why a seedling grown from a finger citron's seed may produce an ordinary citron.

The taxonomy matters to a buyer because it sets the expectation. A fingered citron is not a rare species with a unique chemistry; it is the common citron expressed in an unusual shape. What makes it valuable is the same thing that makes any citron valuable — rind oil — concentrated in a form that exposes much more of that rind to the air.

## Why there is almost no juice

> **Direct answer:** The same developmental failure that produces the fingers leaves almost no closed cavity for pulp. The fruit's value therefore sits entirely in the rind, and the rind is where the oil is.

Follow the structure and the economics become obvious. Carpels fail to fuse → no sealed interior develops → pulp has nowhere to accumulate → the fruit is mostly rind → the rind is where the oil glands sit → the oil is the fragrance. A fruit that cannot hold juice can still hold scent, and this one holds a great deal of it.

That single chain explains several things a reader might otherwise treat as separate facts. It explains why the fruit is never sold as eating citrus. It explains why the rind is the part that gets candied. It explains why the fruit is judged by scent and condition rather than by weight or sweetness. It also explains why the fingers are thick: with no juicy interior to fill, the rind takes up most of the fruit's volume.

| Part | What it is | What it is worth |
|---|---|---|
| Rind | Thick, oil-bearing outer layer divided into segments | The fragrance; the whole value of the fruit |
| Pith | The white layer under the rind | Bulk; no aromatic value |
| Pulp | Almost absent | Nothing to eat or juice |
| Fingers | The separated rind segments | Surface area — the reason the scent travels |

The fourth row is the one that connects form to function. More fingers means more exposed rind, and more exposed rind means faster release of volatiles. The shape is not decoration laid over the fragrance; it is a structure that changes how quickly the fragrance leaves.

## Scent without a flame

> **Direct answer:** The finger citron perfumes a room by evaporation from its rind oil. Nothing is lit, nothing is ground, and nothing is distilled — the fruit is simply present, and the air moving past it does the rest.

This is the part of Chinese fragrance culture that incense writing tends to skip. A room can be scented by a material that is never burned: fruit on a table, flowers in a vase, wood in a cabinet. The mechanism is evaporation rather than combustion, and the result is a scent that arrives slowly, sits close to the object and never produces smoke.

The chain runs like this: rind oil sits in glands near the surface → air moves across the rind → volatile molecules leave the oil and enter the room air → the scent is perceptible at a distance of a metre or two → the oil reserve declines → the scent weakens. Every stage is passive. There is no heat to accelerate the process and no smoke to carry it.

That passivity has a practical consequence for anyone used to incense. A burned material announces itself across a room and then stops when the stick ends. A display fruit works the other way: it is quiet, local and continuous, and it ends not with a finish line but with a gradual decline. Judging it by the standards of incense — intensity, throw, a defined burn time — will make it seem weak. Judging it as a room object, it is doing exactly what it was chosen to do.

The distinction is worth keeping in the vocabulary, because the two categories fail differently. A bad incense material fails by smelling harsh or by going out. A bad display fruit fails by having no scent at all, or by having a scent that sours as the rind turns.

## Reading the scent in stages

> **Direct answer:** The fruit's scent is brightest in the first minute, softens into a floral sweetness as the nose adapts, and settles into a quiet rind note. Warmth raises the intensity and shortens the life of the oil at the same time.

Smelling a finger citron properly takes longer than a single inhale. The first impression is the sharpest: a bright citrus lift with a floral edge, closer to a perfumed peel than to a lemon. That brightness is the most volatile fraction of the rind oil, and it is also the first thing to go as the fruit ages.

What follows is partly the material and partly the nose. Olfactory adaptation reduces the perceived intensity of a constant smell within minutes, so a fruit that seems to fill a room on arrival can feel quieter half an hour later without having released less oil. The floral and balsamic part of the profile tends to remain legible after the citrus edge has faded from perception, which is why a fruit can smell different at the end of an evening than at the start.

Temperature runs through all of it. A warm room increases the rate at which volatiles leave the rind, so the scent is stronger and the reserve empties sooner. A cool room slows both. That trade-off is the whole physics of a display fruit: you can have intensity or longevity, and the room decides which one you get.

The chain is short and worth remembering: warmth → faster evaporation → stronger scent now → less oil later. It explains why a fruit placed near a radiator makes a strong first impression and a disappointing second week.

There is no useful vocabulary of top, heart and base here, because those terms describe a composed perfume unfolding on skin. A single fruit is one material releasing one oil at a declining rate. Describing it in perfume language overstates the structure; describing it as bright-then-soft is closer to what actually happens.

## 清供: the display tradition

> **Direct answer:** 清供 is the practice of arranging auspicious objects — fruit, flowers, scholars' stones, vessels — for display, especially at the New Year. The finger citron is one of its classic fragrance fruits, valued for scent and shape together.

The tradition is an arrangement, not a recipe. Objects are placed on a table or an altar in a composition that carries meaning, and the finger citron earns its place twice: the shape reads as a hand, and the scent fills the space around the arrangement. Neither quality is subordinate to the other.

This is why the fruit is displayed rather than processed. Candying it preserves the rind for eating; drying it preserves the shape for a season. Displaying it fresh does something neither of those does — it keeps the oil where it belongs, in the rind, releasing slowly into the room. The offering and the fragrance are the same act.

The classical incense compendium 香乘 does not list 佛手 as an incense ingredient. That absence is informative rather than accidental. The compendium deals with materials that are burned or otherwise worked into combustible preparations; a fresh fruit displayed for its scent belongs to a different practice with its own logic. Reading the absence as a gap in the record would be a mistake. It is a boundary between two crafts.

In a modern home the practice translates without much effort. A single fruit on a plate, kept away from direct sun and heat, does the work. The arrangement can be as simple as one fruit and one vessel, which is also how it appears in much of the classical record — the object matters more than the quantity.

## Display practice

> **Direct answer:** Put the fruit on a shallow dish in a cool, still spot out of direct sun, handle it by the body rather than the fingertips, and remove it when soft spots appear. The display is the processing.

Most of what goes wrong with a display fruit goes wrong in the first day. Fruit piled in a bowl bruises where the fingers press against each other; fruit left in a hot window loses its oil quickly; fruit sealed in a container has nowhere to release its scent and sits in its own humidity. A single layer on an open dish avoids all three.

Placement is a balance between two needs that pull in opposite directions. Air movement carries the scent into the room, so a completely still corner keeps the fragrance to itself. Strong draught depletes the rind faster, so a doorway or a fan is worse than a quiet table. The usual answer is a spot with gentle incidental air movement — a room people walk through, away from the vent.

Handling matters more than it looks. The fingers are rind, and rind bruises. Lifting the fruit by a finger concentrates pressure on a thin section and leaves a soft spot that becomes the first site of rot. Lift from the base with the whole hand, and put the fruit down rather than dropping it.

Grouping is a legitimate choice. Two or three fruits on one dish read as a stronger arrangement and put more oil into the room, and the effect lasts as long as the fruit with the shortest life. A single fruit is the quieter option and easier to keep in condition.

The end is predictable. The rind stiffens, the colour dulls, the fingers lose their firmness, and the scent thins to a memory of itself. A soft or discoloured spot is the signal to remove the fruit before it affects the arrangement — and before it attracts anything it should not.

## What "fresh" means for a fragrance fruit

> **Direct answer:** Freshness in a finger citron means an intact, firm, oil-rich rind with a strong clean scent. It is the only variable that reliably tracks what the fruit is worth, and it is measured in days rather than months.

There is no published grading ladder for this fruit, and there does not need to be one, because the market is small and the judgement is direct. The signals are few and they are readable without instruments.

| Signal | What it tells you | What it does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Scent at arm's length | Volatile oil still present in the rind | How long it will stay that way in your room |
| Firmness of the fingers | Water content and rind condition | Whether the oil is rich or thin |
| Surface of the rind | Handling damage, bruising, rot | Age since harvest |
| Colour of the skin | Ripeness stage — green through yellow | A quality ranking; both stages are sold |
| Weight for its size | Moisture, density of the rind | Aromatic strength |
| Number of fingers | Form and visual balance | Anything about scent |

The last row is the one worth arguing about, and the argument is editorial. A fruit with many slender fingers is striking on a table, and buyers pay for that. It does not follow that more fingers means more fragrance. Finger count is a shape trait; the oil sits in the rind regardless of how the rind is divided. Treat the count as an aesthetic decision and the scent as a separate one.

The same caution applies to colour. Green and yellow fruits are both sold, and both can be fragrant. Colour records the stage of ripeness, not a grade. A yellow fruit that has lost its scent is not better than a green one that still carries it.

## Forms, and what each one costs

> **Direct answer:** The fresh whole fruit is the fragrance form; candied rind is a food; dried rind is a compromise that keeps the shape and loses much of the scent. The three are not interchangeable, and only the first belongs to the display tradition.

| Form | What happens to the oil | Best use | What is lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh whole fruit | Intact in the rind, released slowly | 清供 display, room fragrance | Nothing — this is the reference form |
| Candied rind | Largely driven off or masked by sugar | Confectionery | The fragrance, replaced by sweetness |
| Dried rind | Mostly gone; a faint residue may remain | Shape retention, craft use, some infusions | The top notes, the freshness, the volume of scent |

Candying is a preservation method, and preservation always costs the volatile fraction. Heat drives off the light molecules first — the citrus top notes — and sugar covers what is left. The result can be excellent food and is not a fragrance material.

Drying is the more tempting mistake, because a dried finger citron still looks like a finger citron. It keeps the silhouette and loses the point. The rind shrinks, darkens and stiffens, and the oil that made the fruit worth displaying has largely left. A dried fruit on a table is a decorative object, not a scent source.

The practical rule that follows: buy fresh for fragrance, buy candied for the table, and do not expect the dried form to do the work of the fresh one.

## Why a fresh fruit is not a perfume ingredient

> **Direct answer:** The classical use never extracts the oil. The fruit is the delivery system — the rind holds the oil, the air takes it, and the room receives it. That is a different model from an essential-oil crop, where peel is processed in quantity for a concentrate.

It is tempting to treat the finger citron as a raw material waiting to be processed, and that framing misleads in both directions. It suggests the fruit is undervalued unless distilled, and it suggests that a scented fruit and a bottle of citrus oil are the same thing at different concentrations. Neither follows.

The material is perishable, watery and sold in small quantities, which is the opposite of what an oil house needs. Citrus oil production works from peel in bulk, pressed or distilled, with the fruit as a by-product stream. The finger citron enters the world as a display object and leaves it the same way. Its oil is never isolated in the tradition, and the tradition's logic does not require it to be.

What follows for a reader is a shift in how value is judged. An oil crop is evaluated by yield, consistency and chemistry. A display fruit is evaluated by the strength and cleanliness of the scent in the room, the condition of the rind, and how long the impression lasts. Applying the first set of criteria to the second produces a false negative: a perfectly good fruit looks like a poor raw material.

The distinction also explains why the fruit is not in the incense compendium. A material that is never burned and never extracted has no place in a book about combustible preparations. Its absence is a classification, not an oversight.

## Four ways a room gets scented

> **Direct answer:** A room can be scented by burning, by evaporation, by spraying and by slow release from dried material. The finger citron belongs to the second method, and comparing the four shows why its quietness is a property of the method rather than a weakness of the fruit.

| Method | What carries the scent | How it starts | How long it lasts | What it leaves behind |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Burning | Smoke and hot gases | Immediately, with a flame or coal | As long as the material burns | Ash, smoke, particulate matter |
| Display fruit | Evaporating rind oil | Slowly, over the first hours | Days to weeks, declining | A spent fruit to discard |
| Spray or diffuser | Atomised oil or vapour | At once, then fading | Hours, unless replenished | Empty vessel, no residue in the air |
| Dried sachet | Slow evaporation from dried material | Gradually | Weeks to months, weak throughout | A scentless bag at the end |

The table is a set of trade-offs rather than a ranking. Burning gives the strongest presence and the most residue. A spray gives instant control and no persistence. A sachet gives patience and very little intensity. A display fruit sits in the middle: more presence than a sachet, far less than smoke, and no combustion products at all.

The row that matters here is the second. Its onset is slow because evaporation is slow, and its decline is gradual because the reservoir is finite. A reader who expects the fruit to behave like a stick of incense will read the slow onset as weakness. A reader who expects a room object will read the same behaviour as the point.

The comparison also clarifies what the fruit cannot do. It will not scent a large, open, airy space, because the oil leaves the rind at a rate set by temperature and air movement, not by demand. It will not deliver a chosen intensity at a chosen time. What it will do is sit in a small room and be quietly present, which is a different brief and, in the 清供 tradition, the intended one.

## The oil in the rind

> **Direct answer:** Citrus rind oil is a light, terpene-rich mixture held in tiny glands in the peel. The most volatile part leaves first, which is why the bright top of the scent fades before the softer floral note.

The rind is not a solid wall with scent painted on it. It is a layer of tissue studded with oil glands, and the oil inside is a mixture of compounds with different boiling points and different vapour pressures. The lightest molecules leave the surface first, and the heavier ones linger. That single fact explains the shape of the scent over time: bright at the start, softer later.

It also explains why a bruise changes the smell. Damaging the rind ruptures glands, and the oil that was sealed inside is suddenly exposed over a larger area. For a short while the bruised spot smells stronger than the intact rind around it. Then the exposed oil oxidises, and the same spot turns dull or sharp. A fruit that smells unusually intense at one point is not necessarily a better fruit; it may be a damaged one.

The chain runs: gland ruptured → oil exposed → rapid evaporation → a burst of scent → oxidation of the exposed film → a flat or off note at the site. This is the mechanism behind the advice to handle the fruit gently, and it is also the reason a display fruit should not be prodded repeatedly to check whether it still smells.

Terpene chemistry is the reason citrus oils are described as "light" in perfumery. The molecules are small and volatile, so they lift quickly and disperse quickly. A resin, by contrast, is heavy and slow. The two materials occupy opposite ends of the same continuum, and the finger citron sits firmly at the light end — which is why it perfumes a room without ever filling it the way a resin does.

## A buyer's checklist

> **Direct answer:** Confirm the species, smell the fruit before anything else, then judge rind condition and firmness. Freshness is the specification; the name is the risk.

| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identity | Require *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis* on the paperwork, in Latin | Separates 佛手 from 香橼 and from 佛手柑 |
| 2. Scent | Smell at arm's length before handling | The rind oil is the product; a scentless fruit has no value |
| 3. Condition | Check every finger for bruising, soft spots and mould | Damage spreads quickly in a fruit with exposed segments |
| 4. Firmness | Feel the fingers; they should be firm, not limp | A limp fruit is past its display life |
| 5. Form | Judge finger count and balance last | Aesthetic, not aromatic |
| 6. Quantity | Buy for the display period, not for storage | The scent declines from the day of purchase |

The order is deliberate. Identity first, because everything downstream is wasted on the wrong citrus. Scent second, because it is the only variable that cannot be repaired. Condition and firmness third, because they determine how long the fruit will last. Form last, because it is the only variable that is purely visual.

The wider sourcing discipline behind these steps is set out in the [wholesale guide](/blog/wholesale-guide/). The finger citron is an unusual case within it — a fresh perishable rather than a stored material — but the discipline of specifying identity before appearance applies throughout.

## Buying when you cannot smell the fruit

> **Direct answer:** If the fruit is not in front of you, buy on freshness proxies and an agreement about condition, not on a photograph. Scent does not travel through a listing, and no image proves that a rind still holds oil.

This is the normal situation for most buyers, and it is the one where the checklist above needs adjusting. Steps two and four — scent and firmness — cannot be performed at a distance. What remains is a set of proxies, none of them conclusive, all of them better than a picture.

- **Harvest timing.** Ask when the fruit was picked and how long it has been in transit. A short answer with a date is worth more than "fresh stock".
- **Condition language.** Ask specifically about bruising, soft spots and mould at the base, and ask for the answer in writing. A seller who answers precisely is describing a real fruit.
- **Packing method.** Fruit packed in a single layer with padding arrives in better condition than fruit tipped into a sack. The packing tells you what the seller expects to happen in transit.
- **Quantity for the window.** Order what will be displayed within the fruit's useful life, not what will fill a storeroom.
- **A stated end point.** Agree what happens if the fruit arrives soft or scentless. A seller who will not discuss it is telling you something.

The logic behind the list is that every proxy is a claim about freshness, and freshness is the only thing being bought. A photograph can show shape and colour, both of which survive a bad fruit. It cannot show oil content, firmness or the smell of the rind, which are the three variables that decide whether the purchase was worth making.

There is an honest version of this trade and a convenient one. The honest version says the fruit is at the end of its window and prices it accordingly. The convenient version sends a photograph of the best fruit in the batch. Nothing in a listing distinguishes the two, which is why the written condition agreement matters more than the image.

## Where it grows

> **Direct answer:** The citron is native to the Himalayan foothills and has been grown in China for a very long time. Chinese cultivation of the fingered form is associated with several provinces, including Zhejiang, Guangdong, Fujian and Sichuan.

Geography matters less here than it does for a resin or a wood, because the fruit is traded fresh and the supply chain is short. A finger citron is not shipped across an ocean to be displayed; it is grown, picked and sold within a region and its near neighbours. That keeps the material close to its growing areas and makes seasonality a real constraint rather than a marketing word.

The practical effect on a buyer is a calendar. Fruit is available in the weeks after harvest, and what is on a table in one month is not what is on a table in another. A display tradition built around the New Year is built around a season, and the fruit's availability is part of why the practice has a calendar shape rather than a year-round one.

## The season is part of the material

> **Direct answer:** A fresh display fruit is a seasonal good, and its season is tied to the winter display calendar. Supply is regional and short, which is why the fruit is bought in the weeks it is available rather than stored against the year.

This is not a material that sits in a warehouse. It respires, loses water and gives up its oil from the moment it is picked, so the trade runs on a short clock: harvest, move, display, discard. A buyer who plans a display for a fixed date is planning against a harvest, not against a shelf.

The seasonality has a cultural shape as well as a commercial one. The fruit's association with New Year display is the reason demand concentrates in a particular part of the year, and the reason growers time their crop to that demand. Supply and custom reinforce each other, which is why the finger citron reads as a festival material rather than an everyday one.

The early part of the window is usually the better part for a buyer, because the fruit has travelled less and the rind has had less time to lose oil. Late-season fruit can still be good, but it has spent more of its reserve before it reaches the table, and the decline after purchase starts from a lower point.

For anyone outside the growing regions, that means availability is uneven. Fruit may appear in a market for a few weeks and then vanish, and what arrives later in the season is not the same quality as what arrived first. Buying early in the window and buying for immediate display are the two habits that follow from the calendar.

## Tradition and evidence

> **Direct answer:** The 清供 and room-fragrance uses are historically documented cultural practice. Medicinal uses recorded in the tradition are traditional belief, not established clinical fact, and this page makes no health claims.

The identity of the plant is settled work: the fingered form sits inside *Citrus medica*, and the standard floras treat it that way. Its relationship to the plain citron is settled for the same reason — a variety is not a separate species, and the naming records that.

The display use is a different kind of evidence. Nobody needs a laboratory to establish that people put the fruit on tables; the record is cultural, and it is documented as cultural practice, including the New Year setting.

The medicinal material is a third kind again. Statements in the classical literature describe what was recorded and believed; they do not constitute clinical findings, and this page carries them as traditional belief. The absence of the fruit from 香乘 belongs in the same category of textual evidence — a documented omission from a specific book, not proof about the plant.

The boundary is worth stating plainly because the material attracts two kinds of writing that blur it. One treats the fruit as a medicine and imports claims the sources do not support. The other treats it as mere decoration and drops the cultural record entirely. Both lose information. What the record supports is a display practice with a long history, a clear mechanism and a limited set of claims.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The finger citron is displayed rather than burned, so the usual incense smoke warnings apply only if someone chooses to burn the rind. Fresh fruit should be kept where it will not be eaten by pets or left to rot.

If the rind is burned anyway — as a scent experiment, not as a tradition — the ordinary combustion cautions apply: smoke and particulate matter are produced, ventilation matters, and concentrated smoke should not be inhaled. Burning is not the recommended use, and it is not how the fruit earned its place. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general framework.

> **Direct answer:** The recurring questions are about identity, scent, longevity and whether the fruit can be burned; short answers follow, with the detail above.

**What is finger citron (佛手)?** The fingered form of the citron, *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis*, in the Rutaceae. Its fruit divides into thick, oil-rich segments with almost no pulp, and it is displayed as a 清供 fragrance fruit rather than burned.

**Does finger citron count as citron (香橼)?** One species, two forms. 香橼 is ordinary *Citrus medica*; 佛手 is the variety in which the carpels fail to fuse and the fingers appear. Both exist for their rind, and each has a separate entry here.

**Is finger citron the same plant as bergamot (佛手柑)?** No — the naming trap in its clearest form. 佛手柑 is Chinese for *Citrus bergamia*, an oil citrus from a different branch of the genus. The characters overlap and the plants do not.

**What does finger citron smell like?** Citrus and floral at once, bright and sweet, with a perfumed rather than tart character. The scent comes from oil in the thick rind, not from juice, and it reaches the room by evaporation.

**Why does my finger citron lose its scent?** Because the oil is volatile and the rind is the reservoir. Once the fruit is picked, the oil begins to leave the rind, and the process accelerates in warmth and moving air. A fruit that has gone scentless has spent its oil, and no amount of display will bring it back.

**Can I burn the rind as incense?** You can, but it is not the traditional use and it is not what the fruit is good at. Combustion destroys the light citrus notes and produces smoke; the display tradition exists precisely because evaporation does the job without either.

**How long will one fruit scent a room?** It varies with the fruit, the room and the air movement, so no fixed figure is honest. The reliable statement is directional: the scent is strongest in the first days after harvest and declines steadily, faster in a warm, dry, draughty room than in a cool, still one.

## Summary

Think of 佛手 as a citron that never closed. The carpels stay apart, so the peel divides into fingers, no cavity forms for pulp, and the oil-rich rind becomes the entire product. In the 清供 tradition it scents a room by evaporation alone — no flame, no smoke, no residue. Freshness is the only meaningful grade, the binomial is the only safe way to specify it, and the 柑 in 佛手柑 belongs to a different citrus entirely.

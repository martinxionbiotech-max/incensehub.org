**Direct answer:** 香草荚 (xiāngcǎo jiá) is the cured pod of *Vanilla planifolia*, an orchid vine whose home range lies in Mexico and Central America. The pod is picked green and nearly scentless; the vanilla people recognise is created afterwards, by a curing process that turns a bound precursor into free vanillin. It has no place in the old Chinese aromatic canon, and this page treats it as what it is: a modern flavour and fragrance material with a tropical American origin.

## Key facts

**Direct answer:** The traded object is a fermented and dried orchid pod, not a flower, resin, or wood. Its value rests on a curing chemistry that happens after harvest, which is why two pods of the same origin can smell completely different.

## The pod on the vine

**Direct answer:** Vanilla is an orchid, and a peculiar one: a climbing vine with aerial roots, reduced leaves, and a flower that must be pollinated before a single pod can form. The pod is the fruit, and the "bean" of trade is that fruit cured and dried.

*Vanilla planifolia* grows as a hemiepiphyte. It starts on the ground or on a host tree, climbs with aerial roots that cling to bark, and produces fleshy stems and leaves that are little more than scales. In the wild it is a forest plant of humid, shaded places. Under cultivation it is trained on posts or trees, pruned to keep the vine within reach, and shaded enough to mimic the canopy.

The flower is the bottleneck. Vanilla flowers are short-lived, and outside the plant's native range the specialist bee that pollinates it is absent. Commercial plantations therefore rely on hand pollination, a delicate operation performed flower by flower with a small stick or blade. A vine that flowers abundantly can still set few pods if the pollination is rushed or the weather is wrong. This is one reason vanilla is expensive before curing even begins: the labour is in the flower, not only in the drying shed.

The fruit itself is a long, slender capsule filled with tiny seeds and a dark, oily pulp. It takes months to mature on the vine. Picked green, it is firm, waxy, and almost odourless. Left too long, it splits and spoils. The harvest window is narrow, and the picker's judgment matters as much as the curer's.

Commercial cultivation is a trellis crop, not an orchard. Vines are trained up living trees or wooden posts, shaded during establishment, and pruned so that the flowering stems hang within arm's reach. The plants need high humidity, steady warmth, and protection from wind; a dry spell at flowering can cost the crop. Because the flowers must be pollinated by hand outside the Americas, the grower's labour calendar is set by a single short window. That combination — a long growing season, a brief pollination window, and months of curing — explains why vanilla is among the most labour-intensive flavour crops in the world, and why cheap "vanilla" is usually something else.

Botanically, vanilla is the only orchid that supplies a major traded aroma. That fact is worth pausing on, because orchids are usually grown for flowers, not for pods. The vanilla trade is a fruit trade inside a flower family, and the pod's value comes from a chemical transformation that no other orchid is cultivated to perform at scale.

Its wild range runs from Mexico through Central America. The species is absent from the Chinese flora; it has no Flora of China entry. Farms in southern China and elsewhere in the tropics now grow it, but a farm is not a pedigree. A pod grown in southern China is still a New World species, and the page should say so plainly.

## The scent is made, not harvested

**Direct answer:** A green vanilla pod has almost no vanilla smell. The characteristic aroma appears only after curing, when an enzyme splits glucovanillin and releases free vanillin. That is the core fact: the scent is manufactured by processing, not present in the fruit at picking.

Pick a green pod and cut it open. It smells green, slightly resinous, maybe a little like cut grass. It does not smell like vanilla. The vanillin is present in the tissue, but it is bound to a sugar molecule as glucovanillin, and the bound form is not volatile. It cannot reach the nose.

Curing changes that. The pod is killed with heat, then held warm and moist so that its own enzymes can act. A glucosidase breaks the bond between vanillin and glucose, and free vanillin appears. The tissue browns as phenolic compounds oxidise. Water leaves slowly, concentrating what remains. The result is the dark, oily, intensely fragrant bean of commerce.

The chain can be written as a sequence:

green pod → heat treatment stops growth and kills tissue → warm, moist holding lets endogenous enzymes work → glucovanillin is hydrolysed to vanillin and glucose → slow drying concentrates the aromatics and browns the pod → cured bean.

Each arrow is a control point. Too little heat and the tissue stays alive, resisting the cure. Too much heat and the enzymes are destroyed before they can work. Too fast a dry and the centre stays wet while the surface hardens. Too slow a dry and mould moves in. The difference between a great bean and a mediocre one is often a matter of hours and humidity, not a difference in species.

This is why the word "fresh" behaves oddly around vanilla. A fresh pod is not the best pod. A fresh bean, in the trade, means a recently cured one. The raw fruit is an intermediate, not a finished aromatic. The same logic applies to 陈皮 (aged tangerine peel), where aging transforms the peel; vanilla is a New World cousin of that idea, not a Chinese one, but the pattern is familiar to anyone who works with aged plant materials.

## Curing, step by step

**Direct answer:** Traditional curing moves through four overlapping movements — picking, killing, sweating, and drying — and the schedule bends to the weather rather than to a clock. The curer is managing heat, water, and enzyme activity at once.

The pods come off the vine mature but still green. They are sorted by size and condition, because a thick pod and a thin one will not dry at the same rate; damaged pods are diverted before they can spoil the batch. The kill comes next, either as a hot-water dip or as a stay in a heated room. The point is to stop the fruit's own growth and to make the tissue permeable, so that the enzymes are free to act.

Sweating follows. The warm, wet pods are piled in cloth or boxes and kept warm for days. The tissue softens, the colour deepens, and the first real vanilla smell appears as glucovanillin breaks down. The air in a sweating room is heavy and fruity, with a faint alcoholic edge; it is not yet the finished note. Drying is the long tail, often a rhythm of sun by day and shade or indoor warmth at night, with the pods turned so that they lose water evenly. The aim is a supple, dark pod with no wet centre and no hardened shell.

The sequence is not a recipe with fixed hours. A wet season slows the dry; a hot spell can push it too fast. A curer who works by the clock rather than by feel will produce pods that look right and smell flat. The finished pod should bend rather than snap, feel heavy for its size, and smell sweet and resinous with no vinegar edge.

Curing is also where the difference between "vanilla" and "vanilla flavour" begins. A well-cured pod has a complex aroma built from vanillin plus hundreds of other compounds. A poorly cured pod may have vanillin and little else, or vanillin plus off-notes. The label on a bottle rarely tells you which.

## A name younger than the plant

**Direct answer:** 香草荚 is a modern descriptive name, not a term from the old Chinese aromatic canon. The plant is American, the name is recent, and the page does not invent a Chinese antiquity for it.

The Chinese name breaks down cleanly: 香 (fragrant) + 草 (herb) + 荚 (pod). "Fragrant herb pod" is a description, not a botanical classification. The alternative 香草兰 adds 兰, "orchid," which is closer to the truth. Botanically the plant belongs to Orchidaceae; the 草 in the name is descriptive shorthand, not a claim about plant family.

The word 香草 is older and broader in Chinese. It can mean fragrant herbs in general, and it appears in many contexts that have nothing to do with vanilla. 香草荚 is the specific modern compound for the vanilla pod. That specificity matters when reading labels: a product described only as 香草 may be flavoured with vanilla, with a synthetic vanillin, or with something else entirely.

The English word has its own history. "Vanilla" comes from the Spanish *vainilla*, a diminutive of *vaina*, "sheath" or "pod." The name describes the shape, not the scent. It entered European languages after the Spanish encounter with Mesoamerican vanilla, and the plant's cultivation spread through the colonial tropics. None of that history is Chinese, and none of it should be dressed up as Chinese.

This is the provenance point in its plainest form. The plant's wild range is American, running from Mexico into Central America. The name 香草荚 is a modern coinage. Neither the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) nor the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) lists it among the classical 香材. In China it belongs to the modern kitchen and the modern fragrance counter, with a small presence in contemporary incense. A page that claimed otherwise would be inventing a tradition, and this one does not.

## Forms: four commercial objects

**Direct answer:** The pod, the extract, the absolute, and the pure molecule are four different products with different chemistry, dosage, and labelling. The pod is the reference; the other three are derived or synthesised versions of the same idea.

| Form | What it is | Aroma character | Best use | What it is not |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole cured bean | The dried pod | Complete, complex, resinous | Infusion, grating, finishing | Not a concentrated extract |
| Extract | Pod extracted in alcohol and water | Sweet, rounded, familiar | Baking, desserts, tinctures | Not alcohol-free |
| Absolute | Solvent extract for perfumery | Deep, balsamic, less sharp | Fine fragrance, incense accords | Not the same as extract |
| Vanillin | The pure molecule, natural or synthetic | One-dimensional, intense | Industrial flavour, fragrance | Not the whole bean |

The whole bean is the reference because it contains the full aroma, including the minor compounds that give a good pod its depth. Extract captures much of that profile in a liquid, but the solvent and the process change the balance. Absolute is a perfumery material, often darker and heavier than extract, and it behaves differently in a blend. Vanillin is a single molecule: useful, cheap, and incapable of reproducing the whole.

The four are often confused in conversation because all of them can be called "vanilla." In a specification, they are not interchangeable. A recipe that calls for a bean and receives extract will taste sweeter and flatter. A perfume formula that calls for absolute and receives extract may turn cloudy or smell thin. A label that says "vanilla flavour" may contain vanillin and no pod at all.

For incense, the form determines the behaviour. A whole pod is a leathery plant material that chars and smoulders; it contributes body and a slow sweet smoke. An extract brings alcohol and sugar into the mix, which changes ignition and burn. An absolute is a sticky, aromatic resin-like material that doses in small amounts. Vanillin is a crystalline solid that volatilises readily on gentle heat. Each is a different tool.

## Natural and synthetic vanillin

**Direct answer:** "Vanilla" on a label does not say whether the vanillin came from a pod or a factory. Natural and synthetic vanillin are chemically the same molecule, but they arrive through different supply chains, carry different costs, and sit under different labelling rules.

| Question | Natural vanillin | Synthetic vanillin |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Cured pods, or other plant precursors | Guaiacol and similar feedstocks |
| Aroma | Vanillin plus trace companions | Vanillin alone |
| Cost | High, tied to the bean harvest | Low and stable |
| Label | "Natural vanilla" claims depend on market rules | "Vanilla flavour" or "vanillin" |
| Best use | Premium flavour, fine fragrance | Volume flavour, industrial fragrance |
| Risk | Adulteration and mislabelling | Overuse; flat, one-dimensional profile |

The chemistry is identical in the pure compound. The difference is in the company it keeps. A natural vanilla extract contains vanillin plus hundreds of other compounds — phenolic, fruity, floral, woody — that shape the way the sweetness is perceived. Synthetic vanillin is a solo note. In a blind smell test, many people can tell the difference in a custard; in a heavily flavoured product, many cannot.

This is not an argument that natural is always better. It is an argument that the two are separate products with separate economics. A buyer who specifies "vanilla" without specifying the form or the source has left the most important variable open. A formulator who uses synthetic vanillin where a whole-bean infusion was intended will get a cleaner, flatter result and may need to add other materials to restore the missing depth.

The label rules vary by market, and they are not always intuitive. "Natural vanilla flavour" may mean an extract from pods, or a natural vanillin from another plant source, depending on the jurisdiction. "Vanilla flavour" may mean synthetic vanillin with no pod content. The safe reading is to ask for the specification, not to infer from the front of the pack.

## Reading a bean at the bench

**Direct answer:** Judge a bean by feel, smell, and cut surface before you judge it by origin. A plump, supple, deeply fragrant pod with a clean aroma is a good bean; a dry, brittle, or mouldy pod is not rescued by a famous appellation.

| Grade | Feel | Surface | Aroma | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High | Plump, supple, oily | Dark, sheen, maybe crystals | Deep, creamy, complex, clean | Infusion, finishing, premium extract |
| Sound | Supple, slightly thinner | Dark brown, few splits | Clear vanilla, less depth | Everyday baking, extract |
| Working | Firm to dry | Dull, split, some shrinkage | Flat vanilla, faint hay | Sauces, strong-masking recipes |
| Poor | Brittle or wet | Mould, heavy splits | Musty, sour, or smoky | Not recommended |

The first test is physical. A good bean bends without cracking and feels heavy for its size, because it still holds moisture and oil. A bean that snaps like a dry twig has lost the interior that carries the aroma. A bean that feels wet or slippery is a mould risk. The second test is smell: a clean, sweet, rounded note with a resinous edge, not a vinegar or mushroom note. The third is the cut: the interior should be dark, moist, and packed with seeds, not hollow or stringy.

Cut a bean lengthwise and look at the pulp. A well-cured pod is packed with tiny seeds suspended in a dark, oily paste; the cavity should be full, not shrivelled. The seeds themselves carry little aroma, but their presence tells you the pod was harvested mature and cured without collapsing. A hollow or stringy interior is a sign of a pod that dried too fast or was picked before it filled. The cut also shows the colour gradient: dark at the surface, slightly lighter at the centre, with no grey or green patches. Grey suggests mould; green suggests immaturity.

"Vanillin frost" is the white crystalline bloom that sometimes appears on the surface. It is vanillin that has migrated to the surface and crystallised. Some buyers prize it as a sign of richness; others wipe it off as a cosmetic issue. The honest reading is that it is a signal, not a certificate. It suggests a bean with plenty of vanillin and a moisture history that allowed migration. It does not prove the curing was clean, and its absence does not prove the bean is poor.

Origin is the last variable, not the first. Madagascar "Bourbon" vanilla has a long reputation for a reason: the islands' curing tradition and the genetics of the planting material can produce a full, creamy profile. But a badly cured Bourbon bean is still a badly cured bean. A properly cured pod from another origin can beat it. The appellation tells you what to expect; the pod in your hand tells you what you have.

## Where vanilla sits in a blend

**Direct answer:** Vanilla is a base note and a blender. It sits under the brighter materials, rounds their edges, and leaves a sweet, creamy trail. It is not a top note, and it is rarely the centre of a historical Chinese formula because it has no place in that tradition.

In perfumery, vanilla is described as a fixative: a material that slows the evaporation of lighter notes and gives a composition a longer tail. The word is traditional and slightly misleading. Vanilla does not chemically lock other molecules in place. It adds a heavy, slowly evaporating note that persists while the top notes leave, so the overall impression lasts longer and the transition feels smoother.

The chain is perceptual rather than chemical:

vanilla base → low-volatility vanillin lingers on the surface → the sweet note remains after the citrus and florals fade → the composition reads rounder and longer → the perfumer calls the effect "fixing."

In a blend, vanilla pairs naturally with materials that share its warm, sweet register: benzoin, peru balsam, labdanum, and 零陵香, whose sweetness comes from coumarin. It also pairs with woods and resins that can carry its weight without turning cloying. It does not pair well with very dry, austere materials unless the formula wants a deliberate contrast; a little vanilla in a smoky wood blend can read as a soft centre, and too much reads as dessert.

The dose is small. Vanillin is potent, and a blend that smells pleasant on the stick can turn heavy in the burn. In incense, the sweetness is carried by smoke, which concentrates and darkens it. A dose that works in alcohol may be too much in a smouldering cone. Test in the final form, not in the bottle.

Heat changes the balance in a second way. Vanillin is one of the first aroma compounds to leave a warm surface, so a blend that smells restrained at room temperature can turn sharply sweet when burned. The effect is not linear: a small increase in vanillin can shift the whole composition from "warm" to "candy." That is why an incense formula built around vanilla should be tested at the intended dose and in the intended form, and why a perfumer's accord is not automatically a workable incense recipe.

## Three ways to put vanilla into an incense

**Direct answer:** Vanilla reaches a burning blend as ground pod, as a tincture, or as vanillin crystals, and each behaves differently in the cone. The choice is a design decision, not a label detail.

A ground pod brings the whole aroma, but it also brings sugar and fibre. The sugar chars, the fibre smoulders, and the smoke picks up a dark, slightly burnt sweetness. Use it where that roasted edge belongs, and keep the particle size coarse enough that the pod does not clog the mix. A pod ground to flour disappears into the binder and can make the cone burn unevenly.

A tincture carries the aroma in alcohol. The alcohol must leave before the incense is burned, or it will flare and spit. In practice the tincture is mixed into the dry materials and the batch is left until the solvent smell is gone. What remains is the resinous, fruity part of the pod without the fibre. This is the closest thing to a natural vanilla incense, and it is also the least consistent, because the strength depends on the pod and the steep.

Vanillin is the simplest and the most deceptive. A few crystals can perfume a batch; a few too many turn it into candy. The crystals melt early, release a strong sweet note, and leave little residue. That makes vanillin useful as a top-up when a blend needs sweetness at the front, and risky when the formula already has a sweet base. Dose it by weight, in a small trial, never by eye.

The three can be combined, but each addition changes the burn. Pod plus vanillin gives depth and lift; tincture plus vanillin gives a cleaner, more uniform sweetness; all three at once usually overshoots. Change one variable at a time and burn a test cone before scaling the batch.

## A modern guest in the Chinese palette

**Direct answer:** Vanilla entered Chinese use through modern food and fragrance, not through the classical incense tradition. Its presence in Chinese desserts and in some modern incense is real, but it is a recent borrowing, not an inherited 香材.

The modern Chinese palate knows vanilla through baking, ice cream, custard, and the broader global dessert vocabulary. It is a familiar sweet note, and it sits comfortably beside dairy, caramel, chocolate, and fruit. In fragrance, it appears in gourmand compositions and in the sweet base of many consumer products. In incense, it appears in modern and Western-style blends, often as an extract or as vanillin.

None of that makes it classical. The *Xiangcheng* and the *Bencao Gangmu* belong to a different materia medica, built on native and naturalised plants with their own symbolic and therapeutic language. Vanilla is not in that language. A page that placed it there would be committing a category error, and a formulator who treated it as a traditional material would be borrowing authority the plant does not have.

The honest position is also the more interesting one. Vanilla is a case study in how a material enters a tradition from outside: first as a novelty, then as a flavour, then as a habit. The Chinese sweet palate has absorbed it without asking for an old pedigree. That is normal for food. It is only in the incense world, where provenance carries so much weight, that the absence of an older record feels like a defect. It is not a defect; it is a fact about the material's history.

## What the label can and cannot tell you

**Direct answer:** A label can tell you the form, the origin, and sometimes the species; it usually cannot tell you the curing quality, the vanillin content, or whether the pod is spent. Those are bench judgments, not label judgments.

The useful label information is concrete. "Whole bean" tells you the form. "Madagascar" or "Bourbon" tells you a tradition and a region. "Extract" tells you a solvent was used. "Absolute" tells you a perfumery-grade concentrate. "Vanillin" tells you the product is a molecule, not a pod. Those words narrow the field.

The unhelpful label information is vague. "Premium" and "gourmet" are marketing words. "Natural" is regulated differently in different markets and does not tell you whether the vanillin came from a pod. A country of origin does not tell you the harvest year or the curing method. A long list of dessert words on the front does not tell you the concentration.

The species is worth knowing. Most of the world's commercial vanilla is *Vanilla planifolia*. Tahitian vanilla comes from a different species, *Vanilla tahitensis*, and reads fruitier and more floral. If a seller cannot name the species, that is a sign the specification is thin. If the price is far below the market for the stated form, that is a sign the form or the origin may be misstated.

The most important thing a label cannot tell you is whether the bean was cured well. Curing is a craft process, and its results are visible only in the pod. That is why a sample is worth more than a certificate. Ask for a photo of the actual lot, a smell description from the seller, and a small sample if the order is large. The nose settles what the label cannot.

One trade practice deserves a plain warning. Pods that have already been extracted can be dried and sold as whole beans. They look plausible and smell faintly sweet, but the aroma is mostly gone. There is no reliable visual test; the give-away is a flat, hay-like smell and a dry, light feel. Vanilla paste is a different case: it is a legitimate product made from seeds and extract, useful in baking, but it is not a substitute for a whole bean in a perfume or an incense formula. The label should say which object you are buying.

## Storage and decline

**Direct answer:** Keep beans cool, dark, and sealed; keep extract and absolute tightly closed; keep vanillin dry. Vanilla declines through moisture loss, oxidation, and mould, and the decline is faster in heat and light.

Whole beans are the most stable form, but they are not immortal. Over time they lose moisture, shrink, and harden. The aroma does not vanish at once; it flattens, loses the creamy middle, and turns more resinous and less sweet. A bean that was supple becomes brittle. A bean that was moist can grow mould if the container traps condensation.

The decline chain runs like this:

moisture migrates inside the container → the surface stays damp → mould spores germinate → off-notes develop → the sweet note turns sour or musty → the bean is unusable for aroma work.

The prevention is boring and effective. Use a small airtight container, keep it out of the light, and avoid warm shelves. Do not store beans in the refrigerator unless they are sealed against condensation. Do not put a wet spoon into a jar of extract. Do not open the container more often than necessary.

Extract and absolute have their own clocks. Extract loses alcohol over time and can turn cloudy; absolute can thicken and darken. Neither spoils as visibly as a bean, but both lose definition. Vanillin is stable if kept dry and sealed, though it can cake in humidity.

The practical rule is to buy the amount you will use within a reasonable period. A large jar of beans is not a bargain if half of it hardens before it is used. The same logic applies to extract: a small bottle that is opened often is better than a large one that sits.

## What the evidence supports

**Direct answer:** Four things are settled: the species, its place in Orchidaceae, the curing chemistry, and its modern role in flavour and fragrance. The old Chinese record is absent, and no therapeutic claim is offered.

| Layer | Status | What it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established | GBIF and standard orchid taxonomy |
| Curing chemistry | Established | Glucovanillin hydrolysis during curing |
| Modern flavour use | Documented | Global food and fragrance practice |
| Classical Chinese use | None | Absent from the classical 香材 canon |
| Medical claims | Not established | No clinical evidence presented |

The curing chemistry is the strongest technical layer. Glucovanillin is odourless; the enzyme-mediated release of vanillin is what makes a cured pod smell like vanilla. That sequence is taught in food science and is consistent with the practice of every vanilla-growing region. The details of temperature and duration vary, but the principle does not.

The historical layer is equally clear, in the opposite direction. Vanilla is not in the *Xiangcheng* or the *Bencao Gangmu*. Its Chinese use is modern. That is not a gap in the page; it is the finding. A reader who wants a historical Chinese aromatic should look elsewhere. A reader who wants to understand a modern material will find the useful facts here.

No therapeutic or clinical claim is made here. Vanilla has a folk reputation as an aphrodisiac and a comfort scent; that reputation is cultural, not clinical. A pleasant smell is pleasant; that is not a medical effect and should not be sold as one.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Burning plant material releases fine particles into the air; work in a room with a cross-flow of fresh air and keep your face out of the plume. Vanilla is a food material, but that says nothing about the safety of its smoke or about putting its oil on skin neat.

The safety profile of vanilla as a flavour is well documented. The safety profile of vanilla smoke is simply the safety profile of burning biomass: fine particles, carbon monoxide where air is still, and irritants. A pleasant smell does not make smoke clean. Airflow and restraint are the controls.

Vanilla extract usually contains alcohol and should be kept away from open flames. Adding it to a burning mixture can cause flaring, and a bottle of extract near a heat source is a fire risk. If an extract is used in incense, it should be incorporated into the material and allowed to dry before burning, not poured onto a live coal.

Vanilla absolute is a concentrated perfumery material. It is sticky and can sensitise sensitive skin; handle it with gloves if you are formulating, and do not apply it neat. Vanillin powder is a fine crystalline solid that can irritate eyes and airways; keep it away from the face when weighing.

Keep aromatic materials and finished incense out of reach of children and animals. Nothing in this section is clinical guidance; it is handling advice for a combustible aromatic. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general notes.

## FAQ

**Direct answer:** These questions settle the identity, the provenance, the curing chemistry, the frost on the pod, and the difference between the four commercial forms.

**Is vanilla a spice or a flower?** Neither, strictly. The material is the cured fruit pod of an orchid. The flower is the source of the pod, but the flower itself is not the spice. "Vanilla bean" is the pod; "vanilla flower" is a different thing, and the orchid's ornamental value is separate from its culinary value.

**Does 香草荚 belong to the old Chinese incense tradition?** No. The plant is American in origin, and the Chinese name is a recent description rather than a historical term. Neither the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) nor the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) lists it among the old 香材. In China it belongs to modern flavour work, modern fragrance, and some contemporary incense; the page claims no older lineage.

**What is vanillin frost on a vanilla bean?** It is vanillin that has migrated to the surface and crystallised, often appearing as a fine white bloom. It suggests a bean with a good vanillin content and a particular moisture history. It is a signal of quality, not a guarantee: a properly cured pod can lack it, and a poorly stored pod can carry it.

**Is vanilla absolute the same as vanilla extract?** No. Extract is a flavouring made by steeping or percolating pods in alcohol and water. Absolute is a perfumery concentrate made with solvent extraction, and it is darker, heavier, and dosed very differently. They are not interchangeable in a formula, and one can ruin a blend that was built for the other.

**How should I store vanilla beans?** In a small airtight container, in a cool, dark place, away from heat and moisture. Do not refrigerate unless the container is sealed against condensation. Use within a reasonable period; a bean that has hardened and lost its creamy note will not recover.

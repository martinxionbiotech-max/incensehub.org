> **Direct answer:** Arborvitae leaves (侧柏叶, cèbǎi yè) are the flat, scale-like leafy branchlets of *Platycladus orientalis* (侧柏), a cypress-family tree native to China and Korea. They come from the same tree that yields cypress seed (柏子), but they are a different part with a different role: the leaf is a fresh, green, slightly resinous material used chiefly in materia medica and as a quiet accent, while the seed is the classical incense material of 柏子香.

## One Name, One Part: What 侧柏叶 Actually Is

**Direct answer:** 侧柏叶 is the foliage of the oriental arborvitae — the flat, fan-like sprays of scale leaves, gathered and dried. It is not a flower, not a fruit, not a resin, and not the seed that shares the same tree.

The plant is an evergreen conifer in the cypress family, widely planted in China and Korea for ornament, windbreaks, and timber. Its foliage grows in flattened vertical planes rather than in the radial sprays of many other conifers, which gives the branch a distinctly two-sided look. That growth habit is the origin of the name, and it is also the easiest way to recognize the material in a market: the dried branchlets keep a flattened, pressed appearance even after packing.

The part distinction matters because the tree is more famous for its seed. 柏子 — the seed, sometimes called 柏子仁 — is the material of 柏子香, a classical scholar's incense built on roasted seed. The leaf and the seed are sold separately, prepared differently, and smell different. A buyer who orders "arborvitae" without naming the part can receive either.

**An editorial point:** the leaf is not a lesser version of the seed. It is a different material with its own uses, and judging it by the seed's standards — roasted warmth, nutty depth — guarantees disappointment. Judge it as foliage: fresh, green, quiet, and best used where a conifer note should sit behind something else.

## The Naming Logic: Why "Sideways Cypress"

**Direct answer:** 侧柏 means "sideways cypress," and the name describes the leaf's growth in flat, side-oriented planes. The *Bencao Gangmu* explains it directly: only the flat, sideways-growing leaves are taken for use, hence 侧柏.

The characters are transparent once you see them: 侧 (cè) means side or sideways, and 柏 (bǎi) is the cypress/arborvitae character used across a family of conifers. The compound is not a species name in the modern botanical sense. It is a description of a growth form that also functions as a name.

The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), in its tree section (木之一), records the tree under 柏 and explains: "入药惟取叶扁而侧生者，故曰侧柏" — for medicinal use only the flat, sideways-growing leaves are taken, and that is why it is called 侧柏. The line does two jobs at once. It identifies the part, and it tells the reader that not every leaf on the tree is the material.

That second job is the practical one. A dried sample of arborvitae foliage can include stem, older scale leaves, and the pale inner growth. The classical instruction selects the flat, lateral sprays, which are the part with the strongest green note and the cleanest appearance. Whether a modern supplier follows that selection is a quality question, and it is visible in the bag: a high proportion of brown, woody stem means the selection was loose.

### The 柏 category problem

柏 is a category marker, not a species. It appears in 侧柏, 崖柏, 柏子, and 扁柏, and those names refer to different plants or different parts. A reader who treats 柏 as a guarantee of identity will eventually buy the wrong material. The [thuja](/ingredients/thuja/) and [cedar](/ingredients/cedar/) entries show how far the category stretches: the English word "cedar" alone covers several genera, and Chinese 柏 names are no tidier.

The useful habit is to ask for the binomial. *Platycladus orientalis* is unambiguous. 侧柏 is nearly unambiguous when the part is stated. "柏树叶" is a description that could belong to several trees.

## Same Tree, Two Materials: Leaf and Seed

**Direct answer:** The leaf and the seed come from one species but are different materials in every practical sense — appearance, aroma, preparation, classical use, and price. This site keeps them as separate entries because a knowledge platform records parts, not just plants.

| | Arborvitae leaves (侧柏叶) | Cypress seed (柏子) |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical source | *Platycladus orientalis* — leafy branchlets | *Platycladus orientalis* — seed |
| Appearance | Flat green sprays, scale leaves, thin stem | Small oval seeds, woody, brown |
| Aroma | Fresh, green, slightly resinous | Roasted, nutty, woody |
| Preparation | Gathered and dried | Scalded, soaked in wine and honey, dried, then roasted |
| Classical role | Materia medica | The material of 柏子香 |
| Use in a blend | Quiet green accent | Structural roasted base note |

The preparation difference is the one readers underestimate. Fresh arborvitae foliage is dried, and that is the whole process. The seed goes through a multi-step preparation that changes its chemistry and its smell: scalding, soaking in wine and honey, drying, and roasting. The two materials are not interchangeable at any stage, and a recipe that calls for 柏子 cannot be made with 侧柏叶 just because the tree is the same.

**Mechanism:** fresh seed → scalding sets the interior → wine-and-honey soak introduces sugars and aromatics → drying removes water → roasting drives Maillard-type browning → nutty, toasted notes form. The leaf never enters that chain, which is why it never develops the roasted character.

That is the clearest illustration on this site of a general rule: **when one plant yields several aromatic parts, "the plant" is not a specific enough answer for sourcing.** The part is the material.

## Aroma: Green, Resinous, Quiet

**Direct answer:** 侧柏叶 smells fresh, woody, and green with a slight resinous edge — the scent of crushed conifer foliage. It is lighter and greener than the seed, and it reads as a background note rather than a lead.

The opening is the smell of a hedge after rain: green, slightly sharp, with a resinous undertone that becomes more visible as the leaf is crushed. There is a faint camphoraceous quality in some samples, though far less than in borneol or camphor. The overall impression is cool rather than warm, which places it at the opposite end of the conifer register from the roasted seed.

In a burn, the leaf behaves like a dried herb rather than a resin:

- **Opening.** The green note comes off quickly, sometimes with a slightly sharp edge as the leaf catches.
- **Middle.** A soft woody-conifer body, thin compared with a resin or a heartwood.
- **Finish.** Little persistence. The note fades rather than settling, which is why the leaf is used as an accent and not as a base.

That short arc is the material's limitation and its use. A note that disappears is a poor foundation and an excellent garnish. In a sachet, where there is no combustion and the volatiles release slowly at room temperature, the green note lasts much longer than it does on a coal — a practical point that matters for anyone using the leaf outside incense.

The aroma also changes with age in a way that is easy to miss. Fresh dried leaf is green and bright; a bag that has been open for a season smells like hay, dust, and weak tea. The shift is not a matter of strength. The characteristic green molecules are volatile and oxidative, and once they are gone, no amount of extra leaf brings them back.

## Botany and Range

**Direct answer:** *Platycladus orientalis* is a cypress-family evergreen native to China and Korea, widely cultivated well beyond its native range. It is a long-lived, drought-tolerant tree, and its foliage is the part used here.

The species is placed in Cupressaceae, the cypress family, and is the sole member of its genus in most current treatments. Older literature used the names *Thuja orientalis* and *Biota orientalis*, which is one reason a reader may find the same plant under three genera in different books. The accepted name today is *Platycladus orientalis*.

Its native range is usually given as China and Korea, with cultivation spreading much further through temperate Asia and into gardens elsewhere. The tree tolerates poor soil and dry conditions, which is part of why it appears in temple grounds, cemeteries, and domestic courtyards — settings where it acquired symbolic associations that outlast any particular use of the leaf.

For identification, the foliage is the giveaway: flattened sprays, scale-like leaves pressed close to the stem, and a distinct resinous smell when crushed. The seed cones are small, blue-green when young, and woody when mature. A reader comparing dried material should look for the flat spray structure; a bag of loose, needle-like fragments is either a different conifer or a heavily processed product.

The botanical reference is [*Platycladus orientalis* in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200005428), with occurrence data at [GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/2684855).

## Harvest, Drying, and the Green Note

**Direct answer:** The leaf is gathered and dried, and the drying method decides whether the green note survives. Slow drying in shade preserves it; fast heat drives it off; light and air finish the job over months of storage.

Drying is the whole processing story for this material, which makes it unusually easy to judge and unusually easy to ruin. The volatile fraction that carries the green note is light and reactive. Heat removes it quickly. Sunlight accelerates the breakdown of chlorophyll and the oxidation of the aromatic compounds. Air movement helps drying but also carries volatiles away.

**Mechanism:** fresh foliage → high moisture → slow drying at ambient temperature → enzymes and oxygen remain active longer → chlorophyll degrades and green volatiles oxidize → hay-like, dusty aroma. The same leaf dried fast in shade → moisture leaves before the volatiles do → green note retained → bright, conifer smell.

That is why "shade-dried" is a meaningful phrase in a supplier's description, and why "sun-dried" is not automatically a compliment for a leafy aromatic. Sun drying is fast and cheap; for a leaf whose value is its fresh green note, fast is not the same as good. The exception is a climate so humid that shade drying invites mold — in which case a gentle artificial heat is the lesser evil, and the result is still better than a spoiled batch.

Storage continues the process. A well-dried leaf packed in an opaque, sealed container holds its note for a season or so. A clear bag on a sunny shelf loses it in weeks. The practical test at purchase is simple: open the bag and smell. A green, resinous lift means the drying and storage were adequate. A hay-like or dusty smell means the material is old, and no blending trick will restore it.

## Harvest Timing and the Seasonal Question

**Direct answer:** The leaf can be gathered across the growing season, but young growth and older growth are not the same material. Young sprays are softer, greener, and more aromatic; older foliage is woodier, duller, and carries more stem.

The practical decision is about proportion, not a calendar date. A spring flush gives soft, thin sprays with a high moisture content and a bright note. Foliage cut later in the year has more lignified stem, a lower proportion of green tissue, and a quieter smell. Neither is wrong. They dry differently and they blend differently, and a supplier who can say which growth was taken is describing the material more precisely than one who gives only a month.

Three handling rules matter more than timing:

- **Gather dry.** Foliage collected wet, or before the dew has lifted, carries surface water into the drying bed and invites mold.
- **Dry promptly.** A sack of fresh foliage is a living, respiring mass. Left packed, it heats, sweats, and develops a musty note before it ever reaches a drying rack.
- **Do not strip the branch.** Removing all the foliage from a branch damages the tree and removes the structure that makes the sprays easy to handle and dry evenly.

**Mechanism:** wet foliage → packed in a closed sack → respiration raises temperature and humidity → condensation on the leaves → mold spores germinate → musty, sour note that survives drying. The defect is created in the first few hours, not in the drying room, which is why the fix is at harvest and not at the oven.

The same logic explains why a small grower can outperform a large one on this material. The window between cutting and drying is short, and it rewards attention more than equipment.

## Quality Grading for a Leaf

**Direct answer:** Leaf quality turns on four things — correct part, freshness, cleanliness, and species clarity. There is no formal grading ladder, so the table below is an editorial aid rather than a trade standard.

| Grade (editorial) | Appearance | Aroma | Stem and foreign matter | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh top-grade | Even green, flat sprays intact, not shattered | Bright green, resinous, clear | Minimal stem; no dust | Sachet, accent blending |
| Sound commercial | Mostly green, some brown edges | Green but quieter | Some stem; little dust | General blending |
| Faded | Yellow-green to brown, brittle | Hay, dust, weak tea | High stem proportion | Not for aroma-led use |
| Contaminated | Dull, patchy, visible mold or grit | Musty, sour | Grit, fiber, other leaves | Reject |

Three notes on reading the table. First, color is a proxy, not a proof: a leaf can be green and still smell flat if it was dried hot and stored badly. Second, "intact" is relative — a properly dried leaf is brittle enough to crumble, so the test is whether it shatters into dust or breaks into recognizable pieces. Third, the bottom row is a rejection, not a discount grade. Mold in a dried herb is not something to blend around.

### The part-identity check

Before grading quality, confirm the part. A bag sold as 侧柏叶 should be dominated by flat, scale-leaf sprays. If it is mostly small oval seeds, it is 柏子 — a different material with a different price and a different use. If it is coarse, needle-like, or resinous in a sticky way, it may be another conifer.

The check takes a minute: spread a handful on white paper and look at the silhouette. Flat sprays read as small fern-like fronds. Seeds read as discrete oval bodies. Stem reads as thin woody lines. The proportions tell you what you bought.

## Why the Leaf Is Not a Classical Incense Lead

**Direct answer:** The classical fame of *Platycladus orientalis* belongs to its seed. 柏子香 is built on roasted 柏子, and the leaf appears in the record chiefly as a materia-medica part rather than as the aromatic heart of a formula.

This is a case where the historical record is unusually clear. The tree is named in the materia medica, the leaf is identified as the medicinal part, and the seed is treated as a separate substance with its own preparation. The incense tradition that grew around the tree — the scholar's 柏子香 — used the seed.

The reason is probably sensory. A roasted seed has depth, sweetness, and persistence; a dried leaf has a bright green top note and almost no base. A formula built on the leaf alone would burn thin and fade quickly. The leaf works as a modifier, not as a foundation.

**A boundary worth stating:** "not a classical lead" does not mean "not used." The leaf appears in sachets, in blended incenses as a green accent, and in craft and ritual use where the foliage itself is the point. The distinction is between a material that carries a tradition and a material that participates in one. The leaf participates.

That distinction also protects against a common marketing move: selling the leaf at a premium by association with the seed. The association is real — same tree — but the materials are not equivalent, and the price should reflect the part.

## Where the Leaf Does Earn a Place

**Direct answer:** The leaf earns its place in sachets, as a green accent in blends, in seasonal and symbolic use, and as a botanical study material. It is a supporting player with a distinctive note, not a substitute for the seed.

**In sachets.** Without combustion, the green note releases slowly and lasts far longer than it does on a coal. A simple sachet of dried leaf, perhaps with a little cedar or cinnamon bark, is one of the most direct uses of the material.

**As an accent.** A small proportion of leaf can add a fresh conifer edge to a blend that would otherwise be sweet and heavy. The dose is small by necessity: too much and the burn turns sharp and grassy.

**In seasonal and symbolic use.** The tree's associations — endurance, mourning, temple grounds — give the foliage a role in seasonal arrangements and ritual settings that has little to do with aroma chemistry. That use is cultural, and it is worth naming as such rather than dressing it as pharmacology.

**As a study material.** For a reader learning to distinguish parts of one plant, the leaf-and-seed pair is an unusually clean example. The same tree, two materials, two preparations, two sensory profiles. Once you have handled both, the abstraction "same plant, different part" becomes concrete.

**What it is not good for:** a long-burning base, a resinous fixative, or a substitute for 柏子 in a classical recipe. Those jobs belong to other materials.

## Confusion Matrix: The 柏-Named Materials

**Direct answer:** Several materials carry 柏 in their names, and they are not interchangeable. The matrix below separates the ones a reader is most likely to meet.

| Name | Botanical identity | Part | Aroma | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 侧柏叶 | *Platycladus orientalis* | Leafy branchlets | Green, woody, resinous | This entry |
| 柏子 / 柏子仁 | *Platycladus orientalis* | Seed | Roasted, nutty | Classical incense material |
| 崖柏 | *Thuja sutchuenensis* (and loose trade use) | Wood | Resinous, sweet-woody | Different genus; trade name is unstable |
| 扁柏 | Often *Chamaecyparis* or *Platycladus*, by region | Wood or foliage | Varies | Regional name; verify binomial |
| Cedar (English) | Several genera, e.g. *Cedrus*, *Juniperus* | Wood | Varies widely | A category, not a species |

Two cautions. First, 崖柏 is a name with a conservation dimension: *Thuja sutchuenensis* is a restricted species, and the trade name has been applied loosely to other materials, including deadwood of various conifers. A reader buying "崖柏" should ask for the binomial and for documentation, not just the Chinese name. Second, "cedar" in English is a category word that spans true cedars, junipers, and cypress relatives; the [cedar](/ingredients/cedar/) entry deals with that problem directly.

The general lesson is the one this whole page keeps returning to: **trade names describe markets, binomials describe plants.** When a name is doing commercial work, the binomial is the only reliable anchor.

## From Loose Leaf to Blend: Three Preparations

**Direct answer:** Whole dried sprays, cut leaf, and powdered leaf are three different products with three different shelf lives. The more the material is broken up, the faster the green note leaves it.

| Form | Aroma retention | Best use | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried sprays | Longest | Sachet, display, identification | Bulky; difficult to blend evenly |
| Cut leaf | Moderate | General blending, larger batches | Loses the green note within months of opening |
| Powder | Shortest | Even dispersion in a fine blend | Surface area accelerates oxidation; dust |

The pattern is surface area. A whole spray exposes a small fraction of its tissue to air; a powder exposes nearly all of it. The same reaction that releases aroma when you crush a leaf in your hand also runs continuously in a bag of powder, and it does not stop at the point you would choose.

**Mechanism:** grinding → surface area rises sharply → oxygen and light reach more tissue → green volatiles oxidize → the bright note flattens → what remains is a woody, hay-like base. The powder has not lost its mass; it has lost the part that made it interesting.

For a reader, the implication is to buy the form that matches the use. A sachet wants whole or coarsely cut leaf. A blend that needs the note dispersed evenly can use powder, but should be made close to the time of use. Storing powder for a year and expecting it to perform like fresh leaf is a common and quiet disappointment.

## The Temple Tree: Symbolism and Setting

**Direct answer:** The arborvitae carries cultural associations that have nothing to do with its aroma — endurance, mourning, temple grounds, and the evergreen that does not shed. Those associations shape demand, and they are worth naming as culture rather than as chemistry.

The tree is long-lived, tolerant of poor soil, and evergreen, which made it a natural planting in temple courtyards, cemeteries, and formal gardens. In Chinese literary culture, the cypress and arborvitae register carries connotations of steadfastness and endurance, and the tree appears in poetry and painting as a marker of place and permanence.

That symbolic load does two things in the market. It supports demand for foliage in seasonal and ritual settings, where the material is valued for what it represents as much as for how it smells. It also creates a temptation to dress cultural use as therapeutic use — to move from "this tree stands in temple grounds" to "this leaf does something to the body." The first is cultural history. The second requires evidence the tradition does not supply.

**An editorial boundary:** respecting a tradition means describing it accurately, including its symbolic register. It does not mean converting symbolism into a health claim. A reader can value the material's cultural place and still treat any medicinal statement about it as traditional belief rather than established fact.

## The Materia Medica Record

**Direct answer:** 侧柏叶 appears in the classical Chinese materia medica, and the *Bencao Gangmu* records the tree under 柏 with the naming explanation that also identifies the part. That record is historical and traditional.

What the record contains, stated carefully:

- **The naming passage.** "入药惟取叶扁而侧生者，故曰侧柏" — only the flat, sideways-growing leaves are taken for use, hence the name. This is a documentary fact about what the text says.
- **The part distinction.** The same section treats the seed (柏实/柏子仁) as a separate materia-medica substance, which supports the part-level reading used throughout this page.
- **Traditional applications.** The leaf is listed among materia-medica materials with traditional uses. Those uses are **traditional belief and historical practice**, not clinical evidence, and this page makes no medical claims about them.

The distinction between "the text records X" and "X is true" is the whole discipline of reading historical materia medica. A classical text is excellent evidence for what was believed, traded, and used. It is not a modern trial, and it should not be cited as one.

**Editorial position:** the materia-medica record is worth preserving as cultural and botanical history. It is not a prescribing guide. A reader who wants medical guidance should consult a qualified practitioner, and a reader who wants incense guidance should judge the leaf by its aroma and its behavior on heat.

## Green Conifers Compared

**Direct answer:** Green conifer materials share a register but differ in sharpness, sweetness, and persistence. Placing 侧柏叶 beside pine, cedar, juniper, and thuja shows what is distinctive about it — and why it rarely leads a blend.

| Material | Part used | Aroma register | Burn behavior | Best role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 侧柏叶 (arborvitae leaf) | Leafy branchlets | Green, woody, slightly resinous | Quick, thin, short finish | Accent, sachet |
| Pine needles (松针) | Needles | Sharp, resinous, citrus-pine | Fast, bright, little base | Top-note accent |
| Cedar (雪松) | Wood | Dry, sweet-woody, pencil-like | Steady, moderate persistence | Base and structure |
| Juniper (杜松) | Berry and wood | Gin-like, sharp, resinous | Spicy, quick, drying | Middle accent |
| Thuja (崖柏) | Wood | Resinous, sweet, sometimes sour | Heavy, smoky, persistent | Base, small dose |

The pattern is that the leaf materials sit at the fast end and the wood materials at the slow end. A blend that needs a green opening draws from the first group; a blend that needs duration draws from the second. Using a leaf where a wood is required produces a stick that smells promising for a minute and empty for the rest of the burn.

There is a second pattern in the sharpness. Pine and juniper carry a volatile sharpness that reads as "fresh" in small doses and as "cleaning product" in large ones. Arborvitae leaf is softer than either, which makes it easier to use as a background modifier and harder to use as a statement. Cedar and thuja are sweeter and heavier, which is why they anchor formulas rather than garnish them.

**A practical test:** burn a small sample of each on separate charcoals, in sequence, and note where the smell sits in the room — near the burner or across it. Leaf materials tend to stay close and fade; woods fill the space and linger. That spatial impression is a better guide to role than any adjective list.

## Storage and the Freshness Clock

**Direct answer:** A well-dried leaf holds its green note for roughly a season under good storage, and loses it within weeks under bad storage. Light, heat, air, and moisture are the four variables, and all four are under the keeper's control.

**Light.** Chlorophyll and many aromatic compounds break down under ultraviolet and visible light. An opaque container is better than a clear one, and a closed cupboard is better than a sunny shelf. This is the single most common storage error for leafy aromatics, because a clear jar looks attractive and quietly destroys the contents.

**Heat.** Warmth accelerates every reaction that degrades the leaf, including oxidation. A cupboard away from the stove, the radiator, and the window is enough. There is no need for refrigeration, and there is a positive reason to avoid a domestic freezer: every time the container is opened, moisture condenses on the cold leaf, and the freeze-thaw cycle does more damage than the cool temperature prevents.

**Air.** Oxygen is the reagent in the browning and oxidation reactions. A full, tightly closed container with little headspace slows the process; a half-empty bag rolled at the top does not. Decant a small working amount and keep the bulk sealed.

**Moisture.** Dried leaf is hygroscopic. In a humid climate, a desiccant sachet inside a sealed container helps, provided the container is actually sealed. A desiccant in a permeable bag is a gesture. In a dry climate, the risk runs the other way: the leaf becomes brittle and turns to dust when handled.

**Mechanism:** repeated opening → humid air enters the container → leaf moisture rises and falls with the weather → oxidation and enzyme activity resume → chlorophyll degrades and green volatiles are lost → hay-like, dusty aroma. The sequence is slow enough to be invisible day to day and fast enough to matter within a season.

The practical consequence is a buying rule: buy the quantity you will use in the period the material holds its note, not the quantity that gets the best unit price. A bargain bag that is stale by the time it is opened is not a bargain. Ask for a harvest or packing date; if none is offered, buy small and smell before committing to more.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The safety considerations are the ordinary ones for burning plant material, plus the caution that a dried herb is not a food or a medicine.

- **Smoke and particulates.** Incense combustion produces smoke and fine particles. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep the burner away from children and pets.
- **Mold.** Dried leaf that has been stored damp can carry mold. Discard musty material rather than blending it; the smell is a warning, not a flavor.
- **Sensitization.** Conifer volatiles irritate some people. If handling the dried leaf causes skin or respiratory irritation, stop handling it.
- **Not for ingestion.** This page describes an aromatic and materia-medica material. It is not a food ingredient, and nothing here is a recommendation to ingest it.
- **Fire safety.** Dried leaf catches quickly. Keep it away from open flame except in a proper burner, and never leave a burning preparation unattended.

The site's [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case for incense combustion.

## FAQ

**What is arborvitae leaves (侧柏叶)?** The flat, scale-like leafy branchlets of *Platycladus orientalis*, the oriental arborvitae, a cypress-family tree native to China and Korea. It is the leafy part of the same tree that yields cypress seed, but a distinct material with its own aroma and its own traditional use.

**Is arborvitae leaves the same as cypress seed (柏子)?** No. They come from the same tree but are different parts. 侧柏叶 is the foliage: green, fresh, and dried. 柏子 is the seed: prepared by scalding, soaking, drying, and roasting, and used as the material of 柏子香. They differ in appearance, aroma, preparation, and classical role, and they do not substitute for each other.

**Why is it called sideways cypress (侧柏)?** The *Bencao Gangmu* explains that only the flat, sideways-growing leaves are taken for use, and that this is why the tree is called 侧柏. The name describes the orientation of the foliage sprays, not the location or habit of the tree.

**What does 侧柏叶 smell like?** Fresh, woody, and green with a slightly resinous edge — the smell of crushed conifer foliage. It is lighter and greener than the roasted seed, with a short burn arc: the green note opens quickly and fades rather than settling into a base.

**Is 侧柏叶 a classical incense ingredient?** Not as a lead material. The classical incense tradition around this tree is 柏子香, built on the roasted seed. The leaf appears chiefly as a materia-medica part and as a green accent in blends and sachets.

**How is 侧柏叶 different from pine needles?** Both are green conifer foliage, but pine needles are sharper, more resinous, and more citrus-like, while arborvitae leaf is softer, woodier, and quieter. Pine reads as a top note; 侧柏叶 reads as a background modifier. They are different genera, and they are not interchangeable in a formula even though both belong to the same broad aromatic register.

**How do I buy the right part?** Name the part and the species: 侧柏叶 of *Platycladus orientalis* for the foliage, or 柏子 for the seed. Then check freshness — a bright green, resinous smell for the leaf — and inspect the bag for stem, dust, and mold. If the binomial is not stated, ask for it.

## Summary

Arborvitae leaves (侧柏叶) are the flat, sideways-growing foliage of *Platycladus orientalis*, the oriental arborvitae — the same tree that yields the classical incense seed 柏子, but a different part with a different role. The *Bencao Gangmu* records the tree under 柏 and explains the name from the leaf's growth, while treating the seed as a separate materia-medica substance. The leaf is fresh, green, and slightly resinous, with a short burn arc that suits sachets and accent work rather than a lead role. Its quality is a matter of part identity and freshness, and its medicinal record is traditional rather than clinical.

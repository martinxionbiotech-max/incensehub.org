> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 (yòupí) is what you get when a pomelo — *Citrus maxima*, the big Rutaceae fruit grown across southern China and native to Southeast Asia — is peeled and the skin dried. In the censer it reads as citrus with a bitter, floral-woody undertone, and the thick white inner layer is the reason it reads that way. It is a different material from 陈皮, the aged mandarin peel.

## What 柚皮 is — and why the pith is the point

> **Direct answer:** It is the whole peel of a pomelo, cut from the fruit and dried, with the thick white inner layer left on. That inner layer is not trimmable waste; it is the source of the bitterness that makes the material distinct from every other citrus peel.

A citrus peel is not one substance. It is two tissues with different jobs. The outer coloured layer — the flavedo — holds the oil glands, and pressing it releases the bright, volatile citrus note everyone recognises. The inner white layer — the albedo, or pith — is spongy, nearly odourless when intact, and bitter when chewed. A pomelo builds an unusually heavy albedo, and that proportion is what separates 柚皮 from a thin-skinned mandarin peel.

Most perfume-facing writing treats pith as a defect: something to be avoided, trimmed, or apologised for. That instinct makes sense when the goal is a clean rind oil. It is the wrong instinct here. 柚皮 is a whole-peel material, and its identity is the tension between the two tissues — bright oil above, soft bitterness below. Strip the pith and you have a generic citrus peel with a different name and a higher price.

The distinction has a practical edge. Two lots can carry the same species name and behave nothing alike: one cut thick with the pith intact, one shaved thin and mostly rind. The first reads rounder, softer and more bitter; the second reads sharper and more obviously citrus. Neither is wrong, but they are not the same material, and a formula built around one will not behave the same way with the other.

## The pith decision: three specifications

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel is sold at three broad pith specifications — full pith, half-trimmed and rind-only — and the choice changes the aroma more than the species does. A buyer who has not specified the pith has not specified the material.

| Specification | What the sample looks like | Aroma effect | Best for | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full pith | Thick white backing, rind visible only on one face | Soft, round, distinctly bitter; citrus muted | Warm spice blends, tea-style compositions | Holds water; needs careful drying |
| Half-trimmed | Some white left, rind clearly dominant | Balanced: citrus forward, bitter finish | General blending, household scent | Inconsistent from piece to piece |
| Rind only | Thin, coloured, little or no white | Sharp, bright, close to grapefruit zest | Bright top notes, citrus-led blends | Loses the material's defining character |

The first row is the one that causes the most argument. A maker trained on perfumery materials expects peel to mean rind, because that is where the oil is. In that frame, a thick white backing looks like careless processing. For 柚皮 the frame is wrong: the pith is the reason the material has a name at all, and a rind-only lot is closer to a generic citrus zest than to the peel the formula called for.

The drying consequence is easy to miss. Pith is spongy and holds water, so a full-pith sheet dries far more slowly than a shaved one. A supplier who shaves the peel may be doing it for drying speed as much as for aroma, and a buyer who receives a suspiciously bright, thin lot should ask which decision produced it.

There is no universal right answer. A tea-style blend that wants a soft, bitter backbone is better served by full pith. A bright summer blend may want the shaved version. What fails is ambiguity: ordering "pomelo peel" and receiving whichever specification the supplier had on hand, then building a formula around behaviour that will not repeat on the next order.

## The fruit behind the peel

> **Direct answer:** The pomelo tree carries broadly ovate to elliptic leaves measuring 9–16 cm by 4–8 cm on a winged stalk, and bears a pale-yellow or green-tinged fruit, round to pear-shaped, typically more than 10 cm across, with oil glands large enough to see.

Flora of China gives a description that is worth knowing because it explains the material:

- **Leaves** — broadly ovate or elliptic, 9–16 cm long and 4–8 cm wide, on a winged petiole.
- **Fruit** — pale yellow or yellowish green, round to pear-shaped, commonly more than 10 cm in diameter.
- **Rind** — large, prominent oil dots, visible to the naked eye.
- **Flower** — 25–35 stamens, a detail useful for separating citrus species at flowering time.

The oil dots are the physical basis of the aroma. Each one is a small cavity of essential oil, and the density of those cavities across the rind is why a fresh pomelo releases scent when you merely score the skin. The fruit's size matters for a second reason: a big fruit carries a proportionally heavy rind and pith, so a single pomelo yields far more peel than a mandarin of the same count. That is an agricultural fact before it is an aromatic one.

The species is native to Southeast Asia and has been cultivated in southern China for a very long time. Its presence in the region is old enough that the fruit reads as local in Guangdong, Guangxi and Fujian cooking, even though the botanical origin lies further south. The peel follows the fruit: where pomelos are eaten, peels are available, and a by-product stream becomes a material stream.

For the full botanical account, see [Citrus maxima in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=242412666) and the [GBIF occurrence record](https://www.gbif.org/species/3190160).

## Naming: 柚皮, 柚子皮 and the shaddock problem

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 means "pomelo peel" — 柚 for the fruit, 皮 for the peel — and 柚子皮 is the same thing in looser everyday form. English adds a second layer, because "pomelo" and "shaddock" name the same fruit.

The Chinese construction is transparent, which is a rarity in this material vocabulary. 柚 (yòu) is the pomelo; 皮 (pí) is peel or skin; put them together and you have named both the species and the part. The longer 柚子皮 adds 子, a noun suffix, and means exactly the same thing. Neither form tells you the pith proportion or the drying method, which is where the real differences live.

English is less tidy. "Pomelo" is the common name in modern trade; "shaddock" is an older English name for the same fruit, still used in some botanical and historical writing. A reader who encounters "shaddock peel" in an old text should not go looking for a different species.

The naming problem that actually matters is horizontal, not vertical: 柚皮 sits in a cluster of Chinese peel materials whose names are visually and phonetically close, and whose uses are not. 陈皮, 化橘红, 柚皮 and 荔枝壳 all appear in the same conversations about fruit peels and incense, and a buyer who treats them as variants of "citrus peel" will get a material that does not do what the formula asked for.

## Four peels that get confused

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮, 陈皮, 化橘红 and 荔枝壳 are four different materials from three different plant families. They overlap in name and in the idea of "a dried peel", and nowhere else.

| Material | Species and family | Part and treatment | Aroma register | Why it gets confused |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 柚皮 | *Citrus maxima*, Rutaceae | Whole peel, pith intact, dried | Bittersweet, floral, faintly woody | The default "pomelo peel" |
| 陈皮 | *Citrus reticulata*, Rutaceae | Mandarin peel, aged for years | Warm, mellow, tea-like, sweet-bitter | Same family, similar look, both called "peel" |
| 化橘红 | *Citrus grandis* 'Tomentosa', Rutaceae | Peel of young or nearly mature fruit, dried | Intensely bitter, aromatic, resinous | A pomelo-group citrus, so the fruit name overlaps |
| 荔枝壳 | *Litchi chinensis*, Sapindaceae | Fruit husk, dried | Sweet, floral, slightly tart | A non-citrus peel used in the same blends |

The table is not trivia. Each row implies a different substitution decision:

- **柚皮 for 陈皮** fails on the axis of age. 陈皮's character comes from time; a fresh pomelo peel cannot imitate it, no matter how it is dried.
- **陈皮 for 柚皮** fails on the axis of pith. The mandarin peel is thin, and the dish or blend loses the soft bitterness that pomelo pith supplies.
- **化橘红 for 柚皮** fails on intensity. 化橘红 is a young-fruit peel with a much harder bitter profile; it will dominate where 柚皮 would support.
- **荔枝壳 for 柚皮** fails on family. It sits outside the citrus group entirely, with a sweet floral note and none of the peel-oil brightness.

The wider point is that "citrus peel" is a category, not a specification. A formula that names the category has left the most important variable — which peel, how much pith, how old — unspecified.

## From by-product to material

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel is a by-product of the fruit trade, not a purpose-grown aromatic crop. That single fact explains its price, its variability and the kind of quality control it needs.

Pomelos are grown to be eaten. The peel is what remains after the fruit has been sold, juiced, candied or processed, and the peel stream inherits the habits of that larger business. Where fruit is handled carefully, peel arrives in clean sheets with the pith intact. Where fruit is processed in bulk, peel arrives shredded, mixed across varieties, or damaged by machinery that was never designed to preserve an aromatic layer.

Three consequences follow for anyone buying the material:

- **Seasonality is real.** The fruit has a main season in the cooler months, so fresh peel is a seasonal material and dried peel is the form that carries the year. A "fresh peel" offer in the off-season is either stored, imported, or not what it says.
- **Variety is loose.** The pomelo group includes many cultivars with different rind thickness and oil character. Trade often does not name the cultivar, and a lot may blend several.
- **Care is uneven.** Because the peel is a residue, it receives the drying attention the fruit business can spare, not the attention a perfume material would command. This is the main reason two lots of the same species can differ so much.

The commercial logic is worth stating plainly: the material is inexpensive, and the value is added in handling. A well-dried, well-stored, correctly specified peel is worth more than a cheap lot that has to be discarded, but the difference is invisible on an invoice that lists only "pomelo peel". That is why the specification — species, pith treatment, drying method, lot age — matters more here than the price per kilo.

## The scent: bitter brightness over a soft base

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel smells of citrus first and bitterness second, with a floral, faintly woody undertone and far less sharp acidity than lemon or fresh orange zest. The bitterness is soft rather than medicinal, and it rounds the brightness instead of cutting it.

The opening impression is the rind: a clean citrus lift, close to grapefruit but less aggressive, with a floral edge that appears as the oil settles. Behind it sits the pith, which contributes a gentle bitterness and a texture the nose reads as "dry" rather than "sour". The combination is why 柚皮 can sit in a warm spice blend without turning it into a citrus dessert.

The chemistry behind the lift is ordinary citrus oil chemistry. The dominant terpene in citrus peel oil is limonene, and it is volatile — it reaches the nose quickly and leaves quickly. That volatility is the reason the material behaves as a top note even though its bitterness suggests something heavier. The bitterness itself comes from a different class of compounds, bitter flavonoids and limonoids, which are far less volatile and stay in the material after the bright note has gone.

The practical consequence is a two-stage experience. Freshly ground peel smells bright and almost sweet for the first minutes, then settles into a drier, bitter-woody register. A taster who only catches the first stage will overestimate the material's brightness; one who waits gets the truer picture.

Compared to its citrus relatives, the register is distinct:

| Peel | Species | Character | Best role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pomelo 柚皮 | *Citrus maxima* | Bittersweet, floral, thick pith | Soft citrus accent in warm blends |
| Chenpi 陈皮 | *Citrus reticulata*, aged | Mellow, tea-like, sweet-bitter | Depth and age in medicinal-style blends |
| Grapefruit 葡萄柚 | *Citrus paradisi* | Sharp, bitter, sulphurous edge | Bright top note with attitude |
| Lemon 柠檬 | *Citrus limon* | Clean, sour, high-pitched | Sharp lift, short life |

## Fresh, dried, and the water problem

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 is used fresh or dried, and drying is the step that decides whether the lot stays bright or turns flat and harsh. The peel is wet, thick and oil-rich, so it must lose water fast enough to avoid mould without losing the oil that carries the top note.

The processing sequence is short:

1. **Peel the fruit.** The coloured rind and the white pith come off together, in sheets or in strips.
2. **Decide the pith.** Leave it thick for a rounder, more bitter material; shave it for a brighter, leaner one. This is a specification choice, not a cleaning step.
3. **Dry.** Air, sun or low heat, until the peel is leathery or crisp depending on the target form.
4. **Cut or grind.** Whole sheets for slow release and visual presence; strips for blending; powder for sticks and cones.

The mechanism explains the failures:

**Fresh peel, still full of water → a hot, fast dry drives moisture out of the surface while the interior lags → the surface case-hardens and the oil glands near the skin flash off → the lot ends up dull on top and still damp inside → the damp core moulds in the bag and the whole batch reads musty.**

The opposite approach has its own cost. A slow, cool dry protects the oil but gives mould days to establish, especially in a humid season. The workable middle is moderate heat with moving air: enough to pull water out steadily, not enough to cook the rind. A well-dried peel is leathery-flexible when whole and crisp when thin, with colour close to the fresh rind rather than uniformly brown.

There is a culinary parallel worth knowing, because it shows the same trade-off in the kitchen. Southern Chinese cooks traditionally blanch and soak pomelo peel, sometimes changing the water several times, to draw out bitterness before braising or candying it. Each soak removes some bitter compounds and some aroma with them. The kitchen and the incense bench are solving the same equation from opposite ends: how much bitterness do you want to keep, and what are you willing to lose to remove the rest?

## Why it is burned

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 earns its place as a softening citrus accent: it brings lift without lemon's sharpness, plus a bitter-woody weight from the pith that sweet blends need. It supports rather than leads, and is nearly always blended.

Three properties make it useful in a censer:

- **It bridges sweet and bitter.** Many warm blends are sweet from resins and spice; a citrus peel with pith gives them an edge that keeps the sweetness from flattening.
- **It is rounder than lemon or bergamot.** Where a sharp citrus top note can dominate, 柚皮 sits behind the other materials and shapes them.
- **It carries a food-adjacent warmth.** The same peel that goes into tea and braises reads as familiar in a room scent, which is why it appears in household and temple-adjacent blends rather than only in perfumery experiments.

The limitation is equally clear. The material has no resin and no density, so it cannot anchor a formula or extend a burn. It appears early, contributes its brightness and bitterness, and then hands the composition to whatever heavier material sits behind it. Expecting 柚皮 to carry a stick is like expecting zest to carry a cake.

## Which format suits a peel

> **Direct answer:** Dried peel works best as a coarse cut or a whole sheet warmed gently; powder suits sticks if the mix is built for it; a cone is too hot and a coil too long. The format decides whether the bitterness reads as structure or as an off-note.

Peel needs heat to release its oil. At room temperature a dried strip is surprisingly quiet; warm it and the citrus arrives within seconds. That behaviour makes the format choice unusually consequential.

| Format | How the peel is presented | What it gives | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried sheet | Slow warming, large surface | A long, gentle citrus-bitter exhalation | Uneven drying leaves a damp core |
| Coarse cut | Loose in a sachet or dish | Controllable, even release | Dust escapes through loose weave |
| Powder in a stick | Bound with a base | Convenient, repeatable opening | Pith drinks binder; note buried |
| Powder in a cone | Dense, hot core | Strong first impression | Heat scorches the oil; acrid edge |
| Coil | Long burn, small peel fraction | A faint citrus at the start | Hours of burn with nothing left |
| Fresh peel on a warmer | Low, moist heat | The fullest, softest reading | Short window before it dries out |

Two practical readings follow. For evaluation, warm a whole or coarsely cut piece on a low warmer; that is the closest a nose gets to the material itself. For a finished product, the powder-in-stick route is workable only when the base is chosen for a light, bitter accent rather than a heavy smoke profile.

My editorial judgement is that a cone is the worst common format for this material. The dense centre runs hotter than a stick, and the oil that carries the citrus is the first thing to go. What remains is the pith's bitterness without the brightness that made it pleasant — the same compounds, a much worse balance.

## Milling, pith and the clumping problem

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel powder is light, hygroscopic and pith-heavy, so it absorbs binder and moisture and tends to clump. Coarse cut peel behaves better in a blend than fine powder, and the pith fraction changes how much binder the mix demands.

The mechanics run like this:

**Thick pith, left on the peel → a spongy, high-surface material → in a dry mix it drinks binder and ambient moisture → the mix stiffens and forms lumps → the maker adds water or binder to compensate → the finished stick burns heavier and the citrus note is buried → the batch is functionally duller than its recipe.**

Two adjustments follow. The first is grind size: a coarse cut or a light fluff keeps the material distributed and reduces the amount of binder needed to hold a stick together. The second is pith specification: if the formula wants brightness, ask for a thinner cut; if it wants the bitter roundness, keep the pith and accept that the mix will need more care.

Storage interacts with both. Ground peel is far more hygroscopic than whole peel, and a clumped bag is a warning about moisture, not just about texture. Whole dried peel, kept sealed and dark, holds its oil far longer; grinding close to use is the better habit. The site's notes on [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/) cover the wider behaviour of powder-based blends, and [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) covers the process around it.

## Where it sits in a formula

> **Direct answer:** It is a supporting citrus note, placed in the opening-to-heart transition, and it pairs best with warm spices, light woods and other peels. It should be dosed as a seasoning, not as a base.

Good partners follow from the material's position:

- **Cinnamon and cassia** — the warmth absorbs the bitterness and turns it into depth rather than a sour edge.
- **Clove or 丁香** — a small amount sharpens the citrus; too much and the clove takes over.
- **Sandalwood** — gives the peel something to sit on, so the citrus does not vanish abruptly.
- **Litchi husk 荔枝壳** — a fruit-peel partner with a sweeter, floral character; the two together read rounder than either alone.
- **Light floral notes such as osmanthus** — the floral edge of the peel meets the apricot sweetness of the flower without competing.

Poor partners are the very heavy ones. A dense resin or an oil-rich agarwood will cover a soft citrus peel within seconds, and the material becomes an expensive way to add a faint tang. The mismatch is one of weight, not of quality.

As with any accent, the dose depends on the base, the binder, the format and the target intensity. A number that works in one workshop does not transfer to another, and a page that prints grams without knowing the formula is guessing. The reliable method is to build the base first, then add peel in small increments until the bitterness reads as structure rather than as a flaw.

## Quality: what a lot can tell you

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on freshness, clean drying, an intact pith and a bittersweet scent that lifts when warmed. There is no commercial grading ladder for 柚皮, so the lot is judged on condition and specification rather than on a label.

The signals, in the order I would check them:

1. **Smell, cold and warmed.** A clean peel should release citrus when scratched or warmed in the hand. A flat, dusty or musty smell is a drying or storage failure.
2. **Colour.** Close to the fresh rind — yellow-green to amber — with even drying. Uniform dark brown suggests heat damage; grey or black patches suggest mould.
3. **Pith condition.** Thick, pale and intact if the specification calls for it; no soft or discoloured patches.
4. **Moisture.** Leathery-flexible for whole peel, crisp for thin strips. A bendy strip that feels cool and damp is under-dried.
5. **Cleanliness.** No soil, no fruit flesh, no foreign matter. Peel is the outer surface of the fruit, so dirt and residue questions attach to it more than to the flesh.
6. **Consistency.** A lot that mixes thick and shaved pieces, or fresh and old colour, will behave unpredictably in a blend.

The residue point deserves a measured sentence rather than a scare. Because the peel is the fruit's outer surface, any question about agricultural residues applies to it first. A buyer can ask what statement the supplier holds; a page cannot certify a lot it has never seen. What a page can say is that the question belongs in the specification, alongside species and pith.

"Grade A" on a pomelo-peel invoice is a seller's phrase, not a standard. Treat it as a claim to verify with the six checks above, not as a fact that removes the need to look.

## Storage: keeping a hygroscopic peel

> **Direct answer:** Dried pomelo peel pulls moisture and odours from the air, and its oil oxidises with light and heat. Sealed, dark, cool storage is not a refinement; it is what separates a lot that still tastes of citrus from one that tastes of cupboard.

Three forms, three rates of decline:

- **Whole sheets** keep longest. The oil sits inside intact glands, and the surface exposed to air is small.
- **Cut strips** decline faster. More cut face means more exposed oil, and the strips also compact under their own weight.
- **Powder** declines fastest. Grinding opens the glands, and the resulting material both loses volatiles and absorbs moisture eagerly.

The mechanism is the same one that governs the aroma in the censer:

**Cut or ground peel → oil glands opened and surface area multiplied → limonene and the lighter terpenes evaporate and oxidise → the bright citrus top flattens into a dull, faintly turpentine note → the bitterness remains, so the material now reads harsh rather than balanced.**

Storage practice follows:

1. **Seal it.** A tight-lidded jar or a sealed food-grade bag with the air pressed out. A folded paper bag is not storage; it is a slow leak.
2. **Keep it dark.** Light accelerates oxidation. An opaque container in a cupboard is better than a clear jar on a shelf.
3. **Keep it cool and dry.** Heat drives off volatiles; humidity invites mould. A kitchen shelf above a stove is the worst common choice.
4. **Isolate it.** Peel takes on the smell of whatever sits beside it, and a bag of spices will overwrite the citrus within weeks.
5. **Label the lot.** Species, pith specification and date. Without a date there is no way to distinguish a decline from a bad purchase.

One boundary matters. Re-drying a soft lot removes surface moisture but does not undo oxidation or mould. If the peel smells musty or shows grey-green patches, discarding it is the only sound move; heat does not make a spoiled food-derived material safe to burn.

## Writing a specification for 柚皮

> **Direct answer:** A usable pomelo-peel specification names the species, the pith treatment, the drying method, the lot date and the storage history. Without those five fields, two orders with the same name can be different materials.

Most purchase failures with this peel come from a short description, not a bad supplier. The invoice says "pomelo peel", the buyer imagines one material, and the warehouse ships another. A specification closes that gap by turning assumptions into checkable fields.

| Field | What to write | Why it changes the material |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Citrus maxima*, cultivar if known | Separates pomelo from 化橘红 and from generic citrus peel |
| Part and pith | Whole peel with pith; half-trimmed; rind only | Sets bitterness, density and binder demand |
| Drying | Sun, shade, or low-heat mechanical | Determines whether the top note survives |
| Form | Whole sheet, cut strip, or powder | Controls surface area, storage life and dust |
| Moisture | Leathery-flexible or crisp, as agreed | Predicts mould risk and clumping |
| Lot date | Month and year of drying | Distinguishes a fresh lot from old stock |
| Storage | Sealed, dark, odour-free | Explains why two lots of the same age differ |
| Residue statement | Whatever the supplier actually holds | Puts the outer-surface question on record |

The last row is the one buyers skip and later regret. The peel is the fruit's outer surface, so any question about what was applied to the fruit belongs to the peel before the flesh. A page cannot certify a lot; a specification can at least record what is claimed, and a claim on paper is easier to test than an assumption in someone's head.

A second, subtler point: the specification should name the *purpose*. A peel destined for a warming dish and a peel destined for a stick are not the same goods, even when both are clean and correctly identified. Writing "culinary grade" on an incense order, or the reverse, tells the supplier which trade-offs to make when the drying curve gets difficult. That single sentence prevents most of the mismatch that quality inspection later cannot fix.

## Working with fresh peel

> **Direct answer:** Fresh pomelo peel is brighter and more floral than the dried material, but it is also wet, bulky and quick to spoil. It is best used as a short-lived accent or dried deliberately, not sealed in a jar and forgotten.

Fresh and dried peel are related but not interchangeable:

| | Fresh peel | Dried peel |
|---|---|---|
| Aroma | Bright, floral, high citrus | Deeper, drier, more bitter-forward |
| Weight | Mostly water; heavy for its volume | Concentrated; light |
| Behaviour when heated | Releases oil fast, then steams | Releases slowly and steadily |
| Storage life | Days, refrigerated | Months, if sealed and dry |
| Grinding | Clogs mills; unsuitable | Straightforward once crisp |
| Best use | Immediate warming, kitchen use | Blending, storage, repeatability |

A few practical rules follow from that table. Fresh peel should not go into a sealed jar "to keep the scent in", because the moisture it releases will condense on the glass and give mould a place to start. If you want to keep the aroma, dry the peel first; if you want to smell it now, warm it gently and use it within the day.

Home drying is workable if it is done with air rather than hope. Cut the peel into thin, even strips, spread them so they do not touch, keep the air moving, and turn them once a day. Sun drying works in a dry season, but the strips must come indoors before evening damp, or they will re-absorb moisture overnight and dry unevenly. The finish line is leathery and flexible for a thick strip, crisp for a thin one; a strip that still feels cool and damp in the middle is not done.

One caution about judging fresh peel: the nose is good at the citrus top and poor at the bitterness. A fresh fruit can smell wonderful and carry a pith so bitter that it dominates a blend. If bitterness matters to the formula, judge it on a dried sample, where the water has gone and the bitter compounds read clearly.

Fresh peel is also the most honest way to learn the material's range. Two pomelos from the same market can differ noticeably in oil intensity and pith thickness, and handling both teaches more about lot variation than any description can. That lesson is worth the mess on the kitchen counter.

## Reading a supplier's description

> **Direct answer:** Most pomelo-peel descriptions are written to sound reassuring rather than to specify a material. A buyer's job is to translate each phrase into the question it leaves unanswered.

| What the listing says | What it usually means | What to ask next |
|---|---|---|
| "Premium grade" | A seller's adjective, not a standard | Which specification — pith, drying, lot date? |
| "Sun-dried" | Dried outdoors, weather permitting | Was it finished under cover? How was it stored? |
| "Wild pomelo" | Fruit from untended trees, or a marketing frame | Which species? Is residue history known? |
| "Pith removed" | Rind-forward material | Why was it removed — brightness or drying speed? |
| "Aged peel" | Stored for some period, undefined | Species? Age in years? Conditions? |
| "Citrus peel" | A category, not a material | Which citrus, which part, which treatment? |

The pattern is the same in every row: the phrase describes an intention, and the specification describes a material. A supplier who can answer the follow-up questions is telling you something more useful than any adjective — namely, that the lot was produced deliberately rather than sorted out of a residue stream.

There is a fair way to use this table. Not every small supplier has a formal spec sheet, and a good trader can still describe drying, pith and storage from experience. The test is whether the answers are specific and consistent, not whether they are printed. A vague answer to "which species?" is a warning; a clear answer to "how was it dried?" is a good sign, even if it arrives by message rather than on a document.

My editorial view is that this material is bought twice: once as a price, and once as a set of properties. The price is visible immediately; the properties only show up in the blend. Spending five minutes on the second purchase is what keeps the first from being wasted.

## The aging question: why 柚皮 is not 陈皮

> **Direct answer:** These are two peels with two defining variables. 陈皮 is mandarin peel whose value comes from age; 柚皮 is pomelo peel whose value comes from pith. Aging the pomelo does not produce 陈皮, and a fresh mandarin peel is not 陈皮 either.

This is the most consequential distinction on the page, and it is easy to state precisely:

| | Pomelo peel 柚皮 | Chenpi 陈皮 |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Citrus maxima* | *Citrus reticulata* |
| Defining variable | Pith thickness | Age |
| Character | Bittersweet, floral-woody, soft | Warm, mellow, tea-like, sweet-bitter |
| Processing logic | Dry the whole peel, keep the pith | Dry, then store and let time work |
| Failure mode | Mould from under-drying | Flatness from poor storage or short aging |

Aging is not a neutral wait. Over years, a peel loses water, its volatile oil slowly oxidises, and its harsher edges soften into the mellow, tea-like profile that 陈皮 is prized for. That process depends on the species as much as on time: a thin mandarin peel with the right starting chemistry transforms in a way a thick pomelo peel does not. Putting a pomelo peel in a jar for a decade gives you old pomelo peel, not 陈皮.

The substitution rule that follows is narrow and useful. 柚皮 can stand in for 陈皮 only where the formula needs "a citrus peel with some bitterness" and does not depend on the aged, tea-like note. It cannot substitute where age is the point. Going the other way, 陈皮 can substitute for 柚皮 only if the blend can lose the pith's soft bitterness — which, in most warm-spice formulas, it cannot.

## Cooking and burning: one material, two preparations

> **Direct answer:** The kitchen and the censer want opposite things from the same peel. Cooking usually removes bitterness with water; incense usually keeps it. Understanding the kitchen method explains why a "de-bittered" peel is a different material, not a better one.

The traditional kitchen sequence is a controlled extraction:

1. **Blanch** the peel in boiling water for a short time.
2. **Soak** in cold water, sometimes for hours.
3. **Squeeze** out the water, and repeat the soak if the bitterness is still strong.
4. **Braise or candy** in a sweet or savoury liquid.

Each step pulls water-soluble bitter compounds out of the pith, and each step also carries away some aromatic oil. The cook accepts that trade because the goal is a tender, palatable peel. A cook who skipped the soaking would have a bitter dish; a cook who soaked too long would have a bland one.

The incense maker faces the same curve from the other side. Here the bitterness is the point: it gives the blend structure and keeps the citrus from reading as candy. A peel that has been blanched to kitchen tenderness has already lost the tension that makes 柚皮 useful in a censer. That is why a recipe calling for "de-bittered pomelo peel" and an incense specification calling for "柚皮 with pith" are asking for two different materials under one name.

One safety note belongs here. Candied or sugar-coated peel should not be burned. Sugar caramelises and scorches, and the resulting smoke is acrid and harsher than plain peel. The kitchen product and the incense material are separate goods, even when they started as the same fruit.

## In Chinese tradition

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 has a documented culinary and household life in southern China — braised, candied, made into tea — and a traditional materia-medica entry. Its incense use is an extension of that aromatic household familiarity, not a classical canon role.

Three strands, kept separate:

- **Cooking.** Pomelo peel is traditionally blanched, soaked and braised, or candied, in southern Chinese kitchens. The blanching is a bitterness-control step, and it is the clearest everyday demonstration of how the pith behaves.
- **Tea and drinks.** Pomelo peel appears in honey-pomelo teas and in mixed herbal drinks, usually with the pith partly trimmed. This is documented culinary use.
- **Materia medica.** 柚皮 is recorded in the 本草綱目 果部 tradition, with described uses in traditional Chinese medicine. That is **traditional belief** — materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence, and this page makes no medical claims.

The incense strand is thinner than the culinary one. 柚皮 appears in blends as a soft citrus peel note, and the practice is best described as traditional household aromatic use rather than as a ritual canon. Saying so is not a criticism; it is a more accurate description than the usual "ancient temple incense" framing, and it tells a reader what kind of material they are actually holding.

## Safety: smoke, oil and the clean-air myth

> **Direct answer:** Burning pomelo peel produces smoke and fine particulate matter like any other plant material, and it does not purify the air. The citrus smell may make a room feel fresher; the particle load is unchanged by the pleasant association.

Incense smoke carries fine particles and carbon monoxide whatever the ingredient, and a natural peel is no exception. Work in a ventilated room, keep your face out of the plume, never leave a lit stick unattended, and site burners where children and pets cannot reach them. People with asthma or other respiratory sensitivities should be cautious around any incense smoke.

Two material-specific notes are worth adding. Fresh peel carries a high load of volatile oil, and a pile of fresh peelings left in a warm room will scent the space strongly without any burning at all — a reminder that the aromatic fraction is volatile, not bound. Dried peel that has gone soft or patchy should not be burned; mould on a food-derived material is a reason to discard the lot, not to dry it out and carry on.

This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice, and it makes no claim that burning 柚皮 treats, prevents or improves any condition. See the [safety guide](/safety/) for the wider picture on smoke and ventilation.

## FAQ

**What is pomelo peel (柚皮)?** The dried skin of *Citrus maxima*, the pomelo — a large Rutaceae fruit that originated in Southeast Asia and has been grown in southern China for centuries. Sold whole or ground, it carries a bittersweet, floral-woody scent and a thick pith, and it appears in incense, in cooking and in tea.

**Is 柚皮 the same as 陈皮?** No. 柚皮 is pomelo peel; 陈皮 is aged mandarin peel from *Citrus reticulata*, traditionally associated with Xinhui in Guangdong. They differ in species, in pith, and in what makes them valuable: pith defines the pomelo, age defines the mandarin. Neither substitutes cleanly for the other.

**What about 化橘红 — is that pomelo peel too?** It is close enough to cause real confusion. 化橘红 is the dried peel of young or nearly mature fruit of *Citrus grandis* 'Tomentosa', a different citrus in the pomelo group, traditionally associated with Huazhou in Guangdong. It is far more intensely bitter than 柚皮 and is treated as a separate drug, not as a pomelo-peel variant.

**Why is pomelo peel bitter, and can the bitterness be removed?** The bitterness sits mainly in the white pith, carried by bitter flavonoids and limonoids rather than by the volatile oil. It can be reduced by blanching and soaking, the traditional kitchen method, but each soak also removes aroma. In incense, the bitterness is usually kept, because it is what makes the material distinct.

**Can I dry and burn the peel from a pomelo I ate?** You can dry it, and it will smell of citrus, but a kitchen-dried peel is uneven: the pith may stay damp, the rind may darken, and there is no control over residue or storage. For a repeatable material, buy peel that specifies the species, the pith treatment and the drying method.

## Summary

柚皮 is the dried skin of *Citrus maxima*, a Rutaceae fruit from Southeast Asia that southern China has grown for centuries. Its fruit is large — usually more than 10 cm across — with pale-yellow to greenish skin, visible oil glands and a heavy white layer under the rind. That layer is the material's identity: it supplies the soft bitterness balancing the bright oil above, and it sets 柚皮 apart from thinner citrus peels.

In incense the role is supporting. The peel reads as citrus, bittersweet, floral and faintly woody, arrives early and hands over quickly, and works best beside warm spices and light woods. Quality depends on freshness, even drying, intact pith and clean storage, and no grading ladder exists to lean on. The confusions to avoid are 陈皮, where age rather than pith is the defining variable, and 化橘红, a harder-bitter peel from the same broad citrus group. Identified correctly and dried cleanly, 柚皮 is a modest material with a precise job.

> **Direct answer:** Peony (芍药, sháoyào) is the herbaceous Chinese peony, *Paeonia lactiflora*, a long-cultivated perennial in the peony family (Paeoniaceae). It has been grown in Chinese gardens for more than a millennium, it carries the classical name 将离 (jiānglí, "about to part") in the *Bencao Gangmu*, and its root is a materia-medica item. As an incense material it is close to nothing: the flower is a garden fragrance, and "peony" on a fragrance label almost always names a created accord rather than a plant.

## Key facts

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Chinese name | 芍药 (sháoyào); classical 将离 (jiānglí, "about to part") |
| Botanical name | *Paeonia lactiflora* Pall., family Paeoniaceae |
| Plant type | Herbaceous perennial — top growth dies to the ground each winter |
| Native range | Central and eastern Asia: northern China, Mongolia, Siberia, neighbouring regions |
| Garden history | More than a thousand years of Chinese cultivation; many double-flowered cultivars |
| Material used | Flower (ornamental, aromatic on the plant) and root (白芍 báisháo / 赤芍 chìsháo, materia medica) |
| Aroma | Floral, rosy, sweet, green; intensity varies enormously by cultivar, and some are effectively scentless |
| Incense status | Not a classical 香材 (xiāngcái, incense material); no established extraction or burning tradition |

The table is short on purpose. Almost everything a reader needs to know about 芍药 fits into two columns, because the plant's importance is horticultural and cultural rather than aromatic. The sections below unpack the parts that get muddled in English-language sources: which peony is which, why a celebrated flower can have no smell, and what a formulator should do when a brief asks for "peony."

## What the name 芍药 actually covers

The word "peony" in English is a genus-level label that hides at least two very different garden plants. 芍药 is the herbaceous one: soft green stems, deeply cut leaves, large bowl or pom-pom flowers in late spring, and a whole plant that collapses to the ground when cold arrives. Botanically it sits in *Paeonia lactiflora*, a species native to a broad band of central and eastern Asia running from northern China through Mongolia and into Siberia and adjacent territory.

Two features of the species matter for anyone writing about it. The first is that it is herbaceous, not woody. There is no persistent trunk, no bark, no above-ground framework to prune. The second is that it has been bred hard for flower form. Chinese gardeners have selected 芍药 for centuries, producing doubled, semi-doubled and anemone-form cultivars in white, pink, rose, magenta and near-red. That breeding history explains a great deal of the plant's reputation — and, as later sections show, a great deal of its inconsistent scent.

The root is a separate product from the flower. In the traditional materia medica the root is divided into 白芍 (báisháo, "white peony root"), which is peeled and processed, and 赤芍 (chìsháo, "red peony root"), which is dried unpeeled. Both names refer to root material, not to petals, and neither should be confused with the ornamental flower trade. A buyer who orders "peony root" and a buyer who orders "peony plants" are shopping in two unrelated supply chains.

## A plant that disappears every winter

The annual cycle of 芍药 is the single most useful thing to understand about its cultivation, because the cycle explains the flowering window, the root harvest and the plant's reputation for being both spectacular and brief.

The sequence runs like this: shortening days and falling soil temperatures → the top growth yellows and dies → the crown and its stored root mass survive underground → winter chilling breaks bud dormancy → warming soil in spring → new shoots push up → flower buds swell and open over a few weeks → seed set or dieback begins again. Each arrow is a dependency, not a decoration. Remove the chilling period and the plant flowers poorly or not at all; remove the root reserves and there is nothing to fund the spring push.

That cycle has practical consequences. A garden 芍药 is at its peak for a short window in late spring, and the window moves with latitude and season. Cut flowers are therefore a seasonal trade rather than a year-round one in field-grown supply. The root, by contrast, is lifted after the top has died back, when the plant's reserves are concentrated below ground — which is why root harvest and flower harvest sit at opposite ends of the year.

There is a second consequence that matters to anyone writing about scent. The flower exists for a few weeks; the plant exists all year. A cultivar selected for a two-week display does not need a strong scent to succeed in the garden, and breeding programmes have not consistently selected for one.

## 将离: a name that records a goodbye

The most quoted fact about 芍药 is its classical name. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), in the fragrant-herb section (草之三), lists 芍药 under the name 将离 and glosses it directly: "芍药一名将离" — "the peony is also called 将离." The phrase 将离 means "about to part" or "on the point of separation."

The name is not a botanical description. It records a social practice. The *Shijing* (诗经), in the 郑风 section, contains the poem 溱洧 (Zhēn Wěi), which describes young men and women meeting by the river in spring and ends with the line "赠之以勺药" — "they give each other 勺药." The character 勺 there is an old written form associated with 芍药, and later commentary read the gesture as a parting gift between friends or lovers. Whether or not every detail of that reading is secure, the association is old, textual and specific: the flower was given at a farewell.

That is the whole of the classical claim, and it is worth stating plainly because it is often inflated. 将离 is a cultural name about human partings. It is not evidence of an aromatic use, a medicinal use, or a ritual burning practice. The *Bencao Gangmu* entry places 芍药 in a herbal section, which tells you the compiler cared about the root; the flower's fame in Chinese poetry tells you the culture cared about the bloom. Neither line of evidence turns the plant into incense.

In practice, this distinction is the difference between a good encyclopedia entry and a romantic one. A romantic entry says: ancient flower of farewell, therefore ancient incense. A careful entry says: ancient flower of farewell, therefore a garden plant with a documented cultural name, and no incense record at all.

## 芍药 and 牡丹: the confusion that will not die

The most common error in English-language writing about Chinese peonies is to treat 芍药 and 牡丹 as synonyms or as varieties of one another. They are different plants with different growth habits, different ornamental roles and different materia-medica parts.

| Question | 芍药 (herbaceous peony) | 牡丹 (tree peony) |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Paeonia lactiflora* | *Paeonia × suffruticosa* |
| Stem | Soft, green, dies back each winter | Woody, persists above ground |
| Winter appearance | Nothing above ground but dormant buds | A bare shrub with visible branches |
| Flowering | Late spring, on new growth | Spring, on wood formed the previous year |
| Materia-medica part | Root (白芍 / 赤芍) | Root bark (牡丹皮, mǔdān pí) |
| Old Chinese alias | 芍药, 将离 | 木芍药 ("woody shaoyao") — a historical name |
| English trap | Called "peony" | Also called "peony" |

The alias in the last row is the source of much of the mess. 牡丹 was historically known as 木芍药, literally "woody 芍药," which is a perfectly sensible description of a woody plant that resembles the herbaceous one — and a perfect trap for anyone reading an old text quickly. A phrase like "woody peony" in translation may be pointing at 牡丹, not at a peony with thick stems.

The mechanism behind the confusion is simple: two showy spring-flowering plants in the same genus, one herbaceous and one woody, both called "peony" in English, both central to Chinese garden culture → English writers collapse them → materia-medica references to "peony root" get applied to the wrong plant → buyers order the wrong material. The correction is equally simple, if less satisfying: name the species, name the part, and check whether the plant in front of you has a trunk.

For a collector, the practical test is seasonal. Look in midwinter. A 芍药 bed is bare soil with small red buds at the crown. A 牡丹 shrub is a woody skeleton with branches. Look in spring. 牡丹 flowers on old wood; 芍药 sends up fresh stems and flowers on them. If someone sells you a "tree peony" in a pot and it dies to the ground in its first winter, you probably bought 芍药.

## Why a famous flower can be scentless

Here is the fact that surprises readers most: many garden 芍药 have little or no fragrance. The plant is grown for the size, colour and doubling of its flowers, and a cultivar can be magnificent in all three while offering almost nothing to the nose.

The reason is a breeding trajectory rather than a defect. The chain runs roughly as follows: gardeners select for visual impact → double and semi-double forms are favoured → doubling converts stamens into petal-like structures → the flower's volatile-emitting tissue is redistributed and reduced → scent declines in the selected line → the cultivar is propagated vegetatively because it is prized for its look → the low-scent trait is fixed in the clone. Every step is a plausible horticultural consequence, and together they explain why "double white peony" tells you about appearance and nothing at all about aroma.

The chain has a second branch that affects cut flowers specifically. A grower cuts stems in bud so the flowers travel and open in the vase → the bloom is severed from the plant's supply of water and sugars → petal expansion continues on stored reserves → volatile production, which is metabolically expensive, falls off → the flower opens beautifully and smells of very little. A cut 芍药 that looks superb on day one and fades by day three is not a bad flower; it is a flower that has been asked to perform display without the resources that support scent.

This is where an editorial judgment belongs. Scentlessness is not a quality failure in a garden peony. It is a design consequence. Grading a 芍药 cultivar by fragrance is like grading a rose by its vase life: the criterion is real, but it is not the one the breeder was optimising. The mistake is to assume that a famous flower must be a fragrant one, and then to buy "peony" expecting a scent the cultivar never had.

## What peony actually smells like

Where a 芍药 cultivar does carry scent, it sits in the rosy-floral family. The impression is soft rather than sharp: a sweet, slightly powdery rose-like heart, a fresh green edge from the leaves and sepals, and a clean, almost tea-like finish. It is less spicy than a true rose and less almond-like than plum blossom, and it lacks the honeyed heaviness that some white flowers carry.

The volatile families behind that impression are the usual rosy-floral suspects. 2-phenylethanol gives the soft rose character; citronellol and geraniol add the rounded, slightly lemon-rose body; linalool contributes the clean floral lift; and green leaf alcohols such as cis-3-hexenol supply the freshly cut stem edge. Some cultivars lean sweeter, some lean greener, and a few carry a faint clove-like warmth from eugenol-type notes. These are general families associated with peony-type floral scent, not a fixed recipe, and the balance shifts with cultivar, weather and time of day.

Two practical notes follow. First, peony scent is often strongest in the morning, when the flower is freshly open and the air is cool and still; it fades as the day warms and the petals age. Second, the scent does not survive drying well. A dried peony petal smells of hay, dust and old paper far more than of the flower, because the light volatiles that carry the rosy impression evaporate during drying and the heavier, less pleasant compounds remain.

That is why peony is a poor candidate for the incense jar. The material that smells wonderful on the plant is mostly water and delicate volatiles, and the processes that make plant material storable — drying, aging, grinding — destroy exactly the fraction that made it worth keeping.

## Peony in the burner: an honest answer

芍药 is not a classical Chinese incense material. It does not appear in the 香乘 canon as a burned ingredient, there is no established enfleurage or distillation tradition for its flowers, and peony is not a mainstream perfumery raw material — a rare specialty extract at most. What the trade sells as "peony oil" or "peony fragrance" is almost always a compounded accord, a created smell built from other materials rather than a distillate of *Paeonia lactiflora*.

The chemistry explains the commercial gap. A flower that yields vanishingly little oil, opens for a few weeks, and loses its volatiles on drying cannot support an essential-oil supply chain. Even a solvent extraction would need enormous quantities of fresh petals for a small yield, and the resulting absolute would be a seasonal specialty at best. The industry did not overlook peony; the plant did not offer a viable route.

So what does a formulator do when a brief says "peony"? The honest answer is that the brief is asking for a genre, not a source. A peony accord is typically built from rose alcohols, a clean floral lift, a green stem note and a soft powdery base, tuned to suggest a flower rather than reproduce one. That is a legitimate piece of perfumery. It becomes a problem only when the accord is presented as a botanical extract.

Burning actual petals is worse than useless. Fresh petals smoulder rather than burn, produce acrid smoke, and leave a tarry residue; dried petals flash and smell of scorched cellulose. There is also a safety point that cut-flower suppliers rarely mention: florist stems may carry pesticide residues and are often held in floral foam, so garden flowers are not automatically a clean burning material. Incense smoke of any kind carries particulate matter, and the general rule stands — burn in a well-ventilated space, keep smoke away from children and anyone with respiratory sensitivity, and never treat a smouldering flower as a fragrance delivery system.

## The root is a different product entirely

The root of *Paeonia lactiflora* is where the plant's commercial weight actually sits, and it is a materia-medica subject rather than a fragrance one. Two trade forms dominate. 白芍 (báisháo) is root that has been peeled and typically boiled before drying; 赤芍 (chìsháo) is root dried with its reddish outer bark intact. Classical and modern pharmacopoeial descriptions treat them as distinct articles with different prep, and 赤芍 may also be sourced from related peony species.

Traditional belief assigns 白芍 a nourishing, softening role in classical formulas and 赤芍 a more invigorating, blood-moving one. Those are traditional categories, stated here as traditional belief, not as clinical findings. No claim of efficacy is made on this page, and none should be inferred from the fact that a plant appears in an old book. The relevant facts for an encyclopedia are narrower: the root is a documented materia-medica article, it is prepared in defined ways, and it is not the flower.

Quality cues in the root trade are physical and unglamorous. Sound 白芍 is firm, heavy for its size, and cleanly peeled, with a pale, smooth surface; soft, light or musty pieces indicate poor drying or storage. 赤芍 retains a rough reddish-brown exterior and should be dry and crisp rather than leathery. Ageing, damp storage and insect damage are the usual failure modes, and they are detectable by hand and nose long before any laboratory is involved. Anyone considering a root preparation for health reasons should treat this page as background reading and consult a qualified practitioner — the materia medica is not a DIY manual.

## Why the incense canon is shorter than the garden

A useful question follows from all of this: if China had so many fragrant flowers, why does its incense canon look so narrow? The answer is not that the canon was blind to flowers. It is that a canon is a list of materials that survive storage, travel and transformation — and fresh flowers fail all three tests.

The filter runs like this: a material must be harvestable in quantity → stable enough to store for months without rotting or losing its scent → dense enough in volatile compounds to justify transport → and workable by the techniques a workshop actually has, whether grinding, moulding, distilling or ageing. Resins pass. Heartwoods pass. Roots and barks pass. A soft petal that wilts in a day and smells of water fails at the first step.

That is the structural reason 芍药 is absent. The flower is not obscure, and it was never neglected; it simply belongs to a different economy — the garden, the vase, the poem — where value is delivered fresh and locally. Incense is a preservation technology as much as a fragrance technology. It rewards materials whose smell can be banked.

This reframing matters for readers who go looking for a lost flower tradition. When a plant is famous and absent from the canon, the interesting question is rarely "what did they hide?" It is usually "what property did this material lack?" For 芍药 the missing property is keeping quality: the scent lives in delicate petal tissue, and the moment you try to bank it, it is gone. The same logic explains why so many canonical materials are woods, resins and roots, and so few are blossoms.

## Reading a peony label

"Peony" appears on at least five different kinds of product, and the word means something different in each. The table below is the one to keep open while shopping.

| Label says | What it usually is | What to expect | What would be wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Paeonia lactiflora* | The herbaceous peony plant | Herbaceous top growth, winter dieback, spring flowers | A woody trunk, or evergreen leaves |
| Tree peony / 牡丹 | *Paeonia × suffruticosa* | Woody shrub, flowers on old wood, bark used in materia medica | Dies to the ground in winter |
| Peony root / 白芍 / 赤芍 | Root material for the materia medica | Firm dried root, prepared or unpeeled | Petals, stems, or flowering plants |
| Peony fragrance oil | A compounded accord | A soft rosy-floral smell, consistent batch to batch | Any claim of natural extraction |
| Peony incense | A stick or cone scented with an accord | A floral top note over a base material | Visible petals sold as the fragrance source |

The logic behind the table is a single question: is the thing in front of me a plant, a plant part, or a smell? A plant has a growth habit you can check. A plant part has a physical form you can inspect. A smell has a formula, and the formula may contain nothing from *Paeonia* at all. Most bad buying decisions come from sliding between those categories — ordering a fragrance accord and expecting a botanical, or ordering a root and expecting flowers.

For a buyer, three checks cover most of the ground. Ask for the species name in writing. Ask which part of the plant the material comes from. Ask whether the fragrance is an essential oil, an absolute, or a compounded accord. A supplier who can answer all three is selling something identifiable; a supplier who answers "peony" to all three is selling a word.

## Growing and cutting notes

For a gardener, 芍药 is a long-lived, low-drama perennial provided two conditions are met: full sun and a real winter chill. Plant the crown shallow — deep planting is the classic reason a healthy-looking root refuses to flower — and expect the first season to be about establishment rather than display. Clumps can be left undisturbed for years; division is a renewal operation, not an annual chore, and it is best done when the plant is dormant.

For a cut-flower buyer, the useful checks are stem condition, bud stage and scent. Stems should be firm and green, not slimy at the cut end. Buds should be coloured and beginning to open, not hard green marbles, which may never open after a long cold chain. Scent, where a cultivar has any, is best judged in the morning on a freshly opened flower; a bunch that smells of nothing in the shop will not develop a scent at home.

The seasonal honesty is worth repeating. Field-grown 芍药 is a late-spring flower in the northern hemisphere. Supply outside that window comes from forced or imported production, and the fragrance — already cultivar-dependent — is the first thing to suffer. A consumer who wants the smell should buy in season, from a grower who can name the cultivar, and should not expect the cut stem to match the garden.

## Where the evidence ends

The evidence base for 芍药 is uneven, and separating the layers prevents most overclaims.

| Layer | Status | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established | *Paeonia lactiflora*, Paeoniaceae, herbaceous perennial; Flora of China and GBIF records |
| Classical name | Historically documented | 将离 recorded in the *Bencao Gangmu* 草之三, with the 溱洧 parting-gift association |
| Garden history | Historically documented | Long Chinese cultivation and extensive cultivar selection |
| Materia-medica root | Traditionally documented | 白芍 / 赤芍 as classical articles; traditional belief, not clinical evidence |
| Aromatic use as incense | Not established | No classical 香材 role, no established extraction tradition |
| Modern "peony" fragrance | Commercial fact | Compounded accords, not botanical extracts |

The pattern in that table is common across ornamental plants: strong identity evidence, strong cultural evidence, thin aromatic evidence. The temptation is to let the first two carry the third. This page does not. 芍药 is a garden flower with a beautiful name and a documented root; it is not an incense material, and saying so is more useful than inventing a tradition for it.

## FAQ

**Is 芍药 the same plant as 牡丹?** No. 芍药 is the herbaceous peony, *Paeonia lactiflora*, which dies back to the ground each winter. 牡丹 is the tree peony, *Paeonia × suffruticosa*, a woody shrub that keeps a trunk and branches. They are different species with different growth habits, and their materia-medica parts differ too — root for 芍药, root bark for 牡丹.

**Why is 芍药 called 将离?** 将离 means "about to part." The *Bencao Gangmu* records the name directly — "芍药一名将离" — and the *Shijing* poem 溱洧 ends with lovers giving each other 勺药, an old written form associated with the flower. The name records a farewell custom, not an aromatic or medicinal property.

**Does peony make a real essential oil?** Not as a mainstream perfumery material. The flowers are seasonal, delicate and low-yielding, and there is no established distillation tradition for *Paeonia lactiflora*; a rare specialty extract aside, products sold as peony oil are compounded fragrance oils or accords built to suggest the flower.

**Why do some peonies have no smell?** Scent varies by cultivar. Breeding has emphasised flower size, colour and doubling, and double forms convert stamens into petal-like tissue, which reduces the volatile-emitting surface. Cut stems also lose scent because they are cut in bud and open on stored reserves. A scentless peony is a breeding outcome, not a defective plant.

**Can I burn peony petals as incense?** You can, but you will not get peony scent. Fresh petals smoulder and produce acrid smoke; dried petals smell of scorched plant material. Cut flowers may also carry pesticide residues. If you burn anything, do it in a ventilated space and keep smoke away from children and anyone with breathing difficulties.

**Is peony used in traditional Chinese incense?** No classical 香材 role is documented for the flower. 芍药 belongs to the garden and to the materia medica through its root. Modern "peony incense" is a scented product built on an accord, and it should be described that way rather than as a botanical tradition.

## Summary

芍药 (*Paeonia lactiflora*) is the herbaceous Chinese peony: a Paeoniaceae perennial from central and eastern Asia, cultivated for over a millennium, and one of the oldest ornamentals in the Chinese garden. Its classical name 将离, recorded in the *Bencao Gangmu* and echoed in the *Shijing*, means "about to part" and preserves a farewell custom rather than an aromatic practice. The root is a documented materia-medica article in its 白芍 and 赤芍 forms, described here in traditional terms only. The flower's scent, where a cultivar has one, is a soft rosy-green affair that does not survive drying and does not support an essential-oil trade. 芍药 is a garden and cultural flower, distinct from the tree peony 牡丹, and its honest aromatic status is minimal.

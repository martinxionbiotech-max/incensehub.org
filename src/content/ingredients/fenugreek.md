> **Direct answer:** 葫芦巴 (húlúbā) names the small, hard seed of *Trigonella foenum-graecum*, a legume grown as an annual around the Mediterranean and across western Asia. Roasted, it gives off a warm maple-and-toast smell with a nutty, faintly celery edge; raw, it is mostly bitter. In the Chinese record it arrives late and is named as an import, and it never joined the classical incense canon.

## The plant: an annual legume, not a spice tree

> **Direct answer:** *Trigonella foenum-graecum* is a small annual herb in the legume family, grown for its seed. It has clover-like trifoliate leaves, small pale flowers and slender pods that hold the hard brown seeds sold as fenugreek.

The plant is unremarkable in the field and unmistakable in the kitchen. It grows as an upright annual of modest size, with three-part leaves that give it the clover look characteristic of many legumes. The flowers are small and pale, produced in the leaf axils, and the pods are long, thin and pointed — the look of a fodder legume, which is exactly what the name "Greek hay" records. Each pod carries a row of seeds that are hard, angular and yellowish-brown to reddish-brown.

The binomial is worth unpacking because it describes the plant before roasting rather than after. *Foenum-graecum* means "Greek hay", a name that refers to the smell of the green herb — hay-like, mild, fodder-sweet. That is a different smell from the maple note of the roasted seed, and the gap between the two is the single most important thing to understand about this material. The plant smells of hay; the roasted seed smells of caramel and maple; the two descriptions are both correct and they describe different stages.

Flora of China records the species as an introduced and cultivated plant in China rather than a native one, which fits the documentary picture: a crop that travelled with trade and cooking rather than a plant of the Chinese flora. It is grown widely now — North Africa, the Middle East, South Asia and China all produce it — and the seed moves through spice markets long before it reaches anyone interested in fragrance. That commercial identity as a food ingredient is not a footnote. It shapes the grades, the packaging, the moisture spec and the expectations that come with the material.

## The name as a passport: 葫芦巴 and the 胡 prefix

> **Direct answer:** 葫芦巴 (húlúbā) is a phonetic loan, and its first character 胡 is the classical Chinese marker for a foreign import. The same prefix appears in 胡荽 (coriander), 胡椒 (pepper) and 胡桃 (walnut), and it tells a reader that the naming culture regarded the material as coming from outside.

Chinese material names are compressed records, and this one is unusually legible. 葫芦巴 is not a descriptive compound in the way 桑叶 ("mulberry leaf") is. It is a transcription of a foreign word, written with characters chosen for sound rather than sense, and the 胡 at the front is the tell. In the materia-medica vocabulary, 胡 marks what came from the west or from beyond the frontier — the same marker that sits in front of coriander, pepper and walnut.

| Name | Material | Structure | What the prefix signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 葫芦巴 | Fenugreek | 胡 + phonetic syllables | A foreign seed, transcribed by sound |
| 胡荽 | Coriander | 胡 + 荽 | A western herb, named as an import |
| 胡椒 | Black pepper | 胡 + 椒 | A southern/western trade spice |
| 胡桃 | Walnut | 胡 + 桃 | A Central Asian nut |

The prefix is a signal, not a date. It tells you that the people who named the material perceived it as foreign; it does not tell you when it arrived, by which route, or whether an earlier name existed. Names can be folk-etymologised, re-spelled and reassigned, so the 胡 is best read as corroboration rather than proof. In this case the corroboration is unusually strong, because the *Bencao Gangmu* entry says the same thing in prose: the seed came from overseas.

There is a practical payoff for a reader who learns the pattern. A 胡 name is a prompt to check provenance before repeating a claim about classical use. It is not a verdict — some 胡-named materials were absorbed so early that the distinction between "imported" and "classical" becomes academic — but it is the right first question, and it costs nothing to ask.

## Why the seed smells of maple

> **Direct answer:** The maple note comes from sotolone, a potent aroma compound present in the seed. It reads as caramel, maple syrup and lightly burnt sugar, and it is strong enough that a small amount of roasted seed can scent a whole dish or a blend.

Sotolone — often written sotolon — is one of those compounds that behaves out of all proportion to its concentration. It is the reason a bag of roasted fenugreek smells sweet before anything has been added, and the reason the same bag can smell overpowering if it is left open in a small room. The note it produces is not generic sweetness. It has a distinct maple-caramel signature with a savoury undertone, which is why the seed reads as "maple syrup" to some noses and as "curry powder" to others depending on what else is in the air.

The raw seed is a different proposition. Bite one and the first sensation is bitterness, with a hay-like, slightly green follow-through and a lingering astringency. That bitterness is not a defect; it is the pre-roast state, and it belongs to the same seed that will smell sweet after a spell in a dry pan. The transformation is the material's defining process, and it is worth understanding as chemistry rather than as cookery.

Two things happen during roasting. Water leaves the seed, which concentrates everything else. Heat drives browning reactions between amino acids and sugars, producing toasted and roasted notes — the family of smells that includes bread crust, coffee and malt. At the same time, the sharp volatile fraction that carries the raw bitterness is driven off or altered, so the sotolone character comes forward instead of competing. **Raw seed → heat → moisture driven off and browning reactions under way → harsh top notes reduced → the maple-caramel note becomes the dominant impression.** That is the sequence in one line.

The maple note is also a register marker. Sweet, caramelised, food-adjacent smells belong to what fragrance writers call the gourmand family: the smells of kitchen and dessert rather than of forest and altar. Classical Chinese incense pursued a different set of values — clean, dry, woody, resinous, sometimes floral — and the gourmand register was not among them. This is the real reason fenugreek sits oddly in a classical frame, and it has nothing to do with whether the seed is good. It is a register mismatch, not a quality problem.

## Roasting is the material

> **Direct answer:** Roasting is not a preparation step that leaves the seed otherwise unchanged; it creates the aromatic material. The same seed, raw and roasted, gives two different products with different smells, different bitterness and different best uses.

Anyone buying fenugreek for fragrance is buying a roast result, whether or not the supplier says so. The variable to control is the curve: how hot, how long, how deep the colour goes, and how quickly the seed is cooled afterwards. A fast, hot roast can brown the outside while the inside stays raw and bitter; a slow, even roast develops the maple note through the whole seed. The difference is visible in the cross-section and audible in the bite.

| Roast level | Colour | Aroma | Risk | Sensible use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Pale tan to biscuit | Hay, faint maple, green edge | Raw bitterness survives | Savoury blends where maple should whisper |
| Medium | Chestnut brown, even | Maple, toast, nutty, mild bitterness | Easy to overshoot in a thin pan | The reference aromatic form |
| Dark | Deep brown, oily sheen | Strong toast, coffee, caramel | Burnt bitterness returns and masks the maple | Heavy savoury cooking, not fragrance |

The dark end deserves a warning because it is the most common mistake. Sugar and protein browning is pleasant up to a point and acrid past it, and the transition is fast in a pan that is hotter than the cook thinks. A dark-roasted seed can still smell impressive from a distance and taste harsh up close, and in a blend that harshness reads as a flaw in the material rather than as a flaw in the roast.

Cooling matters as much as heating. Seed left in a hot pan continues to cook after the heat is off, which is why an even roast can drift into the dark column while nobody is watching. Spread the seed to cool, and judge the colour in daylight rather than under a warm kitchen lamp. These are small procedural points, and they decide whether the batch lands in the middle column or the last one.

There is a second, quieter transformation that matters for storage. Roasted seed carries its aroma in an oil-rich matrix that is more stable than the raw seed's volatile fraction, but it is not inert. **Roasted seed → exposed to air and warmth → the toasted fraction oxidises → the maple note flattens and a stale, cardboard edge appears.** Whole seed resists this longer than ground, for the same surface-area reason that applies to every aromatic material.

## A late arrival, dated by its own entry

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), in its herb section 草之四, records 葫芦巴 as a recent item: it notes the seed came from overseas, that pre-Tang formulas do not use it, and that the older bencao does not list it. That is the source dating itself, not a modern inference.

The passage is short and unusually explicit. It notes that physicians of the compiler's own time used 葫芦巴 as an important medicine, then adds that it does not appear in pre-Tang formulas, that the earlier bencao does not record it, and concludes with 蓋是近出 — "probably a recent arrival". A separate phrase, 種出海南諸番, places the seed's origin overseas, in the foreign lands reached by the southern sea route.

Both phrases are doing provenance work rather than medical work, and that is how this page uses them. The first is a date: not in the older texts, not in pre-Tang formulas, therefore late. The second is a direction: not a Chinese native, but an import. Put together, they describe a material that entered Chinese use through trade, was absorbed into materia medica on its own merits, and never acquired the deep textual history that the classical 香材 carry.

That kind of self-dating is rare and genuinely useful. Most "was this classical?" questions have to be answered by inference from absence, which is always weak evidence — a text can be lost, a formula can be omitted, a name can change. Here the source makes the claim directly, which means the classification does not rest on an editorial guess. It rests on a sentence written by the compiler.

The distinction the entry draws is between "used" and "classical". 葫芦巴 was used, and used seriously, as a medicine. It was not part of the older canon, and it did not enter the classical incense repertoire. Those are compatible statements, and conflating them is the most common error in popular writing about later-arriving aromatics. A material can be old in use and recent in record; a material can be important in one domain and absent from another.

## How to test a "classical" claim

> **Direct answer:** Treat "classical" as a claim about documents, not about age or quality. Five checks — earlier formulas, earlier bencao, name morphology, the entry's own language, and incense manuals — will separate a documented classical material from a later arrival.

| Check | The question it asks | What a positive result suggests | Where it can mislead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-Tang formulas | Does the material appear in earlier formula collections? | An established earlier use | Surviving texts are a fraction of what existed |
| Earlier bencao | Does the older materia medica list it? | A documentary trail before the Ming | Entries can be lost, abridged or renamed |
| Name morphology | Does the name carry 胡, 番 or another import marker? | The naming culture saw it as foreign | Names drift and can be folk-etymologised |
| Entry language | Does the entry itself call the item recent? | Direct testimony from the compiler | Depends on edition and transmission |
| Incense manuals | Does it appear in incense-specific literature? | A place in the working canon | Manuals are selective and court-centred |

Applied to fenugreek, the checks converge. The name carries 胡. The entry says the seed came from overseas. The entry says the older bencao is silent and pre-Tang formulas do not use it. And the material does not appear in the incense literature as a classical ingredient. Four signals pointing the same way is about as clear as provenance gets in this field.

Applied to a material with no such entry, the same checks produce a weaker answer, and that is fine. The method's value is that it stops two common shortcuts: treating "old" as a synonym for "classical", and treating "not in the canon" as a synonym for "bad". Fenugreek is the demonstration case. It is an old crop, a serious materia-medica item and a fine spice, and it is still not a classical 香材. All of those can be true at once, and the discipline is to say so without upgrading one claim into another.

## Where it belongs in a blend

> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek belongs at the warm, sweet edge of a composition, where it adds a maple-toast note that no wood or resin supplies. It does not belong at the base, and it does not belong in a blend that is trying to sound classical.

The gourmand register is easy to recognise and hard to place. It reads as kitchen: caramel, toast, malt, dried fruit, coffee. In a modern fragrance composition that register can be the point of the whole piece, and fenugreek is a legitimate way to reach it from a plant material rather than from an isolate. In a classical-style composition it is an intruder, because the classical palette did not pursue edible sweetness. The same note that makes a modern blend interesting makes a classical reconstruction sound wrong.

Within the gourmand register, the seed behaves like a middle note with a strong opening. The maple note is vivid when the material is fresh and exposed, then settles into a softer toastiness. It has some fixative company — resins and balsams hold it — but on its own it does not anchor a composition. A practical placement is a small proportion over a resin or wood base, where the maple note reads as a warm facet rather than as the subject.

The dose question is the one that decides success. Fenugreek is potent, and potency is not the same as usefulness. **A small dose → a maple warmth that rounds the blend → the composition reads sweeter without reading edible. A large dose → the maple note dominates → the blend reads as flavouring rather than as fragrance.** The line between those outcomes is narrow, and it is found by dilution series rather than by percentage advice, because the starting material varies with roast level and age.

For the wider comparison of how materials sit against one another in a formulation, see the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/). For the vocabulary of registers used across the site, see the [scent guide](/blog/scent-guide/).

A classical reconstruction is a different kind of project, and fenugreek is the wrong tool for it. The classical palette was built from woods, resins, barks and a small set of florals, and its sweetness — where sweetness appears at all — comes from resins and balsams rather than from caramelised sugar. Dropping a maple note into that structure does not modernise it; it reads as an ingredient from another kitchen. A formulator who wants warmth in a classical register is better served by the materials the register already contains, and a formulator who wants maple should build a modern composition where maple is welcome.

## Cooking and fragrance are different briefs

> **Direct answer:** A cook wants the whole seed — bitterness, body, maple note and all — because those elements balance against fat, acid and starch. A fragrance brief wants only the volatile fraction, in a dose small enough that the bitterness never surfaces. The same material is being asked for two different things.

That difference explains a confusion that shows up whenever a food spice crosses into fragrance. A seed that is excellent in a curry can be a poor aromatic material, not because it is lower quality but because the qualities that make it good in food are the ones a blend cannot use. Bitterness, astringency and a heavy toasted body are assets on a plate and liabilities in a sachet. Judging the spice by its culinary reputation and then wondering why the blend smells harsh is a brief error, not a material error.

The reverse also holds. A very light, pale roast that keeps a clean maple top note may taste thin and underdeveloped in cooking, because the browning that a cook wants has not happened yet. Neither roast is wrong. They are answers to different questions, and a supplier who serves both markets is usually running two roast profiles rather than one compromise profile.

For a buyer, the useful move is to state the brief before discussing the sample. "I need a maple top note at low dose in a resin base" and "I need a spice that will stand up to slow cooking" will produce different seeds, different roast levels and different conversations. Saying which one you mean costs a sentence and saves a shipment.

## Mucilage: the half of the seed that is not aroma

> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek seed carries a substantial mucilage fraction — a water-soluble gum that swells when wetted. It contributes nothing to the smell, and it changes how a ground sample behaves in any wet application.

The seed's two halves behave differently. The aromatic half is volatile, heat-generated and short-lived. The mucilage half is stable, water-loving and structural. Ground fenugreek stirred into water thickens within minutes as the gum hydrates and the mixture turns slick and slightly ropy. That is not a defect and not an impurity; it is the same property that makes the seed useful as a thickener in cooking.

For anyone working with the material in a paste, the consequence is practical. A powder that gels on contact with water will bind free water in a dough, change the workability of the mass, and alter how the paste dries. **Ground seed → water added → mucilage hydrates → free water becomes bound → the paste stiffens earlier than an inert powder would.** A formulator who treats fenugreek as a drop-in aromatic and doses it by smell alone may find the batch behaving differently from the trial, because the trial used a different proportion of the same seed.

There is a second consequence for storage. Mucilage-rich powder is hygroscopic, and a damp powder is a powder that will cake, clump and eventually support mould. The same water-loving chemistry that makes the seed useful in a wet mix makes it demanding in a dry store. Keep it sealed, keep it dry, and treat a clumped sample as a warning rather than a nuisance to break up.

The wider lesson is that an aromatic material is never only its aroma. Seed coats, gums, starches and oils all influence how a material handles, how it ages and how it behaves in a formula. Reading a material by smell alone is like reading a book by its cover — accurate about one thing, silent about the rest.

## Storage and the roast clock

> **Direct answer:** The aromatic clock on fenugreek starts when the seed leaves the pan, not when it leaves the field. Raw seed stores for a long time; roasted seed and roasted powder decline from the day they are made.

That distinction is easy to miss because the material looks stable. A seed is hard, dry and apparently inert, and a jar of it can sit on a shelf for a year without visible change. The change is in the headspace, not in the appearance. Open the jar after six months and the maple note has thinned into a flat, slightly dusty toastiness, while the seed itself looks exactly as it did.

Three variables govern the decline. Heat speeds it, light accelerates the oxidation of the aromatic fraction, and air carries the volatiles away and brings oxygen in. The remedy is unglamorous: a sealed container, a cool dark place, and a batch size matched to the rate of use. Whole roasted seed keeps measurably better than ground, for the surface-area reason that governs every aromatic solid. **Ground roasted seed → more exposed surface → faster exchange with the headspace → the top note leaves first → what remains is the heavy, slightly bitter base.**

A practical habit is to date the roast, not the purchase. A jar labelled with the roasting date tells you where the material is in its life; a jar labelled with a supplier's batch code tells you nothing you can use. If the seed was bought already roasted, the purchase date is the best proxy available, and it is worth writing down.

Re-roasting old seed is a tempting fix and a poor one. A second pass drives off what little aromatic fraction remains, darkens the seed further, and pushes the bitterness forward. The result smells toasted and tastes harsh — a worse material than the stale seed it started as. Old roasted seed is best retired to the kitchen, where its remaining toastiness can still do some work in a stew.

## Forms and handling

> **Direct answer:** Three forms reach the market: the raw seed, the seed after roasting, and the roasted seed ground to powder. The raw seed is bitter and stable; the roasted seed carries the aroma; the powder is convenient and short-lived.

| Form | Aroma | Shelf behaviour | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole raw seed | Hay, green, bitter | Very stable; months to years if dry | Roasting in-house; spice use |
| Whole roasted seed | Maple, toast, nutty | Good while sealed; fades slowly | The reference aromatic form |
| Ground roasted powder | Strong at first, then flat | Poor; loses the top note quickly | Immediate use in blends |
| Ground raw powder | Bitter, hay | Stable but unpromising | Preparations that will be cooked |

The pattern is the usual one for aromatic solids, with a twist specific to this seed: the aroma is created by heat, so the material's quality clock starts at the roaster rather than at the harvest. Raw seed can sit for a long time without much change. Roasted seed begins to decline as soon as it cools, and ground roasted seed declines fastest of all.

That has a handling consequence. If a blend needs roasted fenugreek, the sensible sequence is to buy raw seed, roast it in the quantity needed, cool it, grind it if necessary, and use it soon. Buying pre-ground roasted powder means buying a material that has already spent part of its aromatic life in someone else's warehouse, with no way to judge how much is left.

## Quality signals

> **Direct answer:** Judge fenugreek on the seed first and the roast second. Sound, dry, uniform seed with no mustiness is the base; an even chestnut roast with a clean maple note and no burnt edge is the result to look for.

| Signal | What to look for | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| Soundness | Hard, dry, uniform seed; no broken or dusty fraction | Storage damage and insect activity |
| Smell, raw | Hay and mild bitterness, no mustiness | Mould and stale stock |
| Colour, raw | Even yellowish-brown to reddish-brown | Bleaching, age, uneven lots |
| Roast evenness | Consistent colour through the batch | Hot spots and raw centres |
| Aroma, roasted | Clean maple and toast, no acrid edge | Over-roast and scorching |
| Moisture | Dry to the touch, free-running | Mould risk in storage |

The raw seed's bitterness is not a fault and should not be graded as one. It is the starting condition, and a sample that is not bitter before roasting is more likely to have been processed than to be superior. The meaningful comparison is always roasted against roasted, at the same roast level, because a dark roast and a light roast are different products.

Uniformity is the signal buyers underrate. A batch with mixed roast levels will smell muddled — some seeds contributing raw hay, others contributing dark toast — and no amount of blending skill fixes a muddled base. Ask for a photograph of the roasted seed spread out, or ask for a sample and look at it in daylight. Colour variation across the batch is visible and it is diagnostic.

## Seed spices compared

> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek, cumin, coriander and fennel are all sold as seed spices, and they sit in four different aromatic registers. Fenugreek is the legume with the maple note; the other three are carrot-family seeds with savoury, citrus and anise characters respectively.

| | Fenugreek (葫芦巴) | Cumin (孜然) | Coriander seed (芫荽籽) | Fennel (小茴香) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family | Fabaceae | Apiaceae | Apiaceae | Apiaceae |
| Species | *Trigonella foenum-graecum* | *Cuminum cyminum* | *Coriandrum sativum* | *Foeniculum vulgare* |
| Character | Maple, toast, nutty-bitter | Earthy, warm, savoury | Citrus, woody, light | Anise, sweet, green |
| Best form | Roasted | Dry-roasted | Dry-roasted or raw | Raw or lightly toasted |
| Register | Gourmand | Savoury-warm | Bright spice | Sweet-anise |

Three of the four are close botanical relatives and one is not, which is a useful reminder that culinary grouping and botanical grouping are different things. Cumin, coriander and fennel all belong to the carrot family and share the umbellifer habit — fine leaves, umbels of small flowers, ribbed seeds. Fenugreek is a legume with a totally different plant form, a different seed chemistry and a different aromatic outcome. The shelf groups them because cooks use them together, not because they are alike.

The practical consequence is that substitution between them fails in different ways. Cumin for fenugreek loses the maple note and adds a savoury earthiness that reads as the wrong cuisine. Coriander for fenugreek loses the sweetness and adds citrus. Fennel for fenugreek replaces maple with anise, which is a different kind of sweetness altogether. See [cumin](/ingredients/cumin/), [coriander seed](/ingredients/coriander-seed/) and [fennel](/ingredients/fennel/) for the individual entries.

## Tradition, record and evidence

> **Direct answer:** The materia-medica record is traditional, and this page treats it as history rather than as clinical evidence. What is established is the botanical identity, the Fabaceae placement, the role of sotolone in the aroma, and the late, foreign-origin arrival described in the *Bencao Gangmu*.

| Layer | Status | What it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established | Species-level sourcing and specification |
| Aromatic chemistry | Established compound, editorial attribution | The maple note's origin |
| Traditional use | Historically documented | Cultural and historical context |
| Health claims | Not established | Nothing on this page |

The interesting layer for a reader of incense material pages is the second one. Sotolone is a real compound with a real sensory effect, and it explains the material's behaviour without requiring any claim about benefit. That is a useful kind of explanation: it accounts for the smell, predicts how the seed will behave when heated, and stops short of anything the evidence cannot carry.

Traditional use deserves the same care in the other direction. The *Bencao Gangmu* entry is a document of its time, written in the vocabulary of its time, and reading it as a modern instruction is a category error. It is evidence that the material was used and how it was classified; it is not evidence that a modern trial would find what the entry asserts. This page quotes the entry for provenance and makes no therapeutic claim.

## Sourcing checks

> **Direct answer:** Buy raw seed if you can roast it yourself, and judge roasted seed by evenness and aroma rather than by grade names. Confirm species, crop, roast state and moisture, and treat culinary grade as a starting point rather than an aromatic specification.

1. **Confirm the binomial.** *Trigonella foenum-graecum*. Fenugreek has few close lookalikes in trade, but the name on the document should still be the species.
2. **Ask whether the seed is raw or roasted.** The two are different products. A listing that does not say is a listing that has not thought about it.
3. **Ask the crop year.** Seed keeps well, but old seed oxidises and the toasted note goes stale.
4. **Ask for the roast level in words.** Light, medium, dark — and ask for a photograph. "Roasted" alone covers a wide range.
5. **Check uniformity in the sample.** Mixed colour means mixed roast, which means a muddled blend.
6. **Smell for acridity.** A burnt edge is the most common defect and the easiest to detect.
7. **Check moisture.** Free-running, dry seed; clumping suggests moisture or oil migration.
8. **Ask about food-grade versus aromatic-grade handling.** A culinary supply chain is not automatically a fragrance supply chain, and the assumptions differ.

There is no published grading ladder for fenugreek as an aromatic material, so the sample carries the argument. The questions above are designed to make a supplier commit to statements that a sample can confirm or contradict, which is a stronger position than accepting a grade word that nobody defines.

## Safety

Burning plant material produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep burning material away from children and pets. Ground fenugreek is a fine powder and raises dust when handled; measure it away from the face and avoid breathing the dust. Whole seed is a choking hazard for small children and should be stored out of reach. Culinary-grade seed is sold as food, but a material handled in a fragrance or incense context is not automatically food-safe after storage and blending, and it should not be eaten from a workshop container. This page is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice; see the [safety guide](/safety/).

## FAQ

**What is fenugreek (葫芦巴), and why is it also written 胡芦巴?** The seed of *Trigonella foenum-graecum*, an annual legume from the Mediterranean and western Asia. Raw, it is bitter and hay-like; roasted, it develops a warm maple-caramel note with a nutty, faintly celery edge. It is a food spice first and a fragrance accent second. The two spellings are both phonetic transcriptions of the same loanword, and the 胡 at the front is the foreign-origin marker; the variation is a spelling difference, not a different material.

**Is fenugreek a classical Chinese incense ingredient?** No. The *Bencao Gangmu* records 葫芦巴 in 草之四 as a recent item, notes that the seed came from overseas, and says that pre-Tang formulas do not use it and the older bencao does not list it. Later use as a medicine does not make a material classical.

**Why does fenugreek smell like maple syrup, and does roasting make it milder?** The note comes from sotolone, a potent aroma compound in the seed. Roasting develops it and reduces the raw bitterness that would otherwise compete with it. That reads as milder, but the total aromatic impact goes up: the maple note is more noticeable than the raw seed's hay smell, and an over-roasted seed turns harsh rather than mild. "Milder" describes the bitterness, not the strength.

**Can fenugreek be used as a base note in incense?** Not well. It has a strong opening and a soft middle, but no fixative power, and it does not hold other materials in place. It works as a warm facet over a resin or wood base, in a small dose, rather than as the foundation of a composition. The ground seed also thickens when wetted, because its mucilage swells on contact with water; in a wet mix it binds free water and stiffens the mass, so it should be tested in the actual formulation rather than assumed to behave like an inert powder.

**Is fenugreek the same as cumin?** No. Fenugreek is a legume with a maple-toast note; cumin is a carrot-family seed with an earthy, savoury character. They are grouped on spice shelves and in spice blends, but they belong to different families, different registers and different uses.

## Summary

Fenugreek (葫芦巴) is the seed of *Trigonella foenum-graecum*, an annual legume native to the Mediterranean and western Asia. Its defining process is roasting, which converts a bitter, hay-like raw seed into a sweet, maple-like, nutty material through browning reactions and the release of sotolone. The *Bencao Gangmu* dates the material in its own words — 唐已前方不見用, 本草不著, 蓋是近出 — and places its origin overseas, which makes fenugreek a documented later arrival rather than a classical 香材. It is a legitimate warm accent in modern fragrance and a poor fit for classical reconstruction, and the distinction between those two uses is the practical point of the entry.

> **Direct answer:** 金银花 (jīnyínhuā) names the dried buds and blossoms of *Lonicera japonica*, a climbing vine in the Caprifoliaceae family that originated in East Asia. The scent is sweet, green and honeyed; the historical record is one of herbal medicine, not of incense. It appears nowhere in the classical compendium 香乘, and its role in modern blends is a crossover from the herb trade.

## What 金银花 is, and what it is not

> **Direct answer:** It is a flower — the bud and blossom of a common climbing vine — sold through herb channels and burned for the sweet scent it carries. It is not a resin, not a heartwood, and not a canonical incense material.

The fastest way to misunderstand honeysuckle is to treat it as a rare aromatic that happens to be pretty. The plant is a vigorous vine, the flower is abundant, and the supply chain is agricultural rather than exotic. That has consequences for how the material should be judged, priced and used.

Three facts do most of the work on this page:

- **Identity.** The species is *Lonicera japonica*, not one of the many other honeysuckles in the genus.
- **Part.** What enters trade is the flower — bud or blossom — not the vine, leaf or root.
- **Pedigree.** The flower belongs to the materia-medica record; it does not belong to the classical incense canon.

The last point is where most writing about this material goes soft. A page can either repeat that 金银花 is "an ancient incense herb" or admit that the incense use is recent and borrowed. The second choice is less flattering and more useful. A reader who knows the material is a crossover will dose it as a floral accent, not as the spine of a formula, and will not pay a canonical-material price for a hedgerow flower.

There is also an editorial point worth stating plainly: a material does not need a Ming-dynasty pedigree to be worth burning. It needs a reliable identity, a clean lot, and a clear idea of what it can and cannot do in a blend. Honeysuckle passes the first two tests easily and the third only in a narrow role.

## Two names, one vine

> **Direct answer:** 忍冬 (rěndōng) is the plant; 金银花 (jīnyínhuā) is its flower. The two names describe one species, *Lonicera japonica*, at different levels of the same organism.

Chinese naming here is unusually tidy once the distinction is clear. 忍冬 — literally "endures winter" — names the vine, a semievergreen climber that keeps foliage through mild winters. 金银花 — "gold-silver flower" — names the bloom, and the name records what the flower does: it opens white and fades to yellow, so a flowering vine carries both colours at once.

That colour shift is the visible signature of the species, and it is also a harvest clock. A stem of paired buds that are still pale will read one way; the same stem a few days later, with one bloom turned gold, reads another. The name is not decorative. It is a field note compressed into three characters.

English adds confusion that Chinese avoids. "Honeysuckle" is a genus-wide word covering well over a hundred *Lonicera* species, from fragrant climbers to shrubs with little scent. A supplier who says "honeysuckle" has named a genus. A supplier who says *Lonicera japonica* has named a species. Only the second statement is a specification.

The naming history has one more layer that matters to anyone reading classical herbals. The old literature tends to enter the plant under 忍冬 and describe the flower within that entry, because the vine was the drug and the flower was one part of it. Modern trade reverses the emphasis: the flower is the product, and the vine is a by-product or a separate stem drug. That inversion explains why searching for "金银花" in a classical text can come up empty even though the plant is discussed at length under another name.

## What Flora of China records

> **Direct answer:** The species is a twining, semievergreen climber with branches that hollow as they age. Its leaf blades run 3–8 cm by 1.5–4 cm and are ovate to lanceolate; its fragrant flowers sit in pairs and carry a two-lipped corolla of 3–5 cm, white at first and yellow later.

The botanical description is worth keeping on hand because it settles most identification arguments without a laboratory:

- **Habit.** A twining climber, semievergreen, with branches that become hollow as they age.
- **Leaves.** Ovate, oblong or lanceolate, 3–8 cm long and 1.5–4 cm wide.
- **Flowers.** Fragrant, paired, sitting in the leaf axils, with a two-lipped (bilabiate) corolla 3–5 cm long.
- **Colour.** White at opening, ageing to yellow; some forms show purple on the outside.
- **Part in trade.** The flower.

Two of those details carry practical weight. The paired arrangement means each node produces two blooms, so a picked stem yields flowers in twos — a small thing that becomes visible when you inspect a lot for evenness. The corolla length, 3–5 cm, is a useful sanity check against substitute species with shorter or longer tubes.

The measurements are identification aids, not grades. A lot with slightly short corollas is not "low quality" if the species is right and the drying is clean. Confusing a taxonomic key with a quality scale is one of the commoner mistakes in material writing, and it leads buyers to reject sound material for the wrong reason.

For the full description, see [Lonicera japonica in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200022324) and the occurrence record on [GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/5334240).

## Origin, naturalisation and the trade behind the flower

> **Direct answer:** The species is native to China, Japan and Korea and is now widely naturalised beyond that range. Its commercial supply follows the herb and materia-medica trade, which is why lots vary more in drying and storage than in rarity.

*Lonicera japonica* is a plant of edges: thickets, forest margins, roadsides, garden fences. That habit explains both its range and its reputation. In its native East Asia it has been collected and cultivated for so long that wild and planted populations blur. Outside Asia it has escaped cultivation widely, and in parts of North America it is managed as an invasive climber rather than grown as a crop.

For a buyer, the ecology translates into a supply fact: this is not a material with a scarcity story. Nobody is poaching honeysuckle. The flower is picked in quantity, dried in quantity, and sold through the same channels that move chrysanthemum, 菊花 and other herbal flowers. Prices track labour and drying quality far more than they track the plant.

That has an odd consequence for quality control. In the agarwood trade, the buyer's first question is whether the material is what it claims to be, because the price gap between real and fake is enormous. With honeysuckle, the price gap is small, so the incentive to adulterate is weak. The risk shifts to carelessness: a lot dried too hot, stored too damp, or held too long will be genuine *Lonicera japonica* and still smell like hay.

What follows from this is a different buying posture. Spend the attention on the condition of the lot, not on the romance of the species. Ask how it was dried, how it has been stored, and how old it is. Those questions predict the experience of burning it far better than the country of origin does.

## The scent: honeyed, green, and shorter than it looks

> **Direct answer:** Honeysuckle smells sweet and honeyed with a green, slightly herbaceous edge — a light, airy floral rather than a heavy white-flower scent. Its impact sits at the top of a burn, which means it announces itself early and recedes early.

The aroma is best described in three layers. The first is sweetness, soft and rounded rather than sugary. The second is a green note, close to crushed stem and fresh leaf, which keeps the sweetness from turning cloying. The third is a faint honeyed warmth that arrives after the green fades and gives the flower its characteristic "golden" impression.

That word "honeyed" needs a caution. It describes a smell, not a content. Honeysuckle flowers are not honey, and the note is an olfactory resemblance rather than a chemical one. Tasting sweetness in a scent is a human perceptual habit, not evidence about sugars in the material.

Compared to its neighbours in the floral cabinet, honeysuckle occupies a bright, shallow register:

| Floral | Family | Register | Behaviour in a blend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honeysuckle 金银花 | Caprifoliaceae | Sweet, green, honeyed, light | Lifts the opening; fades early |
| Jasmine 茉莉 | Oleaceae | Sweet, indolic, heavy | Dominates the heart; needs restraint |
| Osmanthus 桂花 | Oleaceae | Apricot-sweet, fruity | Rounds the middle; adds body |
| Chrysanthemum 菊花 | Asteraceae | Cool, leafy, faintly bitter | Adds a dry herbal edge |

The practical consequence is a matter of timing. A top-note material is most legible in the first minutes of a burn and least legible at the end, so a formula that leans on honeysuckle for identity will feel front-loaded. That is not a defect; it is the nature of a light floral. It does mean that anyone expecting the flower to carry a whole stick will be disappointed, and anyone using it to brighten an opening will get exactly what they paid for.

## Harvest stage decides the lot

> **Direct answer:** The stage at which the flower is picked — tight bud, just-opening bud, or full bloom — changes colour, scent and behaviour in a blend more than most buyers expect. Bud-stage material is the conventional choice; open blooms are cheaper and flatter.

This is the single most useful quality lens for honeysuckle, and it is almost never discussed in retail listings. The same vine, picked a week apart, produces materially different goods.

| Stage | What you see | Scent character | Typical use | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tight bud | Slender, pale green-white, closed | Green, fresh, faint honey | Premium floral lots; materia-medica convention | Under-dried buds mould at the base |
| Just opening | One end loosening, white | Sweet, green, honeyed — the fullest register | Best all-round incense material | Narrow window; easily missed |
| Full bloom | Open corolla, white to gold | Sweeter, flatter, hay-like edge | Cheap filler lots | Petals shatter in handling; colour dulls |
| Stem and leaf | Green matter mixed in | Herbal, weak, slightly bitter | Nothing — it is dilution | Raises ash, muddies the note |

The convention in the herb trade leans toward buds and just-opening flowers. That is partly a materia-medica convention and partly practical: a closed bud holds its volatiles and survives handling, while an open corolla sheds petals into the bag and loses its top notes before it reaches the mill.

There is an honest limit to this table. It describes a direction, not a scoring system, and no published ladder converts bud percentage into a grade. A lot with a high proportion of just-opening flowers and clean colour is a good lot. A lot that is mostly gold, open and dusty is a cheap lot. The judgement is made on sight and smell, not on a certificate.

## Drying: the quiet step that fixes quality

> **Direct answer:** Drying is where a good picking becomes a good lot or a heap of hay. The flower is thin, wet and heat-sensitive, so the drier must remove water quickly enough to prevent mould without driving off the very volatiles the buyer is paying for.

The mechanism is worth following step by step, because every failure in dried honeysuckle traces back to it:

**Fresh blossom, mostly water by weight → thin petals lose moisture fast at the surface → the interior stays damp longer than the outside looks → a hot, fast dry seals a case-hardened surface while the core remains wet → the core moulds in storage → the whole lot smells musty, and no amount of re-drying removes the note.**

The opposite error is just as common. A slow, cool, humid dry avoids the burnt-hay smell but gives mould the time it needs, especially in a rainy season. The workable middle is a moderate, moving-air dry that keeps the petals below the point where volatiles flash off while keeping moisture low enough that mould cannot establish.

Three practical signals separate a well-dried lot from a badly dried one:

1. **Colour.** Pale green-white to soft yellow, reasonably even. Brown edges mean heat damage; grey patches mean mould.
2. **Texture.** Crisp but not brittle. A flower that crumbles to dust at a touch was over-dried; one that bends without breaking is under-dried.
3. **Smell.** Sweet and green when cold, fuller when warmed in the hand. A flat hay smell that does not lift on warming is a lot that lost its top notes in the drier.

Storage continues the same logic. Dried flowers are hygroscopic and will pull moisture from humid air, and they take up odours from whatever sits beside them. A sealed container in the dark, away from spices and cleaning products, is not fussiness — it is the difference between a material that still smells like honeysuckle in six months and one that smells like a cupboard.

## Milling, binder and the problem of a light flower

> **Direct answer:** Honeysuckle powder is light, low in resin and low in density, so it neither burns well on its own nor stays evenly distributed in a dry blend. It needs a base and a binder, and both of those mute the note it was bought to provide.

Milling a flower is not a neutral act. The same material behaves differently as whole flowers, as coarse fluff, and as fine powder, and the difference is mechanical before it is aromatic:

**Light floral powder → low bulk density and no resin to hold a coal → the powder needs a wood or makko base to keep burning → the base and binder together raise the smoke load → the delicate honeyed top note is buried under the base → the maker adds more flower to compensate → the stick becomes dusty and weak.**

That loop is the most common formulation failure with this material. The intuitive fix — more honeysuckle — makes the physical problem worse without recovering the scent. The better fix is structural: keep the flower fraction modest, choose a base with a clean burn, and accept that a light floral is a seasoning rather than a fuel.

Grind size matters for a second reason. Fine floral powder tends to float and segregate in a dry mix, so a stick can vary from one end to the other even when the batch was weighed correctly. A coarser cut, or a brief re-mix before rolling, produces a more even result. The general mechanics belong to the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process, and the behaviour of floral powders is treated in [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## Where honeysuckle sits in a formula

> **Direct answer:** It is a top note and a brightener — a material that opens a floral blend and then steps back. It pairs well with citrus, osmanthus and light woods, and badly with heavy resins that swallow it.

Think of the formula as a timeline rather than a recipe. In the first minute, the most volatile materials speak. In the middle, the heart materials take over. At the end, the resins and woods remain. Honeysuckle lives almost entirely in the first phase, which tells you both where to put it and how much to expect from it.

Useful pairings follow from that position:

- **Citrus peel** — shares the bright, volatile register, so the two reinforce rather than compete.
- **Osmanthus** — adds apricot body just behind the honeysuckle, extending the floral impression without adding weight.
- **Sandalwood or a light wood base** — gives the top note something to sit on, so the transition out of the opening is less abrupt.
- **Green herbs such as 藿香 or a light 艾** — reinforces the green edge, useful in cooling summer blends.

Poor pairings are equally predictable. A heavy resin such as myrrh or a dense agarwood will dominate within seconds, and the honeysuckle will read as a brief sweetness at the light-off rather than a component of the blend. That is not a fault in either material. It is a mismatch of timescales.

Dose is the question every maker asks and no page can answer honestly in grams. The right fraction depends on the base, the binder, the format and the target intensity, and a figure that works in one workshop does not transfer to another. The workable approach is to fix the base and binder first, then raise the flower in small steps until it is legible at the opening without making the stick dusty.

## Which format suits a light floral

> **Direct answer:** A gently warmed sachet or an electric warmer preserves honeysuckle best; a stick gives a brief bright opening; a coil wastes it; a cone tends to bury it. The format decides how much of the note ever reaches the nose.

There are two different questions here, and mixing them up costs money. The first is how to judge a lot: what does this material actually smell like? The second is how to deliver it: what will a customer experience when it burns? Combustion answers the second question and corrupts the first, because smoke and char sit on top of a delicate floral and hide it.

For judging, warm the material without setting it alight — a few flowers in a closed jar held in the hand, or a spoon on a low warmer. The green-honeyed note should appear within a minute. That is the material's own voice. Everything after that is the format talking.

| Format | How the flower is exposed | What survives | What is lost | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sachet, unheated | Ambient air through cloth | Soft honeyed-green over days to weeks | Intensity; fades quietly | Good for linen and small rooms, not a burn |
| Electric warmer | Low, even heat | The truest floral reading | Nothing much; the note simply thins | The best way to evaluate a lot |
| Stick | Binder and base carry the coal | A bright opening, then base | The middle and the close | Workable if the flower fraction stays modest |
| Cone | Dense core, hot centre | The base and the char | Most of the top note | A poor match for a delicate flower |
| Coil | Long, slow, base-heavy burn | A brief opening | Hours of burn with no flower left | Wasteful for this material |
| Loose on charcoal | Direct, hot, immediate | A sudden intense floral | Everything within seconds | Dramatic, hard to control |

The table explains a pattern that shows up in reviews of floral incense: people describe the same material as either lovely or faint, and both are right. The stick that opens beautifully fades by the second minute; the cone never opens at all. Neither result tells you anything about the quality of the flower, only about the physics of the format.

My editorial view is that honeysuckle should be sold and used as a warming material before it is used as a burning one. A maker who has only ever smelled it in a stick has not met the flower. A maker who has warmed a clean lot knows what the blend is supposed to protect, and can tell when a base is drowning it.

## 金银花, 山银花 and the wider Lonicera genus

> **Direct answer:** 金银花 is *Lonicera japonica*. 山银花 is a separate drug name covering other *Lonicera* species, and 忍冬藤 is the stem. The three are related, differently used, and routinely confused in trade.

This is the identification issue that matters most in the Chinese herb market, and it barely surfaces in English-language incense writing.

| Name | Species | Part | Why it gets confused | What it changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 金银花 | *Lonicera japonica* | Flower bud or blossom | The default name for honeysuckle flower | The reference material for this page |
| 山银花 | Several *Lonicera* species, including *L. macranthoides* and *L. confusa* | Flower | Sold under the same loose name in some channels | Different species; not the same drug |
| 忍冬藤 | *Lonicera japonica* | Stem and leaf | Shares the plant name | Herbal stem material, not a floral note |
| "Honeysuckle" | Any *Lonicera* species | Any part | English genus-wide word | Says nothing about species or part |

The split between 金银花 and 山银花 is a pharmacopoeial distinction that followed a long period of trade conflation. For an incense buyer the practical takeaway is simple: a label that says "honeysuckle flower" has not told you the species, and a price that looks unusually good may reflect a different *Lonicera* rather than a bargain on the same one.

None of this makes 山银花 worthless. It makes it a different material with a different name, and the honest position is to keep the names apart rather than to treat one as a counterfeit of the other. Where the species cannot be confirmed, the safe description is "*Lonicera* flower", not *Lonicera japonica*.

## Screening a lot: a practical sequence

> **Direct answer:** Screen honeysuckle in a fixed order — species and part, then bud proportion, colour, cleanliness, smell and moisture — because each step rules out a different failure. No single test replaces the sequence.

A workable sequence for a sample on the bench:

1. **Confirm the claim.** Whole flowers or powder? Species stated? If the answer is "honeysuckle", ask which *Lonicera*.
2. **Look at the stage mix.** Roughly how much is tight bud, how much just-opening, how much full gold bloom? The mix predicts the scent before you smell it.
3. **Check colour evenness.** Pale green-white through soft yellow is normal. Brown edges, grey patches or a dull uniform tan are not.
4. **Sort a handful.** Stems, leaves and dust are dilution. A small amount is unavoidable in an agricultural lot; a lot that is half stem is a different product.
5. **Smell cold, then warm.** Crush a few flowers in the palm and wait a moment. The green-honeyed note should lift. A flat hay smell that stays flat is a drying failure.
6. **Test moisture.** A crisp flower that snaps is dry; one that bends is not. Powder that clumps in the bag is holding water.
7. **Look for mould.** Grey fuzz at the base of buds is the classic sign of a lot that was dried too slowly and stored too long.

Two boundaries are worth stating. First, this sequence screens condition, not species — separating *L. japonica* from *L. macranthoides* reliably needs botanical knowledge or a laboratory, and a nose cannot do it. Second, there is no numerical grade to appeal to. "Grade A" on a honeysuckle invoice is a seller's label, not a standard, and it should be read as a claim to be checked rather than a fact.

## Storage: the slow leak

> **Direct answer:** Dried honeysuckle loses its top note to air, light, heat and moisture long before it looks spoiled. Whole flowers in a sealed, dark container hold their scent; powder in a folded paper bag does not.

The flower is hygroscopic, which means it behaves like a small sponge for water vapour and for smells. Two failures follow. The first is moisture: a lot that was crisp in autumn can be soft by spring if it sat in a humid room, and soft material is a mould risk, not just a texture problem. The second is odour transfer: stored beside spices, soap or a bag of camphor, the flowers will pick up those notes and never give them back.

The mechanism is straightforward:

**Ground flower → a far larger surface is exposed to air → volatiles escape and the residual oil oxidises → the powder smells strong for a week and flat by the end of the season → which is why whole flowers are the better storage form and grinding belongs at the point of use.**

Practical storage rules, in order of impact:

1. **Keep it dry.** A sealed jar with a tight gasket, not a knotted bag. If the container has a desiccant sachet, so much the better.
2. **Keep it dark.** Light accelerates the changes that turn a green note into a hay note. An opaque tin beats a clear jar on a windowsill.
3. **Keep it cool.** A cupboard away from a stove or a radiator is enough; heat is the enemy, and a warm shelf is worse than a cool one.
4. **Keep it alone.** Do not store it with strong spices, resins or cleaning products. The flower will take on their character within weeks.
5. **Keep it labelled.** Date the container. Honeysuckle does not fail dramatically; it declines, and a date is the only way to notice.

If you do freeze a large lot, let the sealed container come fully to room temperature before opening it. Otherwise condensation forms on cold flowers the moment warm air reaches them, and you have manufactured the moisture problem you were trying to avoid.

## In Chinese tradition: medicine, tea, and the incense gap

> **Direct answer:** 金银花 has a long record as a heat-clearing materia-medica flower and a familiar ingredient in herbal teas; it has no corresponding record in the classical incense canon. Those two facts sit side by side without contradiction.

The traditional layer, stated carefully:

- **Materia medica.** 金银花 is a well-known flower in the Chinese herbal tradition, discussed in the 本草纲目 tradition under 忍冬, and traditionally associated with clearing heat. This is **traditional belief** — the language of materia medica, not modern clinical evidence.
- **Herbal tea and cooling drinks.** The dried flower appears in scented teas and in southern Chinese herbal preparations, usually blended with other flowers and herbs. This is documented culinary and fragrant use.
- **Household scent.** Dried flowers have been kept for their fragrance, in sachets and in bedding, which is a domestic use rather than a ritual one.

The gap is equally clear. 金银花 does not appear in 香乘, the Ming compendium that anchors most claims of classical incense pedigree. Its use in incense is a modern crossover from the aromatic herb trade, and no amount of repetition turns a materia-medica flower into a canonical 香材.

That gap is not a scandal. It is a normal pattern in this field: materials move between medicine, tea, food and incense, and the routes are different. What deserves suspicion is the opposite claim — a page that lists every fragrant plant as an "ancient incense ingredient" and never checks a canon.

## What "not classical" does and does not mean

> **Direct answer:** Absence from 香乘 is a fact about one book's contents, not a verdict on the material's worth. It does mean that a seller cannot borrow canonical authority for it, and that a formula should use it as a modern accent rather than a heritage ingredient.

Two errors run in opposite directions here. One is inflation: calling honeysuckle a classical incense herb because the flower is old and Chinese. The other is dismissal: treating a non-canonical material as junk because it lacks a Ming citation. Both miss the point.

The useful position is narrower. The canon records what a particular tradition formalised, in a particular place, for a particular set of ritual and domestic uses. It is a historical document, not a chemical assay. A flower can be pleasant, safe to burn in a ventilated room, and commercially useful without appearing in it.

What follows for a reader is a discipline of phrasing. "金银花 is used in modern floral incense" is accurate. "金银花 has been burned in Chinese temples for a thousand years" is not, and the difference is not pedantry — it is the difference between a knowledge page and advertising.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The risk in burning honeysuckle is the smoke, not the flower's reputation. Any plant material that smoulders produces particulate matter, and a gentle botanical name does not make that smoke gentle.

Incense combustion generates smoke, fine particles and carbon monoxide, regardless of how natural or mild the ingredient sounds. Burn in a well-ventilated room, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, do not leave a burning stick unattended, and keep burners away from children and pets. Anyone with asthma or other respiratory sensitivity should be cautious around any incense smoke.

A separate caution applies to the herb itself. 金银花 sold for tea is food-grade material intended for infusion, not a tested incense ingredient; burning it introduces a different exposure route, and "food safe" is not the same claim as "safe to inhale when burned". This page makes no medical claims for the flower, its smoke or its aroma. See the [safety guide](/safety/) for the wider picture.

## FAQ

**What exactly is 金银花?** The dried flower of *Lonicera japonica*, the Japanese honeysuckle, a climbing vine in the Caprifoliaceae family native to East Asia. The flower is picked as a bud or just-opening bloom, dried, and sold through herb channels. It is a floral material with a sweet, green, honeyed scent, not a resin or a wood.

**Is 金银花 the same as 山银花?** No. 金银花 is *Lonicera japonica*. 山银花 is a separate drug name covering other *Lonicera* species such as *L. macranthoides* and *L. confusa*. They are related plants with separate names, and a loose "honeysuckle flower" label does not tell you which one is in the bag.

**Why does the powder smell weaker than the whole flowers?** Because the aromatic surface is exposed all at once. Grinding releases volatiles that were held inside the flower, so the powder gives a strong first impression and then declines faster than intact buds. Fine powder also packs more densely, which reduces the surface available to the air. Whole flowers keep their note longer; powder delivers it faster and loses it sooner.

**Can I burn 金银花 bought from a tea shop?** You can, and it will smell like the flower, but tea-grade material is selected and stored for infusion, not for combustion. Expect more stem and dust, variable moisture, and no information about how it was dried. If you want a repeatable result, buy from a supplier who describes the part, the stage and the drying method.

**Does honeysuckle belong in a classical Chinese incense blend?** Not in the strict sense. It does not appear in 香乘, so a blend that includes it is a modern composition built on a traditional materia-medica material. That is a legitimate choice, as long as the description says "modern" rather than borrowing classical authority.

## Summary

金银花 is the flower of *Lonicera japonica*, a twining semievergreen vine of the Caprifoliaceae family, native to China, Japan and Korea and now widely naturalised. The Flora of China account gives leaf blades of 3–8 × 1.5–4 cm, ovate to lanceolate, and paired fragrant flowers whose two-lipped corolla measures 3–5 cm and shifts from white to yellow as it ages. The vine is 忍冬, the flower 金银花, and the English word "honeysuckle" covers a whole genus rather than this one species.

As a material, it is a light, sweet, green-honeyed floral with a short reach: a top note that brightens an opening and then recedes. Its quality is decided less by rarity than by harvest stage, drying and storage, and its main identification risk is the 金银花–山银花–genus tangle rather than deliberate adulteration. It has a documented materia-medica identity and no place in the classical incense canon — a crossover material that is worth using precisely when it is described as one.

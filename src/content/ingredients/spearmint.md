> **Direct answer:** Spearmint (留兰香, liúlánxiāng) is the perennial mint *Mentha spicata*, grown across Europe and temperate Asia and naturalised far beyond its native range. Its leaf carries a sweet, green, gently cooling scent built on carvone rather than menthol, which is why it reads soft where peppermint reads sharp. It is a modern culinary and fragrance herb with no classical Chinese incense record, and it is a different species from both 薄荷 (*Mentha haplocalyx*) and peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*).

## Three mints behind one English word

**Direct answer:** The mint family supplies three distinct commercial plants, and Chinese names each of them separately. 薄荷 is the East Asian pharmacopoeial mint, 留兰香 is spearmint, and 胡椒薄荷 is peppermint. They are not interchangeable in a blend, a specification, or a price list.

The trio is the first thing to settle, because every later judgment depends on it. Scent, behaviour under heat, and the wording a supplier is entitled to use all follow from the species. A purchase order that says "mint" describes a category, not a material.

| Chinese name | Species | Common name | Dominant molecule | Character | Standing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 薄荷 (bòhé) | *Mentha haplocalyx* (syn. *M. canadensis*) | Chinese mint | Menthol-led | Cold, green, slightly sweet | Classical Chinese culinary and materia-medica herb |
| 留兰香 (liúlánxiāng) | *Mentha spicata* | Spearmint | Carvone-led | Sweet, round, soft | Modern culinary and fragrance herb |
| 胡椒薄荷 (hújiāo bòhé) | *Mentha × piperita* | Peppermint | Menthol and menthone | Hard, cold, camphoraceous | Modern flavour and fragrance material |

Two relationships in that table are worth holding on to. Peppermint is a hybrid, raised from spearmint and watermint, which is why it combines a mint scent with a chemical profile that neither parent carries in the same proportion. 薄荷 is a separate East Asian species with its own classical standing, and it is not simply "Chinese peppermint". The English word "mint" collapses all three, and the collapse is where buying mistakes begin.

## Why carvone reads sweet

**Direct answer:** The sweetness of spearmint is a chemical fact before it is a poetic one. Its oil is dominated by carvone, a molecule that smells herbal-sweet and faintly caraway-like, while peppermint is dominated by menthol, which smells cold and can feel sharp in the nose.

Carvone and menthol belong to the same broad family of plant volatiles, and they behave nothing alike at the nose. Carvone carries a rounded, almost confectionery sweetness with a green edge. Menthol carries a cold, penetrating signal that many people experience as a physical chill rather than a smell. Put the two side by side and the difference is immediate: one reads as a sweet herb, the other as a medicine cabinet.

There is a detail that makes the point memorable. Carvone exists in two mirror forms, and the mirror image of the spearmint molecule is the characteristic note of caraway. The atoms are identical; their arrangement in space is not. A nose can tell the difference, which is a useful reminder that "minty" is not a property of a formula but of a shape.

The chain from molecule to blend runs like this:

- carvone dominates the volatile fraction → the mint signal arrives as sweet and herbal → the sharp trigeminal edge of menthol is absent → the note folds into sweet, green, or floral accords without dominating them → the blend reads soft rather than medicinal.

That is why spearmint can sit in a dessert, a tea, or a delicate fragrance without announcing itself as toothpaste. The same property makes it easy to overuse in the other direction: a formulator who expects peppermint's projection will use too much spearmint and still find the note quiet.

## Four things people mean by "minty"

**Direct answer:** The word "minty" bundles at least four different impressions — the chill of menthol, the sweetness of carvone, the green smell of a crushed leaf, and the household smell of a mint confection. A brief that uses the word without choosing among them is under-specified.

The first meaning is physical. Menthol acts on the nerve endings that report cold, so a menthol product feels cool in tissue that has not changed temperature. That is a sensation, and it can be strong enough to distract from the smell.

The second is the smell of sweetness. Carvone reads as sugary and herbal at once, which is why spearmint appears in confectionery and why a spearmint note can make a blend feel rounder without making it feel colder.

The third is green. A crushed leaf smells of the plant's own tissue as much as of its oil — grassy, slightly bitter, watery. This is the part that essential oil captures least well, and the part that a fresh-leaf comparison exposes first.

The fourth is cultural. "Minty" in a supermarket or a toothpaste aisle means a specific manufactured profile, usually peppermint plus sugar plus a cooling agent. None of the botanical distinctions on this page survive that association intact.

The chain from a vague brief to a failed sample is predictable:

- a client asks for "something minty" → the formulator chooses one of the four meanings → the sample delivers that meaning and not the others → the client says it is not minty enough → the next version overcorrects with menthol → the blend turns medicinal.

The fix is to make the brief name the effect. "Cool and sharp" points at menthol. "Sweet and soft" points at carvone. "Green and fresh" points at leaf. Those three sentences lead to three different formulas.

## The plant and its range

**Direct answer:** *Mentha spicata* is a herbaceous perennial that spreads by underground rhizome, grows knee-high, and prefers damp ground. It is native to Europe and temperate Asia and is now cultivated and naturalised across much of the temperate world; Flora of China records it largely as a cultivated or naturalised plant.

Start with the rhizome, because it explains the plant's behaviour. A creeping underground stem throws up shoots at intervals and can colonise a ditch, a damp corner, or a whole bed in a season or two. The upright stems carry opposite leaves and a terminal flower spike — the "spike" that gives *spicata* its name. The aromatic oil sits in glands on the leaf surface, which is why the scent is strongest when a leaf is crushed between the fingers.

The geography is broad and human-shaped. The species is native to Europe and temperate Asia, and cultivation has carried it into gardens, fields, and roadside patches across the temperate zone. Flora of China treats it as largely cultivated or naturalised within China rather than as a core wild species. That status matters for provenance claims: 留兰香 is not a rare plant with a single mountain origin, and a story that implies otherwise is marketing rather than botany.

Clonal spread has a commercial consequence. A patch often expands from one rhizome system, so a field can be a single genotype, and successive harvests from it behave alike. Change the planting stock and the scent can shift without any change of species, soil, or method. A buyer who values consistency should ask which stock is grown, not only which species.

The route from garden to sack is short:

- rhizome overwinters → shoots emerge in spring → the canopy closes and shades out weeds → the crop is cut at flowering → leaves are dried or distilled → the lot carries the signature of one field and one season.

This is the difference between an agricultural aromatic and a forest one. A resin accumulates over years and can be blended across harvests. A herb is a snapshot of a single season, and its quality is decided long before it reaches a blender.

## What the names point at

**Direct answer:** "Spearmint" describes the shape of the flower spike, not the leaf; *spicata* is Latin for "spiked". 留兰香 is the Chinese name, and neither label says anything about the chemistry that actually separates this plant from its relatives.

Common names describe what a person can see. A spike, a leaf, a place, a smell — each name picks one feature and lets the others go. The binomial does a different job: it fixes a plant to a taxonomic position, which is why this page keeps returning to *Mentha spicata* even when the prose would read more smoothly without it. A name is a handle. A specification needs a coordinate.

Garden supply blurs the picture further. The genus hybridises readily, and a plant sold simply as "mint" may be a cross rather than a species. Vegetative propagation keeps a clone stable from year to year; seed-raised mint does not, because seedlings recombine. That is why a home grower's patch can smell wonderful and still be the wrong material for a blend that needs a known profile.

The chain is worth following:

- two mint populations flower together → insects move pollen → seed sets across the cross → seedlings vary in leaf, scent, and oil → a garden clone may not match either parent → a buyer who needs consistency must ask for the named stock, not the garden label.

None of this makes garden mint inferior. It makes it unspecified. For a teapot, unspecified is fine. For a formula that has to be repeated, it is not.

## Four forms, four behaviours

**Direct answer:** Spearmint reaches a formulator as fresh leaf, dried leaf, essential oil, or a compounded accord. Each carries the same carvone signature at a different strength and with a different failure mode, so the form is part of the specification.

| Form | What it is | Typical use | Strength | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh leaf | Cut herb, used within days | Kitchen, garnish, fresh infusions | Gentle and green | Wilts and browns quickly; not a trade material |
| Dried leaf | Air-dried leaf and top | Tea, culinary blends, sachets | Moderate, fading with age | Loses the top note; turns hay-like |
| Essential oil | Steam-distilled volatile fraction | Flavour, fragrance, incense accords | Concentrated; measured in drops | Overdose, oxidation, skin irritation if mishandled |
| Compounded accord | Oil plus aroma materials in a carrier | Perfumery, incense fragrance | Designed, not extracted | Can drift from the natural profile; depends on the formula |

The jump from leaf to oil is the one that changes how a material is handled. A recipe that calls for grams of dried leaf becomes a recipe that calls for drops of oil, and the margin for error shrinks with the dose. A small slip in a leaf blend is a slightly stronger cup of tea. The same proportional slip in an oil accord can bury every other note in the composition.

The flavour trade adds another layer that most buyers never see. Commercial spearmint oil is not a single undifferentiated commodity: the trade separates "Native" and "Scotch" types, which come from different populations of the mint complex and carry different aroma profiles. A purchase order that names only "spearmint oil" does not choose between them. This is a specialist distinction, and it belongs in a specification only when the finished product is sensitive to it, but it explains why two oils with the same botanical name can smell like cousins rather than twins.

## From leaf to oil

**Direct answer:** Commercial spearmint oil is usually recovered by steam distillation, a process that concentrates the volatile fraction and leaves the water-soluble and heat-sensitive parts behind. That is why an oil smells like a concentrated idea of the leaf rather than like the leaf itself.

The still is simple in principle. Steam is passed through a packed bed of herb; the volatile compounds vaporise with the steam; the mixture is condensed; the oil separates from the water because the two do not mix. What remains is a hydrosol — the aromatic water — and what is drawn off is the essential oil. Nothing is added and nothing is chemically transformed, but the balance of the material changes, because only the volatile fraction makes the journey.

That selection explains a common disappointment. A perfumer who loves the smell of crushed fresh leaf and orders the oil expecting the same will find a sweeter, heavier, less green material. The green top notes are the most fragile part of the leaf, and some are lost in drying, in the still, or in the bottle. The oil is not a failure of the process; it is the process working as designed, concentrating what survives.

The chain from field to bottle runs like this:

- herb is cut and partly dried → steam passes through the bed → volatile compounds vaporise → the condenser cools the mixture → oil separates from water → the oil carries the concentrated volatiles, while the hydrosol carries the water-soluble aromatics.

A buyer who understands this stops treating leaf and oil as two package sizes of the same thing. They are two materials with different balances, and a blend built on one will not transfer unchanged to the other.

## What spearmint does in a blend

**Direct answer:** Spearmint is a top note with a soft edge. It lifts and sweetens the opening of a blend, then leaves quickly, and it cannot carry a composition's middle or base. Its cooling impression is a smell and a nerve effect, not a drop in temperature.

Volatility decides the schedule. The molecules that make up the mint note are light, so they reach the nose before the resins and woods and are gone before those heavier materials begin to speak. On a burning stick, the heat front drives them off in the first moments. In a sachet, they leave more slowly, but they still leave.

The chain on heat runs like this:

- the ember creates a heat front behind it → volatile compounds ahead of the front vaporise first → the spearmint note peaks in the opening seconds → heavier resins and woods release later → the mint has already gone by the time the base arrives.

That behaviour makes spearmint a useful lifter and a poor anchor. A formulator who wants a cool opening can use it at low dose and let the base do the structural work. A formulator who wants the whole stick to smell of mint will keep adding material and will keep being disappointed, because the note is physically incapable of lasting.

The "cooling" label needs the same care. Menthol creates a sensation of cold by acting on nerve endings that report temperature, and the tissue does not actually change temperature. Spearmint's carvone is not menthol, and its cooling impression is softer and more associative: a green, fresh smell that the culture has learned to read as cool. That is a real sensory effect and a different one from the menthol chill. Confusing the two leads to bad briefs — a client asking for "the cooling of mint" may want a physical sensation that spearmint will never deliver.

In incense specifically, spearmint almost never appears as burned dried leaf. It appears as an oil or an accord added to a blend, where it contributes a sweet green lift to the opening. The dried culinary herb burns thin and fast, contributes little body, and is better kept for the teapot. This is a modern use borrowed from perfumery, and it should be described as such.

## Which mint belongs in the brief

**Direct answer:** Choose by the effect the blend needs, not by the word "mint". Spearmint supplies a soft, sweet lift; peppermint supplies a hard, cold opening; 薄荷 supplies the East Asian culinary and materia-medica reference. The three are not substitutes for one another.

| The brief asks for | Reach for | Why it fits | Why the others fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| A soft, sweet mint lift in a delicate blend | Spearmint oil or accord | Carvone sweetness blends without a medicinal edge | Peppermint overpowers; 薄荷 reads colder and greener |
| A forceful, cold opening | Peppermint oil | Menthol and menthone give projection and bite | Spearmint is too soft at the same dose |
| A Chinese culinary or materia-medica reference | 薄荷 leaf | The species and the tradition belong together | Spearmint has no classical Chinese record |
| A mint note in a sachet | Dried spearmint or 薄荷 leaf | Slow evaporation at room temperature | Oil in an open sachet is wasteful and messy |
| A mint note in a burning stick | A low dose of oil or accord | Volatiles flash off in the opening seconds | Dried leaf burns thin and leaves no base |

The table is a decision aid, not a hierarchy. Spearmint is not a weaker peppermint; it is a different material with a different job. A brief that asks for "mint" without naming the effect is asking the formulator to guess, and the guess will be wrong about half the time.

There is a boundary worth stating. When a client asks for the cooling sensation of mint, spearmint may not deliver it, because carvone is not menthol and the physical chill is a menthol effect. When the client asks for the smell of a sweet mint confection, spearmint is the closer match. The line between a sensory effect and an aromatic impression is the line between the two mints.

## What it blends with, and what it fights

**Direct answer:** Spearmint sits most comfortably beside citrus, light florals, and green herbs, and least comfortably beside heavy resins and sweet bases. The pattern follows from volatility: it belongs to the opening, so partners that also live in the opening stack cleanly, while partners that arrive late leave it stranded.

| Partner family | What happens | Why | Working rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citrus peel and citrus oils | Bright, coherent opening | Both are volatile top notes with overlapping lift | Keep the citrus drier than the mint, or the pair reads as sweets |
| Light florals | A soft green lift under the petals | Carvone sweetness supports without asserting | Low dose; high dose turns soapy |
| Green herbs | A denser green opening | Similar volatility, similar register | Let one herb lead; two leaders blur |
| Resins and incense woods | The mint vanishes before the resin speaks | A large gap in volatility | Bridge with a mid-note or accept a split opening |
| Vanilla, benzoin, sweet balsams | Candy, very quickly | Sweetness added to sweetness | Use a trace, or avoid |

The resin row is the one that surprises people. A formulator adds spearmint to a frankincense blend expecting a fresh lift and finds that the first minute is mint and the next hour is resin, with nothing in between. The materials are not incompatible; they are simply scheduled at different times. A blend needs a middle note — a soft floral, a light spice, a mild wood — to carry the ear from one to the other.

The sweet row is the opposite failure. Carvone already reads sweet, and a sweet base doubles the effect until the composition tastes of confectionery rather than smells of mint. This is a boundary worth marking: spearmint is not a "fresh" ingredient in every context. Put it beside sugar and it becomes candy; put it beside citrus and green herbs and it stays herbal.

These judgments are editorial, drawn from how the material behaves in blends rather than from a controlled trial. They are starting points for a bench test, not rules that replace one.

## Buying leaf and oil

**Direct answer:** Leaf and oil fail in different ways, so they need different checks. Leaf is judged on colour, leaf share, scent, and cleanliness; oil is judged on aroma profile, freshness, and how it behaves when diluted.

| Material | Strong lot | Weak lot | What it signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dried leaf | Grey-green, leafy, sweet on crushing | Brown, stemmy, hay-like | Oxidation and rough drying |
| Dried leaf | Clean, few foreign leaves | Sand, twigs, other herbs | Screening discipline |
| Dried leaf | Crisp and dry | Soft, clumping | Moisture risk in store |
| Essential oil | Sweet, carvone-forward, clean tail | Sharp, solvent-like, flat | Adulteration, age, or poor distillation |
| Essential oil | Clear and mobile | Cloudy or dark | Contamination or oxidation |
| Accord | Reads as spearmint at low dose | Smells of mint syrup at any dose | Over-formulation or cheap aroma chemicals |

For leaf, the inspection is the same one that works for any dried herb. Look first: a thick pale stem is bulk with little oil, brown patches mean heat or moisture damage, and fine dust at the bottom of the bag means the glands have already been broken. Then crush a leaf and smell within a few seconds. The sweet note should arrive before the dry, tea-like base. A lot that smells of hay in the hand will not improve in a sachet.

For oil, the nose is still the primary instrument, but dilution is the discipline. A concentrated oil straight from the bottle overwhelms the nose and hides defects; a drop on a smelling strip, allowed to settle for a minute, shows the profile more honestly. What should remain is a sweet, green, carvone-led scent with a clean tail. A sharp opening that collapses into a flat, waxy residue suggests age or a blend that is not what the label says.

Paperwork answers a different question. A certificate of analysis can confirm identity and contamination limits, and it cannot confirm that the top note survived storage. Ask for the species, the form, the harvest or distillation date, and the lot number. Those four items do more for a buyer than a page of generic assurances.

## A bench test for a spearmint sample

**Direct answer:** Judge spearmint at dilution, on paper, and on heat, and compare it against a known sample of the same form. The nose at full strength is the least reliable instrument in the room.

Start with the container, not the oil. The headspace tells you how the material has been stored; it does not tell you what the oil will do in a blend. A tight, sweet headspace is a good sign, and a sharp or dusty one is a reason to look further.

Then put a single drop on a smelling strip and read it three times: within the first half-minute, after five minutes, and after half an hour. The arc matters more than any single impression. A clean spearmint opens sweet and green, holds a herbal middle, and fades to a soft, slightly waxy tail. A sample that opens sharp and collapses flat has usually aged or been stretched.

Heat is the third test, and the one most relevant to incense. Apply a trace to an unscented stick, light it in a ventilated space, and note what survives the first minute. The mint should be present at the opening and gone soon after; that is the material behaving normally. What should not happen is a chemical sharpness that outlasts the mint or a greasy smoke that smells of the carrier rather than the herb.

The chain explains why dilution is not optional:

- a concentrated oil saturates the olfactory system within seconds → the nose reports the loudest note rather than the balance → the middle and tail disappear from perception → a diluted sample releases more slowly → the arc becomes visible.

A reference sample is the other half of the method. Keep a small bottle of a known good spearmint of the same type and smell the two side by side, never one after the other without a pause. Comparison is the only calibration available to a nose.

## Storage and stability

**Direct answer:** Both leaf and oil lose their best character to oxygen, light, and heat. Leaf fades visibly; oil fades invisibly, which makes oil the more deceptive of the two.

Dried leaf is a volatile material held in a paper matrix. Every time a bag is opened, fresh air replaces the saturated headspace and carries aroma away. Light accelerates oxidation, and warmth accelerates evaporation. A sealed jar in a dark cupboard slows all three; a clear jar on a sunny shelf speeds them up. Whole leaf keeps longer than powder because fewer glands have been broken, and small packages beat large ones for the same reason.

Essential oil has a different stability profile. It does not dry out, but it oxidises, and the process is slow enough to be invisible until the smell turns flat or sharp. Keep the bottle tightly closed, upright, and away from heat and daylight. Do not decant into an open dish and leave it there between sessions. A darkened glass bottle with a tight cap is not fussiness; it is the minimum for a material that is supposed to smell of something.

There is a practical significance for anyone blending at home. The first bottle of an oil is usually the best one a buyer will own, because it is the freshest. Everything after that is a race against the headspace. Buying small and using promptly is the only reliable way to keep the top note in the material instead of in the room.

## A modern herb without a classical pedigree

**Direct answer:** Spearmint has no recorded place in the classical Chinese 香材 canon, and this page does not invent one. Its use is a modern, European-influenced crossover from cooking and perfumery, and that is a sufficient identity on its own.

The absence is easy to state and easy to blur. A reader who sees a mint-family herb in a Chinese encyclopedia may assume an old pedigree, and a marketer may be happy to encourage the assumption. The record does not support it. The classical Chinese aromatic tradition worked with other materials — resins, woods, and a defined set of herbs — and 留兰香 as a named fragrance material belongs to a later, imported culinary culture.

Does that make it a lesser material? No. It makes it a different one. A modern herb can be specified, tested, and blended with the same rigour as an old one, and it carries no obligation to borrow a history it does not have. The honest description — sweet, carvone-led, modern, culinary — is also the most useful description, because it tells a formulator what the material will actually do.

A common misconception runs in the other direction: that a plant with no classical record must be a recent invention. *Mentha spicata* is an old species with a wide natural range. What is modern is its use as a named fragrance and incense material, not the plant itself. Keeping those two facts apart is the discipline this page asks of its reader.

The distinction matters for buyers in a concrete way. A supplier selling "traditional Chinese spearmint" is making a claim that the record does not support, and a buyer who accepts it has learned nothing about the material. A supplier who says "modern culinary mint, carvone-dominant, no classical incense use" has told the truth and given a formulator something to work with.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The risks are smoke, concentrated oil, and careless storage. Dried leaf is a food-style herb; essential oil is a strong aromatic material that should not be treated as a medicine or applied neat to skin.

Burning any aromatic material indoors adds smoke and particulate matter to the air. Ventilate the space, keep the ember away from fabric and paper, and never leave a lit stick unattended. Anyone who reacts to smoke should not be in the room while it burns.

Essential oil is concentrated and should be kept tightly closed and out of reach of children and pets. Do not ingest it, do not apply it undiluted to skin, and do not use it as a substitute for medical care. If a blend irritates the eyes, nose, or throat, stop using it and open a window. These are handling rules, not medical claims, and they apply to every concentrated aromatic in this encyclopedia.

## FAQ

**What is spearmint (留兰香)?** A rhizomatous perennial in the Lamiaceae, *Mentha spicata*, whose native range runs from Europe into temperate Asia and whose cultivation has carried it well beyond that. The leaf is sweet and green, with carvone carrying the mint note; its uses are culinary and, more recently, fragrant.

**How is spearmint different from 薄荷?** By species and by chemistry. 留兰香 is *Mentha spicata*, with carvone behind its sweetness; 薄荷 is *Mentha haplocalyx* (syn. *M. canadensis*), the East Asian plant used in cooking and materia medica, with a colder, menthol-led profile. English folds the two together, Chinese does not.

**How is spearmint different from peppermint?** Spearmint is a species with a carvone-dominant oil; peppermint is a hybrid of spearmint and watermint with a menthol- and menthone-dominant oil. Spearmint reads sweet and soft, peppermint reads cold and sharp, and the two behave differently at the same dose.

**Why is spearmint sweeter than peppermint?** The two oils are built around different molecules. Carvone reads as sweet and herbal, with a faint caraway edge; menthol reads as cold and penetrating. The gap is a difference of kind, not a dial that can be turned up or down.

**Can I burn dried spearmint as incense?** You can, and it will smell thin. The dried leaf burns fast and hot, releases its volatiles in the opening seconds, and leaves little base. It works better in a sachet or a tea than on a burning stick; for incense, an oil or an accord is the practical form.

**Is spearmint oil interchangeable with peppermint oil?** No. They differ in dominant molecule, in sensory character, and in strength of the cooling effect. Substituting one for the other changes the blend, and a specification should name the species and the oil type rather than the word "mint".

**Does spearmint have a classical Chinese incense use?** No recorded one. It is a modern culinary and fragrance herb, and its appearance in incense is a contemporary, Western-influenced use rather than a classical 香材 practice.

## Summary

留兰香 is *Mentha spicata*, a rhizomatous perennial mint with a sweet, green, carvone-led scent. It sits between 薄荷 and peppermint in the encyclopedia's mint trio: sweeter than the East Asian mint, far softer than the menthol-heavy hybrid. Its forms behave differently — leaf fades, oil concentrates, accord is designed — and its place in incense is a modern fragrance note rather than a classical material. The practical lesson is that "mint" is a category, while carvone, menthol, and the species name are specifications. A buyer who names the molecule and the form gets the material they intended; a buyer who orders "mint" gets whatever the word happens to mean that week.

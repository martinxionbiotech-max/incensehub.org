> **Direct answer:** Citron (香橼, xiāngyuán) is the fruit of *Citrus medica*, a thick-skinned citrus in the rue family (Rutaceae). People grow it for the peel, not the pulp: the rind holds the oil, the oil holds the scent, and the scent is the whole point. In Chinese households it is a 清供 table fruit, set out to perfume a room and to carry a seasonal wish. It is the parent species of the finger citron (佛手), it is never burned as incense, and it is not citronella.

## A name that covers more than one fruit

> **Direct answer:** 香橼 is *Citrus medica* proper; 佛手 is its fingered cultivar; 枸橼 is an older name for the same species; and 佛手柑 is bergamot, a different hybrid that merely borrows the characters. Four names, three plants, one long-running confusion.

Chinese fruit names were not built as a taxonomy. They were built as a working vocabulary for markets, kitchens, and offering tables, and a name could travel between species when the shape or the scent resembled something already known. 香橼 is a good example of how far that can go. In a southern market it means the big, rough, lemon-coloured fruit with the spongy white rind. In an old text it may appear as 枸橼. In a temple-town fruit stall the same word may be used loosely for the fingered form, because both are *Citrus medica* and both are sold for the same purpose.

The four names are worth keeping apart:

- **香橼 (xiāngyuán)** — the plain citron, *Citrus medica*. Oval to oblong, often ribbed, with a peel that can be more than a centimetre thick. This page.
- **枸橼 (jǔyuán)** — an older literary name for the same fruit, still met in classical quotations and in materia-medica writing. Treat it as a synonym, not a separate plant.
- **佛手 (fóshǒu)** — the finger citron, *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis*, whose fruit splits at the base into finger-like sections. Its own entry is [here](/ingredients/finger-citron/).
- **佛手柑 (fóshǒugān)** — bergamot, *Citrus bergamia*, a hybrid with a small, smooth, green-to-yellow fruit and a perfumed, bitter, tea-like peel. The name borrows 佛手; the plant does not belong to the citron's variety. See [bergamot](/ingredients/bergamot/).

| Name | What the plant is | What you are holding | Where the scent sits | Usual role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 香橼 | *Citrus medica* | Large, rough, thick-peeled fruit | Dense oil glands in a deep flavedo | Display, candied peel |
| 枸橼 | Same species, older name | Same fruit | Same | Classical and literary reference |
| 佛手 | *C. medica* var. *sarcodactylis* | Fingered fruit, often smaller | Concentrated at the finger tips | Display, offering, candied |
| 佛手柑 | *Citrus bergamia* | Small, smooth, pear-shaped fruit | Thin peel, high perfume | Oil, fragrance, tea |
| 柠檬 | *Citrus × limon* | Ordinary lemon | Thin peel, sharp acid | Food, drink, peel |

The practical consequence is simple. A supplier who answers "香橼" without a botanical name has told you the trade name, not the plant. For a display fruit that may be enough. For anything where the scent matters — a reorder, a substitution, a written description — ask for the species.

## The fruit, part by part

> **Direct answer:** A citron is mostly peel. The outer coloured layer (flavedo) carries the oil glands, the white sponge beneath it (albedo) carries water and pectin, and the pulp is a thin, dry afterthought. Every property that makes the fruit useful follows from that proportion.

Cut one open and the anatomy explains the economics. The flavedo is the thin, coloured skin — yellow when ripe, sometimes green in tropical climates — and it is studded with tiny translucent vesicles. Those vesicles are the oil glands. Press one and a bead of aromatic oil comes out; that bead is the material. Underneath sits the albedo, a white, spongy layer that can be several times thicker than the coloured skin. It holds water, it is rich in pectin, and it tastes bitter rather than aromatic. At the centre, the pulp segments are small, pale, and dry compared with an orange, and the seeds are few.

- **Flavedo** — the scent. Oil glands are densest here, and the oil is a mixture of monoterpenes, limonene chief among them, plus smaller amounts of other volatiles.
- **Albedo** — the bulk. Thick, spongy, pectin-rich, bitter. It is what makes candied peel possible and what makes a fresh fruit heavy.
- **Pulp** — the minority. Little juice, low sugar, a few seeds.
- **Stem end** — the weak point. Water leaves the fruit here first, and so does scent.

That distribution has a direct consequence for handling. The fragrance is not evenly spread through the fruit; it is concentrated in a layer thinner than a fingernail, and that layer is fragile. A bruise that barely marks the white sponge can still rupture glands in the flavedo a centimetre away.

**Mechanism:** bruise the peel → oil glands rupture → volatile oil spreads across the surface → oxygen and light act on it → the sharp, fresh note turns flat and slightly rancid → the fruit smells older than it looks. This is why citrons are carried and stored like soft fruit, not like firewood, and why a fruit that looks sound can smell tired.

The albedo explains the other half of the story. Because it is thick and water-dense, it buffers the fruit against short dry spells and gives the candied-peel maker something to work with. It also dilutes: per gram of whole fruit, a citron carries less oil than a thin-skinned lemon, even though the peel smells stronger up close. Weight is not a proxy for scent.

## Why a fruit can scent a room without fire

> **Direct answer:** The oil in the peel evaporates at room temperature. No flame is needed because the aromatic molecules are already volatile; the fruit simply leaks them into the air, and the rate of that leak depends on temperature, air movement, and how much intact surface is exposed.

This is the same process that lets a cut lemon scent a kitchen, slowed down and stretched over days. Inside the oil gland, the aromatic liquid is under a modest vapour pressure; at the gland's surface, molecules escape into the air whenever the surrounding air is not already saturated with them. Move the air and you remove the saturated layer, so more molecules escape. Warm the fruit and the vapour pressure rises, so the release speeds up. Cool it and the release slows almost to nothing.

Three variables do most of the work:

1. **Temperature.** A warm room pushes more oil into the air. A cold room holds it back. The difference is large enough that the same fruit can seem scentless in one room and strong in another.
2. **Air movement.** Still air builds a boundary layer of vapour around the peel, which slows further release. A gentle draught strips that layer away. This is why a fruit on a windowsill with a crack of ventilation reads stronger than one in a sealed alcove.
3. **Exposed surface.** Scent comes off the outside of the fruit. A fruit lying on one side has roughly half its surface doing the work; a fruit in a shallow dish with air underneath has more.

**Mechanism:** warm, dry, moving air → faster evaporation → a stronger first impression → the oil reservoir drains sooner → the display peaks early and fades early. The opposite combination — cool, still, humid — gives a quieter scent that lasts longer. Neither is "correct". A reception desk wants the first; a closed study wants the second.

There is a boundary worth stating. Heat does not improve a citron, it only speeds it up. Warmth also accelerates ripening, water loss, and mould, so a fruit parked next to a radiator or in direct sun will smell wonderful for a few days and then fail suddenly. The old practice of setting the fruit on a cool, shaded table is not superstition; it is a way of trading peak intensity for duration.

## What it actually smells like

> **Direct answer:** Citron smells like lemon peel with the sharpness softened, a floral middle, and a bitter, slightly resinous edge at the end. It is sweeter than a lemon, less sweet than an orange, less floral than the finger citron, and less perfumed than bergamot.

The opening is citrus, but not the thin, acidic citrus of juice. It is the smell of the outside of the peel: bright, slightly waxy, with a sweetness that reads as ripe rather than sugary. Behind that sits a floral quality — not a flower's perfume exactly, more the suggestion of orange blossom without the honey. The finish is where the citron separates itself: a bitter, almost tonic edge that lingers on the skin after you have handled the fruit.

That bitterness is not a defect. It comes from the same chemistry that makes marmalade taste grown-up: flavonoid compounds in the peel, plus the terpene profile of the oil. At low intensity the bitterness reads as complexity. At high intensity — a fruit past its best, or a handful of peel crushed in the hand — it reads as harsh.

The comparisons that actually help a buyer:

| Material | Opening | Middle | Finish | Practical read |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Citron (香橼) | Soft lemon | Faint orange blossom | Bitter, clean | A display scent; quiet and adult |
| Finger citron (佛手) | Sweeter citrus | Strong floral | Light bitter | More floral, more extroverted |
| Lemon (柠檬) | Sharp, acidic | Green | Pith-bitter | A food smell, not a room smell |
| Bergamot (佛手柑) | Perfumed citrus | Floral, tea-like | Bitter, persistent | A perfumery material |
| Sweet orange | Round, sugary | Faint floral | Almost none | Friendly, short-lived |

A useful rule of thumb, and this is an editorial judgment rather than a laboratory finding: the citron's scent sits closer to bergamot than to lemon in *shape*, but much closer to lemon in *strength*. It has the architecture of a perfume and the volume of a fruit.

## Citron and finger citron: same species, different fruit

> **Direct answer:** 香橼 and 佛手 are the same species, *Citrus medica*, separated at the cultivar level. 佛手 is the form whose fruit divides into finger-like sections; 香橼 is the undivided form. They share a scent family, a season, and a display role, and they are still not interchangeable objects.

Botanically the gap is small — a variety, not a species. Culturally the gap is larger, because the two fruits carry different shapes and therefore different readings. The fingered form is the more theatrical object: it looks like a hand, it invites the 佛手 pun, and it is the one most often photographed in a 清供 arrangement. The plain citron is the quieter object: heavier, rougher, often ribbed, and easier to mistake for an oversized, misshapen lemon.

| | Citron (香橼) | Finger citron (佛手) |
|---|---|---|
| Rank | *Citrus medica* | *C. medica* var. *sarcodactylis* |
| Shape | Oval or oblong, often ribbed | Split into finger-like sections |
| Peel | Very thick, coarse, dense glands | Thinner, fragrant at the tips |
| Scent | Reserved lemon-floral, bitter edge | Sweeter, more floral, more lift |
| Display role | Mass and presence; pairs well with flowers | Focal point; strong silhouette |
| Culinary use | Candied peel, preserves | Candied, preserved, sometimes sliced |
| Trade risk | Sold as 佛手 by careless sellers | Sold as 香橼 by careless sellers |

**Mechanism:** a fruit with more cut surface and thinner sections → more oil glands exposed per gram → a faster, louder release of scent → a shorter useful display. This is one reason the fingered form reads as more fragrant at the stall: it is not necessarily richer in oil, it is simply shaped so that more of the oil is close to the air.

The substitution question deserves a direct answer. If a display calls for 佛手 and you use 香橼, you have changed the silhouette and softened the scent; the arrangement still works, but it is a different arrangement. If a label says 佛手 and the box contains 香橼, that is a misdescription, not a stylistic choice. Scent similarity does not make two named fruits the same product.

## Where the citron came from

> **Direct answer:** *Citrus medica* is native to the Himalayan foothills and northeastern India, and it is one of the oldest citrus in cultivation. It moved west along trade routes long before the modern citrus hybrids existed, and it acquired local names and local forms at every stop.

The citron is old in a way that is easy to underestimate. While the sweet orange and the grapefruit are recent hybrids — products of a few centuries of accidental and deliberate crossing — the citron was already a cultivated fruit when those hybrids were impossible. It appears early in Sanskrit, Hebrew, and Chinese records under different names, and its spread can be traced through ritual and medicine as much as through food.

Cultivation shaped the fruit. Because citrus propagates readily from cuttings and layerings, a good tree could be copied and carried, and local growers selected for what their market wanted: larger fruit for display, thicker peel for candying, more fingers for the ornamental trade. The result is a species with a wide range of fruit forms and a set of regional names that do not line up neatly.

**Mechanism:** long cultivation plus easy vegetative propagation → many local landraces → visible variation in shape and rind → identification by appearance alone becomes unreliable → the botanical name, not the market name, is what settles a dispute. That chain is the reason this page keeps returning to species.

The Mediterranean chapter is worth knowing even though it is not Chinese. In Jewish practice the etrog — a citron, grown under close supervision for the purpose — is one of the four species used at Sukkot. The fruit is the same species as 香橼, selected and certified along a different cultural track. For a reader trying to understand why the citron is so often described as "ancient" and "ritual", that parallel explains more than any aroma description.

## The family tree in plain terms

> **Direct answer:** Modern citrus descends from three ancestral species — citron, pomelo, and mandarin. Lemons, oranges, grapefruits, and bergamots are hybrids built from those three in different combinations, which makes the citron an ancestor rather than a cousin.

This matters for reading labels. A bottle described as "citrus" may contain the peel of a fruit that is two or three hybridisation steps away from the citron. The relationship is real, and it does not make the materials interchangeable.

| Fruit | Parentage (as generally understood) | Relationship to citron |
|---|---|---|
| Citron (*C. medica*) | Ancestral species | Itself |
| Pomelo (*C. maxima*) | Ancestral species | Sibling ancestor, not a parent |
| Mandarin (*C. reticulata*) | Ancestral species | Sibling ancestor |
| Lemon (*C. × limon*) | Citron × bitter orange | First-generation descendant |
| Bergamot (*C. bergamia*) | Bitter orange × citron | Descendant, perfumed peel |
| Sweet orange | Pomelo × mandarin | No direct citron line |
| Grapefruit | Pomelo × sweet orange | No direct citron line |
| Finger citron | *C. medica* variety | Same species, different form |

Two corrections follow. The first is that "citron is the ancestor of all citrus" is an overstatement: it is one of three ancestors, and several major fruits — sweet orange, grapefruit, mandarin — owe it nothing directly. The second is that ancestry is not identity. A lemon carries citron genes and tastes like a lemon; you cannot candy a lemon peel and call it 香橼.

That distinction has practical weight in a substitution. If a formula or a display calls for citron peel and you have lemon peel, you have a sharper, thinner, more acidic material with far less of the floral-bitter middle. The direction of the substitution is predictable, which is useful; the equivalence is not there.

## 清供: display as a practice

> **Direct answer:** 清供 ("pure offerings") is the Chinese practice of arranging fruit, flowers, and objects on a table for scent and auspicious meaning, especially around the New Year. 香橼 and 佛手 are staples of the arrangement, valued as much for what they signify as for how they smell.

The word 清 carries the sense of pure, quiet, refined; 供 is an offering. A 清供 table is not a religious altar in the strict sense and not a bouquet either. It is a composed still life — a shallow dish, a few fruits, a branch of plum or a pot of narcissus, perhaps a stone or a small censer — set where the household will see and smell it. The genre has a long history in painting, which is why the arrangement is often recognised before it is understood.

What the citron contributes is duration. Cut flowers fade in days; a citron keeps releasing oil for weeks if it is handled well. It also contributes a shape that reads as abundance: roundness, weight, a rough skin that suggests a harvest rather than a decoration.

The meanings layered onto it are folk readings, not botany:

- **橼 (yuán)** sounds like 缘 (affinity, fate) and 圆 (round, complete). A round fruit on a New Year table carries a wish for wholeness.
- **佛手 (fóshǒu)** is read as "Buddha's hand", and by sound is often linked to 福寿 (blessing and longevity). These are popular associations, treated here as traditional belief rather than doctrine.
- **The pairing** of citron, finger citron, narcissus, and plum is a standard 岁朝清供 (New Year offering) combination — the fruits for scent and mass, the flowers for the promise of spring.

The practical point is that display is not passive storage. A fruit set out on a table is being used, and its condition is the user's responsibility: it needs air, a dry surface, and a check every few days. A 清供 that has gone soft and spotted has stopped being an offering and become a chore.

## Rind in the kitchen

> **Direct answer:** The thick albedo is the reason citron peel can be candied, and candying is the main culinary use. Blanching leaches the bitterness, a slow syrup replaces the water, and drying sets a translucent, chewy peel that keeps for months.

Candied citron is one of the old conservative technologies of the kitchen: sugar as preservative, peel as the thing preserved. The method is straightforward and the failures are predictable.

1. **Prepare the peel.** Scrape or trim the roughest outer skin if it is very blemished, then cut the peel into strips or quarters. Some cooks keep the flavedo, some remove part of it; keeping it preserves aroma and adds a little bitterness.
2. **Blanch, repeatedly.** Simmer the peel in water, drain, repeat. Each round leaches bitter compounds out of the albedo and softens the tissue.
3. **Simmer in syrup.** Cook gently in a sugar syrup until the peel turns translucent. The syrup replaces the water inside the albedo, which is what preserves it.
4. **Dry.** Drain and dry until tacky rather than wet. Store cool.

**Mechanism:** thick albedo → bitter flavonoids held in the sponge → repeated blanching leaches them into the water → syrup infiltration replaces water with sugar → water activity drops → mould cannot get a foothold. Over-blanch and you also leach the aromatic oil from the flavedo, which is why the peel can end up sweet, chewy, and bland. The trade-off is real: bitterness and aroma sit in the same fruit, and the cook is choosing a point on that line.

Dried peel is the other route. Sun-dried or oven-dried at low heat, it loses its top notes quickly and keeps a bitter-sweet base that can go into tea, a simmering pot, or a spice blend. As an incense material it is marginal: it will smoulder if ground and mixed with a binder, but it is not part of the classical incense canon, and the smoke is closer to burning kitchen peel than to a designed aromatic.

## Buying a fresh citron

> **Direct answer:** Judge the peel first, the scent second, and the weight third. A good display citron is firm, heavy for its size, free of soft spots, and aromatic at the stem end; gloss alone means nothing, and may mean a wax coating.

There is no official grading ladder for 香橼, so buyers fall back on sensory checks. The following grades are a working scheme for a market or a receiving desk, not an industry standard.

| Grade | Peel and surface | Scent at the stem end | Typical faults | Reasonable use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Display grade | Thick, even, oily sheen, intact glands | Clear lemon-floral, bitter edge | None visible | Table display, photograph, gift |
| Good | Thick, slight rub marks | Present but softer | Minor scarring | Display, candied peel |
| Utility | Sound but dull, some shrivel | Faint | Stem-end shrinkage | Candying, peel for the kitchen |
| Reject | Soft patches, split skin, mould | Sour or absent | Rot, bruising, off-odour | Discard |

Four checks take a few seconds each:

- **Lift it.** Heaviness for size suggests water content. A fruit that feels light has already begun to dry.
- **Press the stem end.** It should be firm, not spongy. This is where dehydration starts.
- **Smell the stem end, not the middle.** The oil is densest near the stem and the base; the flank may be scentless even on a good fruit.
- **Look for a uniform film.** A very even, slightly plastic gloss can be a shipping wax. Wax is not a defect in a food fruit, but it seals the glands and mutes the scent.

Here is the editorial judgment that matters most: **a flawless-looking fruit with a weak scent is a bad buy for a display.** The purpose of the object is aromatic, and appearance is only the wrapper. Buyers who shop by photograph alone routinely end up with beautiful, silent fruit.

## How a displayed fruit ages

> **Direct answer:** A displayed citron loses water from the stem end outward, its scent peaks in the first days and then declines, and it ends at the mould stage if it is left in place. The decline is not linear, and the visible signs lag behind the aromatic ones.

The sequence is reliable enough to plan around:

1. **Fresh.** The peel is firm, the glands are full, the scent is at its strongest. This is the peak.
2. **Settling.** The surface dulls slightly and the scent softens. The fruit is still sound; it is simply releasing less.
3. **Stem-end shrink.** A ring of fine wrinkles appears around the stem. Water loss is now visible.
4. **Overall softening.** The fruit gives under a thumb. Oil glands are collapsing; the bitter edge sharpens as the fresh top notes go.
5. **Contact damage.** The patch resting on the dish darkens and softens. This is where mould starts.
6. **Failure.** Fuzz, sour smell, collapse. Discard the fruit and clean the dish.

**Mechanism:** fruit rests on a flat dish → moisture trapped at the contact patch → skin weakens → mould spores germinate → the patch spreads → the whole fruit is contaminated. The fix is mechanical: a dish with feet, a shallow bed of dry leaves, or a turn every few days. Air under the fruit is worth more than any preservative.

There is a second chain worth knowing, because it explains the common complaint that a citron "went off suddenly":

**Mechanism:** low humidity → fast water loss at the stem end → the peel hardens and shrinks → the fruit looks fine while the interior is already drying → a soft spot appears at the base → by the time it is visible, the fruit is spent. Citrons fail from the inside of the peel outward, and the outside catches up late.

## Cold storage and the wax problem

> **Direct answer:** Cold storage slows water loss and extends shelf life, and it also suppresses the very evaporation the fruit is sold for. A cold citron can be sound and scentless at the same time; it needs a day at room temperature before anyone judges it.

This is the least intuitive part of buying a scented fruit. The supply chain is built for durability: cool rooms, controlled humidity, sometimes a wax coating. Each of those choices is rational for a food product and hostile to a display product.

- **Cold** reduces the vapour pressure of the oil, so less reaches the air. Bring the fruit into a warm room and the glands resume, but the transition also produces condensation on the peel.
- **High humidity** slows water loss, and it also encourages surface mould once the fruit is moved to a normal room.
- **Wax** seals the flavedo. A waxed fruit can look glossier and keep longer while releasing almost nothing.

**Mechanism:** wax film over the flavedo → oil glands sealed → volatiles accumulate under the film instead of escaping → the buyer smells nothing at the stall → the fruit is judged poor and discarded or discounted → the actual quality of the fruit is never assessed. The coating has hidden the product's only function.

The practical protocol is simple. If a citron has come out of cold storage, let it sit at room temperature for a day, wipe off any condensation, and smell it again before deciding. If it still has no scent, the problem is the fruit, not the room.

## What "authentic" means here

> **Direct answer:** There is no certification scheme for 香橼, and "authentic" is a weak frame for a fresh fruit. The meaningful questions are botanical identity, freshness, and whether the seller can name the species.

Authenticity language arrives from the resin and wood trades, where a material can be adulterated, blended, or substituted and the buyer cannot see the difference. A whole citron is different: you can hold it, cut it, and smell it. The risks are mislabelling and staleness, not sophistication.

| Check | What it confirms | What it cannot confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Shape and ribbing | Consistent with *C. medica* | Which cultivar, or where grown |
| Peel thickness | A thick-rinded citrus | Whether it is the plain form |
| Segment count and seeds | Citron-type anatomy | Species-level identity |
| Scent | Freshness and oil content | Botanical name |
| Seller's written species | Traceability | Honesty |

The trade-facing version of this is blunt. A serious seller can write *Citrus medica* and distinguish it from *C. medica* var. *sarcodactylis* and *Citrus bergamia*. A seller who can only say "fragrant fruit" is not necessarily cheating, but they are not giving you anything to verify against either. In a category where the name is the main risk, that silence is the thing to price in. The [wholesale guide](/blog/wholesale-guide/) sets out the wider framework for vetting a supplier on exactly this kind of written specification.

One asymmetry is worth naming. A whole fruit is hard to fake and easy to check: the shape, the rind, the smell at the stem end, and the weight all give the buyer something to work with. A peel product — candied, dried, powdered — removes every one of those signals and leaves only taste and smell. That is why the identity argument belongs at the moment of purchase, while the fruit is still whole. Once it has been processed, the practical question shifts from "which species" to "how much of the aroma survived", and the second question is much harder to answer from a sample.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** A citron is not burned, so the combustion risks that dominate incense practice do not apply. The risks that do apply are mould on a displayed fruit and concentrated peel oil on skin.

- **Mould.** A soft, spotted, or fuzzy fruit should be discarded, not trimmed. Spores spread through the spongy albedo well before the surface shows it.
- **Peel oil on skin.** Cold-pressed citrus peel oils can contain compounds that make skin react to sunlight. Handling a fruit is not a concern; expressing oil onto skin and then sitting in the sun is a different matter. Keep concentrated peel oil off the face and off children.
- **Pets and children.** A displayed fruit within reach will be chewed. Citrus peel is not a food for cats or dogs, and the oil is best kept away from both.
- **If you burn incense nearby.** Smoke and fine particles are the issue, not the fruit. Ventilate the room, keep the burner clear of dry peel and paper, and do not leave it unattended. Our [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general rules.

## Summary

Citron (香橼) is *Citrus medica*: an ancient citrus grown for a peel that is thick enough to candy and porous enough to perfume a room. Its scent is lemon-floral with a bitter finish, released by evaporation rather than combustion, which places it in the display tradition of 清供 rather than in the incense canon of 香乘. It is the parent species of the finger citron (佛手), a distant ancestor of the lemon, and a namesake trap for anyone who confuses it with bergamot (佛手柑). Buy it by scent and rind condition, display it with air underneath, and expect a quiet scent that fades rather than a loud one that lasts.

## FAQ

**What is citron (香橼) exactly?** It is the fruit of *Citrus medica*, a citrus in the rue family with a very thick, oil-rich peel and little pulp. In Chinese use it is a fragrant table fruit, displayed as part of a 清供 arrangement and sometimes candied. It is not a wood, a resin, or a grass, and it is not burned as incense.

**Is 香橼 the same as 佛手?** They are the same species in different forms. 香橼 is the undivided citron; 佛手 is *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis*, whose fruit splits into finger-like sections. The scent is related but not identical — the fingered form is sweeter and more floral — and the shapes serve different roles in an arrangement.

**Does citron repel insects?** No. That reputation belongs to 香茅 (lemongrass, *Cymbopogon citratus*) and to the citronella grasses, which are grasses, not citrus. The similarity of the English words is a coincidence of naming, not a shared property. See [citronella](/ingredients/citronella/) for the plant that actually carries the repellent trade.

**Can I burn citron peel as incense?** You can dry it and smoulder it with a binder, and it will produce a sweet-bitter smoke, but it is not a classical incense material and it does not appear in the incense compendium 香乘. Treat burning peel as kitchen experimentation, not as a tradition, and keep the quantities small because the sugar in a candied peel will scorch.

**Why does my citron have almost no smell?** Three common reasons: it was cold-stored and has not warmed up, it carries a shipping wax that seals the oil glands, or it is simply old and its volatiles are spent. Let it sit at room temperature for a day, wipe off condensation, and smell the stem end before concluding anything about the fruit.

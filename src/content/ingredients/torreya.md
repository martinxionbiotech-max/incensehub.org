> **Direct answer:** Torreya (香榧, xiāngfěi) is the kernel of *Torreya grandis*, a yew-family conifer cultivated in eastern China for its large, oil-rich seed. The nut is a valued food, and the *Bencao Gangmu* records it as 榧实 under the literary name 玉山果, "jade-mountain fruit". No classical source treats it as a burned incense material.

## A nut from the yew family

**Direct answer:** *Torreya grandis* is an evergreen tree of the yew family, not a pine and not a broadleaf nut tree. It carries linear-lanceolate leaves, produces a single hard seed partly wrapped in a fleshy aril, and can reach the height of a forest tree.

The yew family is a small group of ancient conifers, and it is not what most readers picture when they hear "conifer". There are no woody cones of the pine kind. The seed sits exposed at the end of a short stalk, enclosed for most of its length by a fleshy covering that is botanically an aril rather than a fruit wall. That detail matters for anyone describing the material precisely: 香榧 is a seed, and the soft outer layer is an aril, not the flesh of a fruit.

The tree's native range is eastern China. The Flora of China account places it between 200 and 1,400 m, on mountain slopes, in open valleys, and along streams. Its recorded range covers Zhejiang, southern Jiangsu, Anhui, Jiangxi, northern Fujian, western Hunan, and northeastern Guizhou. That is a broad band of warm-temperate hill country, humid but well drained, and it matches the tree's preference for sheltered slopes rather than exposed ridges.

The seed is large by conifer standards: ellipsoid to ovoid, 2–4.5 cm long and 1.2–2.5 cm wide. Inside the hard shell is an oily kernel, and that kernel is the reason the tree is cultivated. The shell is thick enough to protect the kernel through storage, and the aril is fleshy enough to be removed cleanly after harvest. The combination is unusual among conifers, most of which offer small seeds or seeds wrapped in cones.

The reproductive cycle is slow. Flora of China gives pollination in April and seed maturity in September–November of the second year. A seed that is pollinated this spring will not be harvested until the autumn of the following year. That two-year cycle shapes the harvest, the cash flow of a grove, and the way the nut is traded. It also explains why a bad flowering year can cast a shadow over two seasons rather than one.

The genus has other species in Asia and North America, so the English name "torreya" is genus-wide. The Chinese nut is *T. grandis*; an offer that names only the genus has not named the product.

## What 香榧 and 玉山果 name

**Direct answer:** 香榧 is the everyday name of the edible seed, 榧实 the classical drug name, and 玉山果 a literary name preserved in the *Bencao Gangmu*. Three registers, one tree.

The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) places the torreya in its fruit section, 果之三, under 榧实, and records 玉山果 as a classical name. The entry then quotes Su Dongpo (苏轼):

> "彼美玉山果，粲为金盘实。" — "That lovely jade-mountain fruit, gleaming as a dish of gold."

Li Shizhen's explanation turns on the wood. The tree, he says, is called 文木, "patterned wood", for its handsome grain, and that is why the character 榧 attaches to it. He adds that the finest come from Yushan county in Xinzhou — the historical prefecture around modern Shangrao in Jiangxi. The etymology is about timber and place, not about scent. That is worth noticing: the most famous name for the nut comes from the beauty of the tree's wood and the reputation of a growing district.

The name 玉山果 is a literary compliment with a geographic anchor. "Jade-mountain fruit" sounds like a colour description, but Li Shizhen's gloss ties it to a place and to the tree's figure. A reader who assumes a jade-green kernel is reading poetry as botany.

The three names also show how Chinese material vocabulary layers. 香榧 is the everyday name, and 香 ("fragrant") is doing ordinary descriptive work. 榧实 is the pharmacopoeia's name, with 实 ("fruit/seed") marking the part. 玉山果 is the literary name, carried by a canonical text and a famous poet. None of them is a species name in the modern sense, and none of them tells a buyer which cultivar, which grade, or which year.

The record's limits are clear. It shows the seed was known, named, eaten, and discussed in materia medica; it does not show an incense use, a fragrance grade, or a medical benefit. Those are separate questions.

## Why a culinary nut has a page here

**Direct answer:** Torreya is catalogued because it is a named seed with a real classical record and a genuine place in China's food history. It is not catalogued as an incense material, and no such role is claimed for it.

An encyclopedia of incense materials faces a temptation: every aromatic plant wants to become an incense ingredient. Torreya is aromatic in a quiet way, it has a famous name, and it would be easy to write a page that implied a burned use. This page does the opposite. It records the material as a food and materia-medica seed, and it says so at the top.

That is not a rejection of the material. It is a condition of trustworthy cataloguing. A knowledge base that inflates every fragrant thing into an incense ingredient loses the ability to tell a reader which materials actually belong in a burner. The boundary is the value. Torreya helps draw it.

The seed also earns its place through comparison. Conifer seeds are a small, confusing category: some are foods, some are incense materials, some are both, and some are neither. Placing torreya beside cypress seed and ginkgo makes the differences visible. The reader can see that "seed" is a morphology, not a use.

There is an editorial point underneath. A material can be worth a page because it is often confused with something else. Torreya is regularly confused with cypress seed in casual Chinese-language search, and the confusion is understandable: both are small, hard, aromatic seeds with a roasted, nutty character. A page that separates them is more useful than a page that pretends the confusion does not exist.

The honest position is therefore positive, not apologetic. Torreya is a real material with a real record, a real aroma, and a real culinary value. It simply is not a classical incense material, and saying so is part of describing it accurately.

## Why the nut is costly

**Direct answer:** Torreya is expensive for structural reasons: a two-year reproductive cycle, a selective hand harvest, and a chain of post-harvest steps that cannot be rushed. The cost is in the biology and the labour, not in a marketing story.

The two-year cycle is the first constraint. A grove carries overlapping cohorts, and the picker must distinguish mature seed from immature seed on the same branch. That is not work a machine can do with the selectivity the crop requires. The harvest is spread over weeks, and each pass takes only what is ready.

The trees add a second constraint. They are tall, and the seed is borne on branches that are not convenient to reach. Harvesting is skilled, seasonal work, and the window is set by the seed's maturity rather than by a factory schedule. A grower who picks early loses fill; a grower who picks late loses seed to the ground.

Post-harvest handling adds the third constraint. The aril must be removed before it heats and spoils, the seed must be dried evenly, the shell must be kept intact for storage, and the roast must be controlled. Each step has a loss rate, and each loss is absorbed somewhere in the chain. The visible price of a bag of nuts is the sum of those steps, not a single farm-gate number.

Yields also vary from year to year. A poor flowering season, a late frost, or a wet harvest can reduce the crop, and the two-year cycle means the effect is not confined to one season. A buyer who sees a price swing should read it as an agricultural signal before reading it as a quality signal.

The commercial lesson is that cost and quality are different axes. An expensive lot can be stale, and a modestly priced lot can be fresh. The price reflects scarcity and handling; the quality reflects fill, oil, moisture, and storage. A buyer who uses price as a proxy for either one is guessing.

## The aroma of a food, not an incense

**Direct answer:** 香榧 smells nutty, woody, and lightly resinous, with a roasted sweetness that belongs to the kitchen rather than to the censer. The scent is real, but it is the aroma of a toasted oil-rich kernel, not the aroma of a material designed to smoulder.

The first impression is fat and warmth. A fresh kernel smells of roasted nuts, with a woody edge and a faint resinous note that recalls the tree's conifer relatives. There is a sweetness underneath, but it is the sweetness of cooked starch and oil, not the sweetness of a flower or a resin. The overall effect is quiet: the nut does not fill a room the way a resin does, and it does not project the way a fresh leaf does.

Heat develops the aroma rather than revealing a hidden incense character. A raw kernel is mild; a roasted kernel is richer, because the heat drives off moisture, browns the surface, and releases oil. That is a food transformation, and it is the same transformation that makes roasted nuts of many kinds smell good. The fact that the result is aromatic does not make the kernel an aromatic material in the trade sense.

The distinction is functional. An incense material is judged by how it burns: how it ignites, how it smoulders, what it leaves, and what the smoke does to the room. A food material is judged by how it chews and tastes. Torreya succeeds at the second and fails at the first. A kernel placed on heat will scorch, spit oil, and produce an acrid smoke long before it produces a pleasant fragrance.

That failure is informative. The oil content that makes the nut delicious is the same oil content that makes it a poor incense material. Fat does not vaporise cleanly at the temperatures of a smouldering coal; it decomposes, and the decomposition products read as burnt rather than fragrant. The mechanism is simple: kernel oil → heat → incomplete combustion → soot and acrid smoke, not a clean aromatic plume.

Smelling 香榧 beside 柏子 is instructive: the cypress seed is drier and more resinous, the torreya rounder and more food-like. The nose can learn the boundary even when the vocabulary cannot.

## What the "fragrant" in 香榧 means

**Direct answer:** In 香榧, the character 香 describes a roasted, nutty, appetising food smell. It does not mean "incense grade", and it does not place the nut in the 香材 category. The same character appears in 香菜 (coriander) and 香米 (fragrant rice), where it marks kitchen appeal rather than ritual use.

Chinese food names use 香 generously. It can mean fragrant, savoury, appetising, or simply "smells good when cooked", and the context decides which. A 香瓜 is a melon; a 香肠 is a sausage; a 香榧 is a nut. None of them is an incense material, and no reader would assume otherwise from the name alone. The confusion arises only when the character is lifted out of the kitchen and read through the vocabulary of incense.

The incense vocabulary is more specific. 香材 names a material selected for burning; 香方 names a formula; 焚香 names the act. Those terms imply preparation, blending, and a designed scent. A food that happens to smell good has none of that. It has a kitchen use, a harvest, and a shelf life. The categories overlap in the character 香 and nowhere else.

The aroma itself confirms the point. The smell of a roasted torreya is a roasted-nut smell, produced by heat acting on oil and protein. It is closer to toasted almond or roasted sesame than to sandalwood or resin. A perfumer could borrow the effect, but the material is not built for the censer: it is built for the mouth.

This matters for search as much as for chemistry. A reader looking for 香榧 may want a nut, a recipe, or a gift; a reader looking for 香材 wants something else. A page that blurs the two serves neither.

"Fragrant" is also a sensory claim rather than a grade. There is no torreya grade based on scent intensity, no aromatic threshold, and no burn test. The buyer's useful questions are about freshness, fill, and uniformity, not about how loudly the nut announces itself. A quiet, fresh kernel is better than a loud, stale one.

## From pollination to roast: the two-year chain

**Direct answer:** The torreya harvest is the end of a two-year reproductive cycle, and the processing that follows is culinary: collect the mature seed, remove the fleshy aril, remove the hard shell, dry, and roast. There is no classical incense-processing step because there is no classical incense use.

The cycle begins with pollination in April. The pollen reaches the ovule, but fertilization and seed growth follow on their own schedule; the seed develops through the first season, overwinters on the tree, and matures in September–November of the second year. That is why a grove can carry two cohorts at once — small green seeds from this year's pollination and mature seeds from last year's — and why the harvest is a careful selective pick rather than a single shake of the tree.

The harvest window matters. A seed picked before maturity has a thinner kernel and a less developed oil; a seed left too long may drop, crack, or be taken by animals. The picker is looking for the colour and feel of the aril, and the judgement is made seed by seed on a tree that carries more than one age class. This is skilled work, and it is one reason the nut is expensive relative to more mechanised crops.

After harvest, the fleshy aril has to come off promptly. Left in a heap, the aril heats up, and the surface becomes a substrate for mould and bacteria. The chain is unforgiving: damp aril → heat builds in the pile → microbial growth → musty or fermented off-flavour → the kernel is damaged before it is ever dried. The practical rule is to dehusk and spread the seed to dry rather than to store it wet.

The hard shell comes next. It protects the kernel during drying and storage, and it is removed close to the point of sale or roasting. A kernel sold in the shell keeps longer; a kernel sold shelled is more convenient and more exposed. The trade-off is the usual one for nuts: protection versus inspection.

Roasting is the final transformation, and it is a chain of its own. Heat drives moisture out of the kernel → the surface temperature rises → browning reactions and oil release begin → the nutty, sweet aroma develops. Past the right point, the same heat drives oxidation of the oil → bitter, rancid, or burnt notes appear. The roast is a narrow window, and it is judged by smell and sound as much as by clock.

There is no incense step to add. The material is not washed in resin, not bound with powder, not aged in a censer. The processing tradition is culinary because the use is culinary. A page that invented a "traditional incense preparation" for torreya would be inventing a tradition, not describing one.

## Grading the nut

**Direct answer:** Torreya is graded as a food, not as an aromatic. The useful criteria are kernel fill, colour, aroma, shell condition, moisture, and size uniformity — all of which predict how the nut will eat, not how it will burn.

A good kernel is plump and heavy for its size. A hollow or shrivelled kernel is a failure of fill, and it usually means a thin, poor-quality nut. Colour should be pale ivory to light yellow; a dark, translucent, or greasy look suggests age, heat damage, or oxidation. The aroma should be sweet, roasted, and nutty, with no paint-like, musty, or rancid edge.

Shell condition is a proxy for handling. An intact, clean shell protects the kernel; a cracked or stained shell lets air and moisture in. Moisture is the quiet failure: a kernel that feels damp or soft, or a batch that clumps in the bag, is a candidate for mould even if it looks fine on the surface.

| Criterion | Good sign | Failure mode | What it predicts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kernel fill | Plump, heavy for size | Hollow, shrivelled | Oil content and texture |
| Colour | Pale ivory to light yellow | Dark, translucent, greasy | Age or heat damage |
| Aroma | Sweet, roasted, nutty | Rancid, musty, paint-like | Oil freshness |
| Shell | Intact, clean, thin | Cracked, stained, mouldy | Handling and storage |
| Moisture | Dry surface, no clumping | Soft, damp, clumping | Mould risk |
| Size | Uniform | Mixed sizes | Even roasting |

Size grading is a commercial convenience with a real consequence. Mixed sizes roast unevenly: the small kernels finish before the large ones, so a batch tuned to the large size over-roasts the small, and a batch tuned to the small under-roasts the large. Uniformity is therefore not cosmetic. It is a processing control.

The grade language of the nut trade is not the grade language of incense. There is no "incense grade" torreya, no resin content threshold, no burn test. A seller who offers such a grade is borrowing the vocabulary of a different trade. The buyer should ask food questions: fill, freshness, moisture, and uniformity.

## What form is on offer

**Direct answer:** Torreya reaches buyers in four practical forms — in the shell, shelled raw, roasted in the shell, and roasted shelled — and each form carries a different freshness risk. For most buyers the form matters more than the size grade.

| Form | What it is | Advantage | Risk | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In shell, raw | Whole seed with the hard shell intact | Longest protection; kernel can be inspected after cracking | Heavy; shell waste; uneven roasting at home | Storage and home roasting |
| Shelled, raw | Kernel only | Convenient; defects visible | Exposed to air; dries; goes rancid faster | Cooking and immediate roasting |
| Roasted, in shell | Whole seed, roasted | Traditional eating form; aroma developed | Roast can be uneven; the shell hides kernel colour | Snacking and gifting |
| Roasted, shelled | Kernel roasted and ready to eat | Uniform, convenient | Shortest shelf life; oil fully exposed | Immediate consumption |

The shell is a barrier, and the barrier is the point. A kernel in its shell has a pocket of still air around it and a hard layer between the oil and the room. Once the shell is cracked or removed, that protection is gone, and the kernel begins to lose freshness at a rate set by temperature, light, and air exchange. The same nut can be a long-keeping commodity in the shell and a short-keeping snack when shelled.

Roasting is a process, not a grade. A roasted nut is not automatically better than a raw one; it is a nut that has been through a heat treatment, and the treatment can be good or careless. A strong roast can mask a tired kernel, which is why aroma alone is not enough evidence. Break one open and look at the colour, then chew a single kernel slowly. The roast should support the nut, not cover it.

The practical decision rule is short. Buy in the shell if storage is the priority; buy shelled raw if you plan to cook or roast within a short window; buy roasted shelled if the nuts will be eaten immediately. The size grade is a secondary question, and a uniform lot is more useful than a large but mixed one.

## Reading a label

**Direct answer:** A useful torreya label names the species, the origin, the form, the roast state, and the lot or harvest window. A label that names only "香榧" and a weight has told you the common name and nothing that predicts the contents.

Species is the first item because the common name is genus-wide. *Torreya grandis* is the Chinese culinary species; other torreyas are different plants. Origin is the second because the crop is associated with particular districts, and a district name is a reputation rather than a guarantee. Form is the third because in-shell, shelled, and roasted are different products with different shelf lives.

The roast state is often omitted, and it matters. "Roasted" can mean a light toast or a dark roast, and the two eat differently. A buyer who cares about the kernel's own flavour should prefer a lighter roast, where the nut is still visible under the process. A buyer who wants a shelf-stable snack may prefer a darker roast, provided the oil has not been pushed into bitterness.

Lot or harvest information is the mark of a supplier who expects to be asked. A harvest window is more useful than a printed "best before" alone, because it tells the buyer how old the crop is. A lot number is useful for traceability if something goes wrong.

Several claims deserve a cautious reading. "Wild" is a strong claim for a long-cultivated tree; most of the commercial crop is grown, and a wild claim should come with a documented source. "Organic" is a certification claim, not a mood, and it should be backed by a certificate that covers the actual lot. "Hand-picked" is plausible for this crop, but it is a process description, not a grade.

Origin marketing can mislead without lying: a famous county can describe a blend. A buyer who wants a specific origin should ask for lot documentation, not the front of the bag.

## A buyer's checklist

**Direct answer:** Eight checks cover most of the risk — species, form, fill, colour, aroma, moisture, shell condition, and lot age — and the order matters, because a later check cannot rescue a failure at an earlier one.

| Order | Check | Pass | Fail | Why it comes here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Species | *Torreya grandis* named | "Torreya" or "香榧" alone | A different species makes every later check irrelevant |
| 2 | Form | In shell, shelled, or roasted, stated | Form unclear | Form sets the shelf-life expectation |
| 3 | Fill | Plump, heavy for size | Hollow, shrivelled | Fill predicts oil and texture |
| 4 | Colour | Pale ivory to light yellow | Dark, translucent, greasy | Colour reveals age and heat damage |
| 5 | Aroma | Sweet, roasted, nutty | Rancid, musty, paint-like | Aroma confirms the oil is fresh |
| 6 | Moisture | Dry, free-running | Damp, clumping | Moisture predicts mould |
| 7 | Shell | Intact, clean | Cracked, stained, mouldy | The shell is the storage barrier |
| 8 | Lot age | Harvest window or lot number | No traceability | Age explains what the other checks show |

The order is a diagnostic sequence, not a preference. If the species is wrong, the fill and colour are interesting but useless. If the form is unclear, the buyer cannot know whether the shell is supposed to be there. If the kernel is dark and greasy, a sweet aroma may be the roast covering a tired nut.

The last check is the one buyers skip. A lot number and a harvest window tell you how old the material is, and age is the single best predictor of what the other checks will find. A fresh lot can survive imperfect storage for a while; an old lot cannot be rescued by good storage.

None of these checks requires a laboratory. They require light, a nose, a knife, and the willingness to break one nut before buying a bag. That is the practical significance of the whole page: the expensive mistakes in this trade are category mistakes, and category mistakes are cheap to avoid.

## Storage: the oil is the clock

**Direct answer:** Torreya keeps as long as its oil keeps. The shell slows the clock, but it does not stop it, and once the kernel is exposed the ordinary chemistry of nut storage takes over.

The mechanism is the same one that governs any oil-rich nut. Oxygen attacks the unsaturated fat in the kernel → peroxides form → the fat breaks down into smaller molecules → the aroma turns from roasted-sweet to paint-like, waxy, or bitter. Heat speeds every step, and light adds its own energy to the reaction. The result is not a food that becomes dangerous in a dramatic way; it is a food that becomes unpleasant, and then stale.

Storage should therefore target the three accelerants. Keep the nuts cool, keep them dark, and keep air away. An airtight jar in a cool cupboard is better than a paper bag on a warm shelf. For longer storage, sealing and refrigerating or freezing can slow the decline, provided the nuts are brought back to room temperature before the container is opened, so that condensation does not form on the kernels.

The shell is the first line of defence, and it is why in-shell nuts are the better storage form. A cracked shell is a wound: it lets air reach the kernel and gives mould a place to start. Sort out cracked and stained nuts before storage rather than after.

The sensory check is simple and worth doing before any purchase in quantity. Smell the nuts in the bag; a fresh lot smells roasted and sweet, while a tired lot smells of old oil. Break a kernel and look at the colour; a dark, translucent, greasy kernel is a warning. Chew one nut and wait a few seconds; the aftertaste is where rancidity shows up first, because the volatile breakdown products linger.

This is also why a "best before" date should be read as a storage claim rather than a harvest claim. The date tells you what the packer expects under stated conditions. It does not tell you how the nuts were handled before packing, and it does not survive a hot car or an open bag.

## Three seeds that get confused

**Direct answer:** 香榧, 柏子, and 银杏 are three different seeds from three different plant groups, and only one of them is a classical incense material. The confusion comes from the shared word "seed" and from a similar roasted, nutty aroma.

| Name | Plant | Group | Role | Aroma |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 香榧 (fěi) | *Torreya grandis* | Taxaceae (yew family) | Culinary nut; materia medica | Nutty, woody, resinous, roasted |
| 柏子 (bǎizǐ) | *Platycladus orientalis* | Cupressaceae (cypress family) | Incense seed | Roasted, nutty, softly resinous |
| 银杏 (yínxìng) | *Ginkgo biloba* | Ginkgoaceae (ginkgo family) | Food seed, prepared | Starchy, sweet; fleshy coat unpleasant |

The first contrast is taxonomic. Torreya is in the yew family; cypress seed is in the cypress family; ginkgo is in a family of its own and is not a conifer in the usual sense. The second contrast is use. Cypress seed is the one that belongs in a censer; torreya and ginkgo are foods. The third is aroma. All three can smell nutty when roasted, but only cypress seed has the dry, resinous register that reads as incense.

The practical confusion is between torreya and cypress seed, because both are small, hard, brown seeds with a roasted scent and a Chinese name that a non-specialist may not recognise. A buyer who orders "柏子" and receives "香榧" has received a food, not an incense material, and the mistake may not be obvious until the material is heated. The reverse mistake is less costly but still a category error.

Ginkgo deserves its own caution. The edible part is the kernel inside a hard shell, and the fleshy outer coat is notorious for its unpleasant smell and for the care needed in handling. Ginkgo is not a substitute for torreya in any sense, and its preparation has its own rules. It appears in this encyclopedia as a food seed with a long cultural record, not as an incense material.

The broader lesson is that common names are not identities. "Seed" describes a morphology; "nut" describes a food use; "incense material" describes a burning use. A single material can occupy more than one category, and the categories do not imply one another.

## Safety: kernel, shell, and the limits of "edible"

**Direct answer:** The edible part of *Torreya grandis* is the kernel, after the fleshy aril and the hard shell are removed. Edibility of the kernel does not extend to the leaves, bark, or fleshy covering, and it does not make the material safe to burn indoors without ventilation.

The precise framing matters because "torreya is edible" is true only of the kernel and only after ordinary preparation. The tree is in Taxaceae, a family that includes plants whose foliage and seeds are not food. A reader should not generalise from a bowl of roasted 香榧 to the whole family, and should not treat the aril or the shell as edible just because they surround an edible kernel.

For the kernel itself, the practical risks are the ordinary risks of tree nuts. Some people are allergic to tree nuts, and torreya is not exempt. Stored nuts can go rancid, and a rancid nut tastes bitter and oily in a way that is easy to recognise. Damp storage can grow mould. Small children can choke on whole nuts. These are not exotic hazards; they are the reasons nut storage and serving have ordinary rules.

Roasting is the usual commercial preparation, and it improves both texture and aroma. A buyer should follow the preparation the supplier specifies rather than assuming that a raw kernel is ready to eat. The page does not offer a preparation protocol because the safe details vary by product form and by local food regulation, and because this is an incense encyclopedia, not a cookbook.

The incense safety note is separate and non-negotiable. If someone experiments with burning torreya, the material will produce smoke and particulate matter, and the fat content will produce acrid decomposition products. Burn only in a well-ventilated space, use a heat-resistant holder, keep the material away from children and pets, and never leave a burning material unattended. A food aroma does not make a smoke safe to inhale. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general rules.

There is one more boundary worth stating. Torreya is not a medicine in this page's framing. The classical materia-medica record is a historical fact, and it is presented as such. No dose, preparation, or indication is offered, and no claim is made that eating the nut prevents or treats anything.

## Tradition and evidence

**Direct answer:** The culinary record is well documented and the classical naming record is genuine. The medicinal uses in the old texts are traditional belief, not modern clinical evidence. The page keeps those categories separate.

The documented facts are narrow: a Taxaceae tree from eastern China, a large oily edible kernel, a *Bencao Gangmu* record under 榧实 and 玉山果, and a long cultivation history. That is enough to identify the material.

The traditional belief is a different layer. Materia-medica texts discuss the seed in their own vocabulary, and that vocabulary is not the vocabulary of modern clinical research. A historical text can be quoted as history without being endorsed as therapy. The page quotes the names and the poem because they are cultural evidence; it does not translate them into medical advice.

The absence of an incense record is also a fact. It is not an insult to the material and not a gap that needs filling. The classical Chinese incense canon has its own materials, and torreya is not one of them. A reader who wants to know what torreya is should be told the truth: a celebrated nut with a literary name, not a forgotten censer ingredient.

## FAQ

**What is torreya (香榧)?** The kernel of *Torreya grandis*, an edible conifer seed from eastern China. It is eaten as a nut, usually after roasting, and the classical materia medica records it under 榧实 with the literary name 玉山果.

**Why is torreya called jade-mountain fruit (玉山果)?** The name is literary, not a colour description. The *Bencao Gangmu* preserves 玉山果, quotes Su Dongpo's line about the fruit on a golden dish, and links the tree's name to its patterned wood.

**How does torreya smell when roasted?** Nutty, woody, and lightly resinous, with a roasted sweetness. It is the aroma of an oil-rich kernel, not the aroma of a resin or a wood, and it is quiet rather than projecting.

**Does torreya belong in a censer?** No classical source puts it there. The record is culinary and materia-medica, and the kernel's oil content makes it a poor burner: heated fat decomposes into acrid smoke rather than a clean aromatic plume.

**How is torreya distinguished from cypress seed?** They belong to different conifer families and different trades. Torreya is a yew-family nut eaten as food; cypress seed is a cypress-family seed used in incense. A shared word and a roasted smell are the only things they have in common.

For the nearest comparisons, see [ginkgo nut (银杏)](/ingredients/ginkgo/), [cypress seed (柏子)](/ingredients/cypress-seed/), and [pine resin (松香)](/ingredients/pine-resin/); the [scent guide](/blog/scent-guide/) places torreya's quiet nutty note.

## Summary

Torreya (香榧) is the large, oily kernel of *Torreya grandis*, a yew-family conifer cultivated in eastern China. Its classical standing comes from the materia medica — 榧实 and 玉山果 in the *Bencao Gangmu*, with Su Dongpo's poem and Li Shizhen's etymology — and its aroma is the quiet, roasted, woody smell of an oil-rich nut. It is food, not incense; it is not a substitute for cypress seed; and its page exists to keep those distinctions clear.

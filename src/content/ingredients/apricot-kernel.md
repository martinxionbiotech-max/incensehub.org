**Direct answer:** 杏仁 (xìngrén) is the seed that sits inside an apricot's stone. The tree is *Prunus armeniaca*, a rose-family fruit species with a long history of cultivation in China and Central Asia, and the kernel — not the flesh — is the material this page describes. It smells sweet, nutty and marzipan-like, with a bitter edge that comes from the same chemistry as the sweetness. The classical record files 杏仁 as a food and a materia-medica item, not as a staple of the incense burner, and this page keeps that distinction.

## Key takeaways

> **Direct answer:** Six variables decide what a batch of 杏仁 is: species, sweet-or-bitter type, form, freshness, fat content, and the amygdalin load that follows from the type. The table sets them out, and the sections below explain what each one changes.

Three habits cause most of the confusion around this material. The first is reading an almond smell as proof of almond content, when the smell is a molecule that several stone-fruit kernels produce. The second is reading the word "sweet" as a safety grade, when it only describes a lower — not absent — cyanogenic load. The third is reading a line in a materia medica as an incense pedigree, when the two genres of writing describe different uses of the same plant.

## What the kernel actually is

> **Direct answer:** 杏仁 is the embryo inside the apricot's stony pit. The apricot is a drupe, and its layers — skin, flesh, stone, seed — are four different materials with four different uses and four different risk profiles.

A drupe is a fruit built around a single hard pit, and the apricot is a textbook example. The outer skin (exocarp) is thin and downy. The flesh (mesocarp) is the part eaten fresh, dried, or cooked, and it is where the fruit's own perfume sits. The stone (endocarp) is the woody shell that protects the seed. Inside that stone sits the kernel: two fat cotyledons wrapped in a thin brown seed coat, rich in oil and protein, and carrying the bitter chemistry that gives the material its character.

The Chinese name is precise about which of those layers is meant. 杏 (xìng) is the apricot; 仁 (rén) means a seed or kernel. 杏仁 therefore points at the seed itself, leaving out the fruit and the pit. That precision matters for a buyer, because apricot flesh is a bland, sugary fruit material while the seed is fatty and cyanogenic. They are not interchangeable in any application, and a supplier who ships one when the other was ordered has shipped a different material with a different hazard.

There is a second layer of terminology worth fixing early. The hard "stone" or "pit" is the endocarp. The thin brown skin directly around the kernel is the seed coat. The pale kernel beneath it is the part used. When a specification talks about "shell" it may mean either the woody pit or the seed coat, and the two are removed by different steps — cracking versus blanching — so ambiguous language in a purchase order can hide a processing difference.

Composition explains behaviour. A kernel is roughly half oil, a substantial share protein, with sugars and fibre making up the rest, which is why it tastes rich and why it does not burn like a wood or a resin. Fat melts before it chars, and melting fat produces a greasy, acrid smoke. No amount of blending skill turns a fatty seed into a self-supporting incense material; the fat is the reason the material belongs in the kitchen and the perfumer's palette more than in the censer.

## Where the tree grows, and what the botany says

> **Direct answer:** *Prunus armeniaca* is a small tree, 5–8 m tall and occasionally to 12 m, with a rounded crown, broad ovate leaves, and white to pink flowers that open before the leaves. The fruit is a drupe that ripens white, yellow, or orange, often flushed red, and the kernel inside it is the traded material.

Flora of China describes the apricot as a tree of 5–8 m, reaching 12 m in good conditions, with a spherical crown and broad-ovate leaves. Blooms precede the leaves and measure 2–4.5 cm across, with petals in white, pink, or a red flush — the trait that makes the tree a spring ornamental as well as an orchard crop. The drupe follows, ranging from white through yellow to orange, frequently blushed with red where the sun hits it.

Its home range runs through East and Central Asia, and cultivation in China and further west is old enough that the wild ancestry is tangled. The epithet *armeniaca* is a historical attribution rather than a statement of origin: early European botany associated the tree with Armenia, while the cultivated lineages are older and further east. Flora of China keeps the species in *Armeniaca*; other taxonomic databases place it in *Prunus*, and both names point at the same tree. A buyer does not need to settle the taxonomy, but a label that gives only "apricot" and no species has skipped a step that matters when other stone fruits are in the same warehouse.

One commercial fact shapes everything about kernel supply: the kernel is usually a by-product. Orchards are planted for fruit, and the pits are what remains after drying, canning, or juicing. That has three consequences. Kernel supply rises and falls with the fruit crop rather than with demand for kernels. The cultivar is chosen for fruit quality, not for kernel size or chemistry, so lots vary more than a purpose-grown seed crop would. And the price of a kernel lot is tied to the cost of cracking and drying pits, which is labour-heavy and often done at small scale.

For a buyer, the by-product origin explains a pattern of complaints that otherwise look like fraud: inconsistent kernel size, mixed cultivars, and variable bitterness between lots. Those are the normal signatures of a side-stream material. They are not proof of adulteration, and they are not a reason to skip a specification — they are the reason a specification is necessary.

## Names, labels, and the sweet–bitter split in the trade

> **Direct answer:** The written name is stable — 杏仁 for the kernel, 甜梅 as a classical name for the fruit — while the trade labels are regional. Chinese kitchens commonly separate 南杏 ("southern apricot") from 北杏 ("northern apricot"), and the pair describes sweet versus bitter kernels, not two botanical species.

The old name 甜梅, glossed as "sweet plum", stands in the *Bencao Gangmu* entry for 杏, and it tells a reader what the fruit was valued for: sweetness, at a time when that was a luxury in a fruit. The name attaches to the fruit, not to the kernel, and it says nothing about the seed's bitterness. Reading 甜梅 as a promise that the kernel is sweet is exactly the kind of small misreading that a name-based purchase decision runs on.

The 南杏 / 北杏 pair is a kitchen convention. 南杏 ("southern apricot kernel") is the larger, milder, sweeter kernel used in soups and desserts; 北杏 ("northern apricot kernel") is the smaller, more bitter one used sparingly for its stronger character. The geographical words describe a trade route and a type, not two species with two ranges. Regional usage varies, and a supplier may use the labels loosely, so the words alone are not a specification. What a buyer actually needs is the type, stated plainly, plus a description of the process the kernels have been through.

English adds its own trap. "Apricot kernel" is sometimes used for the sweet culinary kernel and "apricot seed" for the same thing, while "bitter apricot kernel" marks the high-amygdalin type. In Chinese, the almond is 扁桃仁 or 巴旦木, not 杏仁, and older translations sometimes blur the two because the aroma is so similar. A label that says "almond" may mean the true almond (*Prunus dulcis*), a bitter almond, or an apricot kernel standing in for either. The plant name is the only reliable anchor.

There is a further authenticity question that labels rarely answer. Stone-fruit kernels from peach, plum, and apricot can resemble one another once the pits are cracked, and the trade is not always species-pure. That is an editorial caution rather than an accusation: mixed lots happen, and the practical response is a named species on the paperwork and, for industrial volumes, an identity check rather than a look at the sack.

## The almond note is a molecule, not a species

> **Direct answer:** The marzipan smell is benzaldehyde, a small aldehyde released when amygdalin in the kernel is broken down. Apricot, bitter almond, peach, and cherry laurel all produce it, which is why they smell related without being the same plant.

Amygdalin is a cyanogenic glycoside: a sugar unit attached to a cyanohydrin, which is a carbon carrying both a nitrile group and a hydroxyl. In an intact kernel the glycoside and the enzyme that acts on it are kept apart in different compartments, so nothing happens. Crush the kernel, add water, and the compartments merge. The enzyme β-glucosidase removes the sugar, the intermediate prunasin is cleaved again, and the resulting mandelonitrile breaks down into benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide.

That sequence is the whole story of the material, and it is worth writing as a chain because every step has a practical consequence:

intact kernel → amygdalin and β-glucosidase held in separate compartments → crushing, grinding, or chewing merges them → water allows the enzyme to work → prunasin → mandelonitrile → benzaldehyde plus hydrogen cyanide → the almond note and the toxic hazard appear together.

The two products explain two things at once. Benzaldehyde is the note the perfumer wants: sweet, cherry-almond, slightly woody, with a hint of marzipan. Hydrogen cyanide is the reason the same kernel is a regulated food material. They are not side products of each other; they are fragments of the same molecule, released by the same reaction, in the same moment. A material that smells more strongly of almond is, other things equal, a material that has released more of both.

That chemistry is shared across the genus. Bitter almond (*Prunus dulcis* var. *amara*) is the classic high-amygdalin source. Peach kernel (桃仁) carries the same glycoside and has its own classical record. Cherry laurel foliage bruises to the same smell. Even sweet almond, eaten by the handful, contains a trace of the same compound. The differences between them are quantitative — how much glycoside, how much enzyme activity, how the material is processed — rather than a difference in kind.

Which leads to a correction worth stating plainly: a marzipan smell in a finished product is not evidence that apricot kernel is present. Benzaldehyde is a standard aroma chemical, made synthetically at industrial scale and used in flavours and fragrances without any plant material at all. The smell tells you which molecule is in the air. It does not tell you which plant, which part, or which process put it there. For a buyer, that means the specification has to be documentary and chemical, not olfactory.

## How the aroma is built

> **Direct answer:** 杏仁's scent has three layers: a volatile aldehyde top that reads as marzipan, a soft fatty body from the fixed oil, and a bitter base that sits partly on the tongue rather than in the nose. The top layer is the aromatic one, and it is also the layer that disappears first.

Benzaldehyde is a small molecule with a low boiling point relative to the oils around it, so it leaves the material readily at room temperature and leaves even faster when the kernel is heated. That is why a bowl of ground kernel perfumes a kitchen within minutes and why the same bowl smells flat a day later. The note is a top note by physics, not by fashion.

The fixed oil provides the body. Apricot kernel oil is mostly triglycerides of oleic and linoleic acids, with a bland, faintly nutty taste and almost no odour of its own once refined. It carries the mouthfeel of the kernel and the sense of richness that a taster reads as "nutty", but it contributes little to the aroma. This split — volatile note in the meal, fixed oil in the press — is the single most useful fact for anyone trying to use the material in a fragrance context.

Bitterness is a separate channel. The tongue detects it, and it persists after the aroma has gone. Part of the bitterness comes from amygdalin and its fragments, part from the kernel's own bitter principles, and the balance varies with type and processing. A kernel can therefore smell sweet and taste bitter, which is not a contradiction but two different chemical systems reporting at once.

Heat complicates the picture. Warming a kernel drives off benzaldehyde, which lowers the aroma, and at the same time promotes oxidation of the oil, which adds stale, cardboard-like notes. Roasting therefore moves the material in two directions at once: less fresh marzipan, more toasted fat. The result can smell pleasant — toasted nuts are a familiar and welcome aroma — but it is no longer the same note, and a formulator who substitutes roasted meal for raw meal will find the blend reads differently after a week.

## Sweet versus bitter: the axis that decides everything

> **Direct answer:** The sweet-versus-bitter split is the most important fact about a kernel lot, because it tracks both the aroma and the cyanogenic load. Sweet kernels are lower in amygdalin and used as food; bitter kernels are higher and treated as a hazard, not a flavour.

| Trait | Sweet kernel | Bitter kernel |
|---|---|---|
| Amygdalin load | Lower | Higher |
| Taste | Mild, oily, softly marzipan | Sharply bitter, lingering |
| Aroma | Gentle benzaldehyde, creamy | Stronger, sharper almond note |
| Kitchen role | Soups, desserts, kernel drinks | Small quantities in traditional preparations |
| Safety | Still cyanogenic; not a free food | Documented concern; not a casual snack |
| What to ask for | Type stated, plus process | Type stated, plus assay and process |
| Where it fails | Sold as "safe" because sweet | Sold as a health product |

The word "sweet" is comparative. It means the kernel has less amygdalin than the bitter type, not that it has none. Sweet kernels still contain the glycoside, still release benzaldehyde when crushed, and still produce hydrogen cyanide when the enzyme is allowed to work. A person who eats a handful of sweet kernels is not eating a neutral food; they are eating a food with a known cyanogenic component that is normally consumed in modest amounts and often after processing.

The word "bitter" is not a reliable test either. Bitterness varies with cultivar, harvest, storage, and the part of the kernel tasted, so a kernel that tastes mild can still carry a meaningful load. Using taste as a safety screen is doubly unwise because tasting is itself an exposure route. The professional substitute is a declared type plus, at industrial volume, an analytical assay for amygdalin.

Traditional kitchens developed processes around the bitter type rather than pretending it was safe. Soaking, boiling, and discarding the water are standard steps in preparing bitter kernels for food, and the logic is straightforward: amygdalin is water-soluble, and heat both leaches it out and denatures the enzyme that would liberate cyanide. Those steps reduce the load. They do not turn the kernel into an unlimited food, and they are not a licence to improvise with raw material. The traditional practice is best read as evidence that the hazard was recognised, not as evidence that it was eliminated.

## The classical record, and what it does not say

> **Direct answer:** In the fruit section (果之一) of the *Bencao Gangmu*, the entry is 杏; the kernel 杏仁 is treated there and the name 甜梅 is given. The text places both fruit and kernel in the kitchen and the medicine cabinet. That is a genuine record of use. It is not a record of incense use, and the two claims should not be merged.

The *Bencao Gangmu* is a late-16th-century materia medica compiled by Li Shizhen, and its fruit section (果之一) is a catalogue of what a literate household knew about fruit as food and as household remedy. The entry for 杏 covers the fruit, its kernel, and the name 甜梅. The register is practical: taste, preparation, what the material was used for, and what caution applied. Reading it as a pharmaceutical dossier would be anachronistic; reading it as evidence that 杏仁 was a familiar kitchen and medicine-cabinet material is fair.

What the record supports is narrow and solid. 杏仁 was known, named, and used. It was understood as a seed with a bitter character and a preparation step. It belonged to the food-and-medicine world rather than to the world of costly aromatics. None of that is in dispute.

What the record does not support is the modern habit of treating any classical mention as a quality certificate for incense. The incense canon — the *Xiang Cheng* (香乘) and the formulas around it — is a different genre with a different logic. Its materials are chosen for how they smoulder, how they hold a note, and how they behave in a compounded mixture. A fatty food seed does not fit that logic, and its absence from the canon is not an accident of history. It is a consequence of the material's physical behaviour.

There is an honest way to state the position, and it is the one this page takes: 杏仁 has a real classical record as food and as materia medica, and it is not a classical 香材. Its aroma can be used as a nuance in modern perfumery. Its history belongs to the kitchen and the medicine cabinet. Both statements are true, and neither needs the other to be inflated.

## Processing: what each step does to the note

> **Direct answer:** Processing runs from cracking the stone to shelling, blanching, drying, grinding, and pressing. Each step moves the material further from the intact seed: the aroma is released by rupture, the enzyme is stopped by heat, and the oil is separated from the aromatic meal.

The first step is mechanical. Stones are cracked and the kernels freed, then the thin seed coat is loosened, often with hot water, and rubbed away. Blanching does two things at once: it removes the coat and it begins to denature the enzyme, which is why blanched kernels smell milder than raw ones even before they are cooked.

Grinding is the step that changes the material most. An intact kernel is a sealed package; a ground kernel is an open one. Cell walls rupture, amygdalin meets β-glucosidase, and the reaction begins. The room fills with the marzipan note, and the meal becomes the form in which the cyanogenic chemistry is most available. This is the moment when good ventilation and a mask stop being optional in a workshop.

Heating the meal drives the reaction in a different direction. Above the temperature at which the enzyme loses activity, no new benzaldehyde is generated, and the aldehyde already present evaporates. What remains is a toasted, fatty material with a much weaker top note. The same heat accelerates oxidation of the oil, so the gain in "roasted" character is paid for in shelf life. A formulator choosing between raw and roasted meal is choosing between a live top note and a stable but duller body.

Pressing separates the oil from the meal. The volatile note stays largely with the meal, because benzaldehyde is more soluble in the aqueous and solid phase than in the expressed oil, and the crude oil is left bland. Refining removes free fatty acids, colour, and odour, producing the clear, nearly scentless oil sold for cosmetics and cooking. That is why apricot kernel oil is a carrier, not an aromatics material: the note was never in the oil to begin with.

The practical summary is short. If the goal is aroma, the useful form is freshly ground meal, handled wet and used quickly. If the goal is a stable carrier, the useful form is refined oil. Trying to get both from one form is the mistake that makes a formula disappoint.

## Forms, and how each one behaves

> **Direct answer:** 杏仁 is traded whole, blanched, ground, and as pressed oil. The whole and blanched forms are food materials; the meal is the aromatic form; the oil is a bland carrier with almost no scent.

| Form | What it is | Aroma | Sensible use | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried kernel | Intact seed in its coat | Faint until broken | Food, traditional preparations | Chewing releases the chemistry |
| Blanched kernel | Coat removed, lightly heated | Milder, rounded | Cooking, desserts | Heat has already reduced the enzyme |
| Ground meal | Ruptured kernel, high surface area | Strongest marzipan note | Blending, pastry, perfumery trials | Releases the most cyanide; use fresh |
| Cold-pressed oil | Crude expressed oil | Faint nutty, not almondy | Cooking, cosmetics | Not an aroma material |
| Refined oil | Filtered, deodorised | Nearly odourless | Carrier oil, skincare | The note has been removed |

The table is also a warning about labels. A seller offering "apricot kernel oil" for fragrance is offering a carrier, not a note. A seller offering "apricot kernel powder" for incense is offering a food material that will smoke and spit. Neither is fraudulent in itself; both become a problem when the buyer's expectation has not been set by the form.

For a small-scale maker, the whole kernel is the most forgiving purchase. It stores better than meal, its condition is visible, and the aroma is only released when the maker decides to release it. Meal is convenient and perishable. Oil is stable and aromatically empty. Choosing a form is therefore choosing how much control to keep over the chemistry.

## Storage and shelf life

> **Direct answer:** The clock on 杏仁 is the fat, not the aroma. Whole kernels keep for months in cool, dry, dark conditions; ground meal declines in weeks; pressed oil is the most stable form but also the least aromatic.

Fat oxidation is the slow reaction that ends a kernel's useful life. Oxygen attacks the unsaturated bonds in the oil, producing aldehydes and ketones that smell of old paint, cardboard, or soap. The reaction runs faster with warmth, light, and exposed surface, which is why whole kernels outlast meal by a wide margin: the seed coat and the intact cell structure limit the exposure.

Moisture matters for a second reason. Dry kernels are chemically quiet, because the enzyme that releases benzaldehyde needs water to work. A damp sack wakes that chemistry up, and the same moisture feeds mould. Keeping the material dry therefore does two jobs at once: it protects the fat and it keeps the cyanogenic reaction dormant.

A practical storage rule follows. Buy whole kernels when storage life matters, keep them cool, dark, and sealed, and grind only what will be used in the coming weeks. Meal can be frozen in small portions if a larger batch must be held, but freezing does not reverse oxidation that has already begun; it only slows what is left. Oil should be treated as a carrier with its own shelf life, stored away from heat and light, and smelled before use. Rancidity in a carrier oil is easy to detect and impossible to blend away.

The chain is short and unforgiving:

moisture and warmth → enzyme activity resumes and the oil begins to oxidise → benzaldehyde is released while the fat turns → the material smells bitter, soapy, and stale → no downstream step recovers the fresh marzipan note.

For a buyer, the practical significance is that shelf life is a specification, not an afterthought. A lot with no harvest date and no storage history is a lot whose age is unknown, and age is the variable that decides whether the kernel tastes of apricot or of old oil.

## Quality: a working decision table

> **Direct answer:** Quality in 杏仁 is mostly a question of type, freshness, and soundness, not of size or polish. The decisive variables are the declared type, the condition of the fat, and the absence of mould, insects, and foreign matter.

| Check | What good looks like | What failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Type declared | Sweet or bitter, stated on the lot | "Apricot kernel" with no type |
| Species declared | *Prunus armeniaca* named | Generic "kernel" or "seed" |
| Kernel fill | Plump, uniform, intact | Shrivelled, split, broken |
| Moisture | Dry to the touch, no clumping | Soft, tacky, warm in the sack |
| Fat condition | Clean nutty smell, no sharpness | Rancid, paint-like, or soapy |
| Mould and insects | None visible, no musty smell | Webbing, dust, discoloured patches |
| Foreign matter | No stone fragments or grit | Grit at the bottom of the sack |
| Documentation | Crop, type, process, assay | Nothing beyond a trade name |

Size grading is the check buyers notice first and the one that matters least. A large kernel is not a better kernel; it is a kernel from a cultivar selected for fruit size. The variables that change performance are invisible in a photograph: the amygdalin load, the age of the fat, and the moisture that has been sitting in the sack since drying.

Mould deserves its own line because it is a food-safety issue rather than an aroma defect. Stone-fruit kernels are oily seeds stored in bulk, and oily seeds in damp conditions are a familiar substrate for mould growth. A musty smell is not a style note to be blended away; it is a reason to reject a lot. The same applies to insect damage, which brings frass and off-notes that no amount of roasting fixes.

For industrial buyers, the documentation is the quality system. A lot that names the species, the type, the crop year, and the process can be compared with the next lot. A lot that names none of those things cannot be compared with anything, and every delivery becomes a new first impression. That is the practical argument for a short spec, not a bureaucratic one.

## The stone-fruit kernel confusion map

> **Direct answer:** Apricot kernel sits in a family of kernels that share benzaldehyde and differ in amygdalin load and use. Sweet apricot, bitter apricot, bitter almond, sweet almond, peach kernel, and cherry laurel are six different materials with one common note.

| Material | Plant | Amygdalin | Scent | Usual role | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sweet apricot kernel | *Prunus armeniaca* | Lower | Soft marzipan, oily | Food, desserts | Cyanogenic at volume |
| Bitter apricot kernel | *Prunus armeniaca* (bitter type) | Higher | Sharp almond, bitter | Traditional preparations, small doses | Documented hazard |
| Bitter almond | *Prunus dulcis* var. *amara* | High | Intense almond | Flavouring, historic pharmacy | High; regulated |
| Sweet almond | *Prunus dulcis* | Low | Mild, sweet, nutty | Food, carrier oil | Low but not zero |
| Peach kernel | *Prunus persica* | Present | Bitter, almond-adjacent | Classical materia medica | Cyanogenic |
| Cherry laurel | *Prunus laurocerasus* | Present in foliage and seed | Strong almond on bruising | Ornamental; historic distillate | Not a food material |

The map is useful because it separates two questions that a smell-based judgement fuses. The first question is "which molecule am I smelling?" — and the answer is usually benzaldehyde, whichever row of the table the material comes from. The second is "which plant, which part, and how much of the glycoside?" — and that answer cannot come from the nose. A buyer who can keep those questions apart will avoid most of the expensive mistakes in this corner of the trade.

The peach row is worth a note of its own. 桃仁 has a classical record and a place in traditional practice, and it is not a substitute for 杏仁 in a formula or a kitchen. The two kernels look similar once processed, smell related, and behave differently enough that substitution should be a deliberate decision with a stated reason, not a supply-chain convenience.

## What happens when the kernel meets fire

> **Direct answer:** 杏仁 burns badly on its own. The fat melts and smokes before the solid chars, the aldehyde flashes off in the first moments, and what remains is a greasy, bitter, toasted smoke with no ember structure to sustain it.

The sequence is worth following because it explains the material's absence from the incense canon better than any argument from tradition:

solid kernel → heat arrives → fat melts and migrates to the surface → the melted fat burns with a smoky, yellow flame or smoulders greasily → the benzaldehyde fraction evaporates ahead of the char → the remaining protein and fibre char → acrid, bitter, toasted notes dominate the plume.

Two consequences follow. First, the aromatic window is short: the note a maker wants is front-loaded, and a long burn gives mostly the fat-and-char tail. Second, the material needs a substrate. A resin or a wood holds an ember because its structure supports a slow smouldering front; a fatty meal collapses, spatters, and goes out. In a stick or a cone, ground kernel can be carried by the binder and the wood powder, but it is a passenger, not the engine.

This is the technical reason the page keeps insisting that 杏仁 is not a 香材. It is not a judgement about the plant's dignity or the depth of its record. It is a statement about combustion: the material's physical form works against the way incense burns. Where the note is wanted, the perfumer's route — a small amount of the aldehyde, on a structure that can carry it — is the route that works.

## If you use it in a blend

> **Direct answer:** Use 杏仁 as a small accent on a structure that can burn, not as a base or a lead note. In 君臣佐使 terms it is a 佐 or 使 — an assistant that rounds a sweet accord — and its usefulness is measured in restraint.

The note pairs naturally with materials that share its edible-sweet register: benzoin and other balsams, sandalwood, vanilla-like notes, and soft florals. It also has an affinity with bitter partners, because the kernel's own bitterness gives the sweetness an edge; a green herbal note or a citrus peel keeps the accord from collapsing into dessert. What it does not pair with is a heavy smoke-forward blend, where the fat simply adds weight and acridity.

Dose logic runs against intuition. The cold material smells strong, so a maker's first instinct is to use little; in the burner the same material reads weak, so the instinct is to use more. More meal means more fat, which means a worse burn and a greasier plume. The way out is not a bigger dose but a different carrier: a tincture or an aroma preparation delivers the note without the fat, and a small amount of meal used as a textural accent delivers the idea without pretending to be a fuel.

There is a boundary here that is editorial rather than technical. A blend can use 杏仁 as a culinary-adjacent sweet note and be honest about it, or it can market the material as a classical 香材 and be misleading. The first is a legitimate modern composition. The second borrows authority the record does not give.

## Safety, stated plainly

> **Direct answer:** Two separate hazards attach to 杏仁: smoke from burning any incense, and cyanide from the kernel's amygdalin. The first is managed by ventilation and distance; the second by not treating raw kernels as a casual food.

Smoke first. Incense combustion produces particulate matter and carbon monoxide, and a small closed room concentrates both. Burn in a ventilated space, keep the burner away from the breathing zone, use short sessions rather than long ones, and keep children and pets out of the immediate area. Loose meal can spit and spark, so use a fire-safe holder on a stable, non-combustible surface. None of this is specific to apricot kernel; it applies to every burning material on this site.

Cyanide second, and more carefully. Amygdalin is not itself the poison; it is a precursor that releases hydrogen cyanide when the enzyme acts on it. That means the hazard depends on crushing, moisture, and time, which is why an intact kernel is not the same risk as a spoonful of fresh meal. Chewing is the most efficient way to start the reaction, because it supplies both the rupture and the water. Bitter kernels are the higher-load type and are not a casual snack; raw bitter kernels should not be eaten at all.

Heating reduces the load without guaranteeing its removal. Boiling and soaking leach the water-soluble glycoside and denature the enzyme, and traditional kitchen practice relies on both. The reduction is real and the residue is not zero, which is why "cooked" is not a synonym for "safe", and why home processing of bitter kernels is a poor place to experiment. Industrial processes can measure what they have achieved; a kitchen cannot.

Children are the group to think about first, because a small body reaches a given dose sooner and because kernels look like sweets. Keep bulk material out of reach, label it, and do not leave ground meal in an open bowl. If someone swallows a quantity of bitter kernels, treat it as a poisoning concern and contact a poison centre or emergency service rather than waiting for symptoms.

Health claims deserve a flat refusal. Amygdalin has been sold in some markets as a supplement under the name "vitamin B17" and promoted with claims that are not established; the compound is not a vitamin and its marketed uses are not supported by clinical evidence. This page makes no medical claim in either direction, and nothing here should be read as advice to consume or to avoid a kernel for a health reason. Anyone with a medical question should ask a clinician. Some jurisdictions set limits on hydrocyanic acid in stone-fruit kernels sold as food, and those limits vary [need-source].

## FAQ

> **Direct answer:** These five questions cover the practical core: what the kernel is, how it differs from almond, whether it burns, how the sweet and bitter types compare, and what the pressed oil actually smells of.

**What is apricot kernel (杏仁)?** The seed inside an apricot's pit. It comes from *Prunus armeniaca*, a member of the rose family, and China and Central Asia are its long-standing cultivation heartland. The kernel is oily and protein-rich, smells of marzipan when crushed, and carries amygdalin, a cyanogenic glycoside. Its record is culinary and medical; it is not a canonical incense material.

**Is apricot kernel the same as almond?** No. They are different species in the same genus, and the resemblance is chemical rather than botanical: both release benzaldehyde from amygdalin, so both smell of almond. Sweet almond (*Prunus dulcis*) is a low-amygdalin food; apricot kernels come in a mild type and a bitter type; bitter almond carries the highest load of the three. The shared note is a molecule, not an identity.

**Can you burn apricot kernel as incense?** You can put it in a burner, but it does not behave like an incense material. The fat melts and smokes, the aldehyde flashes off early, and the material cannot hold an ember on its own. It works as a small accent carried by a binder or a wood powder, and it fails as a fuel or a base note.

**What is the difference between sweet and bitter kernels, and are bitter ones safe?** The two are grades of one seed, and what separates them is how much amygdalin they carry. The kitchen labels 南杏 (southern, mild) and 北杏 (northern, bitter) describe the pair, though the words vary by region and are not botanical ranks. Bitter kernels are not a casual food: chewing releases hydrogen cyanide, and traditional soaking and boiling reduce the load without guaranteeing its removal. Raw bitter kernels should not be eaten, and a swallowed quantity is a poisoning concern.

**Does apricot kernel oil smell of marzipan?** Very little. The volatile benzaldehyde stays mostly with the pressed meal, and refining removes what remains, so the oil is bland and faintly nutty. That is why the oil is used as a carrier and the meal is the form that carries the aroma.

## Summary

杏仁 is the kernel of *Prunus armeniaca*, a Rosaceae fruit tree of Central and East Asian origin, and its story is a lesson in how a single molecule can define a material. Amygdalin, held inside the intact seed, breaks down on crushing and wetting into benzaldehyde and hydrogen cyanide: the marzipan note and the toxic hazard are products of the same reaction. Bitter almond, peach kernel, and cherry laurel run the same reaction, so the family resemblance in scent is chemical rather than botanical.

The classical record is real but narrow. 《本草纲目》果之一 carries 杏, its kernel 杏仁, and the name 甜梅, all in a culinary and medical register; the 香乘 tradition does not treat the kernel as a lead material. The physical reason is simple: a fatty seed melts, smokes, and cannot hold an ember, so it works as an accent rather than a fuel. The sweet-versus-bitter split, the freshness of the fat, and the declared species are the variables that decide what a lot actually is. Treat 杏仁 as a documented food seed with a genuine aromatic nuance, and the material will not disappoint; treat it as a classical incense ingredient, and both the record and the burner will contradict the claim.

> **Direct answer:** Dragon's blood — 血竭 (xuèjié) in Chinese — is the dried red resin that forms on the fruit of *Daemonorops draco*, a climbing rattan palm of the Arecaceae family native to Southeast Asia. It burns as a sweet, woody, balsamic resin and works as a red pigment at the same time, which is why one material turns up in incense blends, lacquer and craft colour, and the materia medica. The English name is shared with unrelated red resins, so the species on the label matters more than the name on the box.

## Key facts

> **Direct answer:** The essentials fit in a short list: the name, the plant, the material, the scent, the colour, and what the resin was used for.

- **Name in Chinese:** 血竭, read *xuèjié* and written with the characters for blood and exhaustion.
- **Plant of origin:** *Daemonorops draco*, also listed as *Calamus draco*, a rattan palm in the Arecaceae.
- **Material:** the dried resin that gathers on the fruit — not a wood, not a bark, not a distilled oil.
- **Scent:** sweet and balsamic, with resinous and woody depth, and a smoke edge once warmed.
- **Colour:** deep red to dark red-brown in the lump, vivid red once ground.
- **Classical role:** incense resin, red pigment and dye, and an entry in the materia medica.
- **Forms sold:** lumps and powder.
- **Grading:** no published commercial ladder; the judgement rests on species, colour and scent.
- **Regional trade name:** jernang in the Malay-Indonesian sphere.

## The name covers more than one resin

> **Direct answer:** "Dragon's blood" describes a colour and a trade category before it describes a plant. The material on this page is 血竭, the fruit resin of the rattan palm *Daemonorops draco*. The dragon-tree resins of *Dracaena* species carry the same English name and come from a different plant group entirely.

This is the first thing to settle, because everything else on the page — aroma, colour, blending behaviour, price expectation — follows from which resin you actually hold. The name travelled with the colour. Any red resin that looked like dried blood could be sold under it, and several were. The result is a label that has never been a botanical statement.

Two materials sit behind the label most often:

- **血竭 (xuèjié)** — the fruit resin of *Daemonorops draco* (syn. *Calamus draco*), a climbing palm. This is the classical Chinese material, the one recorded in the materia medica and used in lacquer and incense.
- **Dragon-tree resin** — the red resin of *Dracaena* species, including *Dracaena cinnabari* on Socotra and *Dracaena draco* in the Canary Islands. A tree, a different family, a different chemistry, and a different supply chain.

Both are red. Both are resins. Both are called dragon's blood in English. Neither fact makes them interchangeable.

| Material | Plant and group | Origin | Form in trade | Classical Chinese use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 血竭 | *Daemonorops draco*, a rattan palm (Arecaceae) | Sumatra, Borneo, Malay Peninsula | Fruit-surface resin, lumps or powder | Incense, pigment and dye, materia medica |
| Dragon-tree resin | *Dracaena cinnabari*, *Dracaena draco*, trees | Socotra, Canary Islands | Wound resin, lumps | Pigment and craft colour outside the Chinese incense canon |
| Red colourant sold as resin | No single botanical source | — | Powder, sometimes pressed lumps | None — a screening category, not a material |

The third row is not a plant. It is the possibility every buyer has to rule out, and it belongs in the table because a vivid red powder is the easiest thing in this trade to extend with a cheaper red colorant. Treat that as a risk to check rather than an accusation against any particular seller.

What follows from this is a simple purchasing rule: a line that says "dragon's blood" has not specified a material. It has specified an appearance. Until the line also says *Daemonorops draco*, the buyer has left the choice of species to whoever fills the order.

## What kind of plant produces it

> **Direct answer:** *Daemonorops draco* is a climbing rattan palm in the Arecaceae, the palm family. It is not a tree, and the resin is not tapped from a trunk — it gathers on the surface of the fruit.

Rattans are palms that climb. They produce slender, flexible stems that reach through the forest understorey rather than building a freestanding trunk, and the genus *Daemonorops* belongs to that group. The species is native to Sumatra, Borneo and the Malay Peninsula, where it grows in humid forest. The resin appears as a red exudate on the fruit, and that single botanical detail decides how the material is harvested, how it looks, and why it is not a wood.

Taxonomy has moved the plant. *Daemonorops draco* is often placed in *Calamus* as *Calamus draco*, so both names describe the same palm. A supplier may use either; the synonym is not a sign of a different product. The [GBIF record for *Daemonorops draco*](https://www.gbif.org/species/2735014) is the reference point used here.

One consequence deserves spelling out, because it is a recurring category error. Dragon's blood is not a heartwood, not a bark, and not a distillation. A page or a product that calls it "dragon's blood wood" is describing something the plant does not produce. The material is a fruit-surface secretion that has been scraped off and dried.

## How the resin is collected

> **Direct answer:** The fruit is harvested, the red exudate is scraped from its surface, and the scrapings are dried into lumps or ground into powder. There is no tapping step and no distillation step.

The sequence is short, and each stage leaves a mark on the material a buyer eventually receives.

1. **Fruiting.** The palm sets fruit, and the exudate coats the surface as it develops.
2. **Scraping.** Harvesters remove the red resin from the fruit surface.
3. **Gathering.** The scrapings are collected together, often with fragments of fruit scale and plant debris.
4. **Drying.** The mass hardens into brittle red lumps.
5. **Grinding.** Lumps become the vivid red powder sold for blending and colour work.

The mechanism chain runs like this: resin sits on the fruit surface → it is scraped rather than wounded out of wood → the scrapings carry whatever was on the outside of the fruit → drying locks those inclusions into the lump → the lump's fracture is therefore a record of how cleanly the harvest was done. That is why an experienced buyer looks at a broken face of resin rather than only at the outside colour.

Collection follows a fruiting cycle rather than a trunk-wounding cycle. That matters commercially. A resin tapped from a standing tree can be harvested on a schedule; a resin that comes off fruit is tied to the season and to the condition of that season's crop. Lot-to-lot variation is built into the material, and a specification that assumes pharmaceutical consistency across years is asking the supply chain for something it does not naturally deliver.

## What happens when it meets heat

> **Direct answer:** Dragon's blood behaves like a resin, not like a wood: it softens, then bubbles and smoulders, releasing a sweet balsamic smoke. The red colour does not go up in that smoke.

Heat the resin and the volatile fraction leaves first. Those volatiles are what the nose reads as resinous, sweet, woody and balsamic, with the faint smoky edge that comes from the burning itself. What stays behind is a charred residue carrying the pigment.

The chain is worth following closely, because it explains a question that comes up constantly: heat → the resin softens → volatile compounds vaporize → the smoke carries the aromatic note → the non-volatile red pigment remains in the residue → the smoke is not red and does not dye anything. A red cloud is theatre, not chemistry.

This is also why direct flame is the wrong way to judge the material. Flame chars the outside before the interior has warmed, and charred resin smells of char. A gentle heat source lets the resin melt and release its own note, which is the note a blend is buying. The difference between "sweet and balsamic" and "sharp and tarry" can be nothing more than the distance between the resin and the coal.

## Colour and scent are separate specifications

> **Direct answer:** The red comes from pigment; the smell comes from volatiles. They are different fractions of the same lump, and a lot can be strong in one and weak in the other.

Most materials in an incense cabinet are bought for one property. Dragon's blood is bought for two, and the trade has a long habit of collapsing them into one. A deep red lump looks like a good lump. That inference is not safe.

| Attribute | What it is for | How it is judged | What it cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour depth | Pigment value in lacquer, dye and craft work | Visual comparison of a fresh broken face against a known sample | Whether the species is *Daemonorops draco* |
| Scent at room temperature | The aromatic note the blend will carry | Warm a small piece gently and smell the vapour | How the material will behave on a hot coal |
| Fracture | Cleanliness of the resin and the harvest | Break a lump; look for a uniform, glassy red interior | Age, or whether the batch was blended |
| Residue after heating | How much non-volatile material is present | Burn a weighed pinch on a heat source and inspect the ash | The quality of the aromatic fraction |

An editorial judgement follows, and it is the one this page keeps returning to: colour is the easiest property in this trade to imitate and the hardest to verify from a photograph. Scent is slower to assess and much harder to fake at scale. A buyer who judges only by colour is judging the one variable a blender can control without touching the resin.

The reverse mistake exists too. A material chosen purely on aroma, with no thought to colour, will disappoint anyone using it in lacquer or dye work — the whole second half of its value. The two specifications have to be written down separately, because a single word, "quality", hides which one is being discussed.

## Names that describe a look

> **Direct answer:** A name built from an appearance invites substitution, because anything that shares the appearance can claim the name. "Dragon's blood" is a description of redness before it is the name of a plant.

Most materia-medica names fall into one of two habits. Some name the plant — 檀香 for sandalwood, 沉香 for agarwood — and the name travels with the species. Others name a quality the material displays, and those names drift. 血竭 sits in the second group: it says *dried blood*, and a dried-blood colour is something several resins can supply.

The drift is not a modern problem, and it is not a fraud story either. It is what happens when a naming system records appearance instead of botany. Traders in different ports, handling different plants, could all use the same phrase honestly and mean different sacks. The confusion enters the record long before it enters a purchase order.

The consequence for a reader today is practical. When a name describes a look, the burden of identity shifts onto the specification. A buyer who writes "dragon's blood" has repeated the appearance; a buyer who writes the binomial has named the plant. The first is a request for a colour. The second is a request for a material.

The same pattern shows up across the fragrance trade, and it is worth recognising as a pattern rather than memorising individual cases. Names built on appearance — colour words, shape words, "blood", "gold", "dragon" — tend to gather unrelated materials under one label. Names built on a plant tend not to. When a label resists being resolved to a species, that resistance is information.

## Red is not one material

> **Direct answer:** Several red materials pass through incense and craft work, and they are not interchangeable. A resin, a wood, a mineral and a formulated dye carry colour by different mechanisms and behave differently under heat — one of them should never be burned at all.

Once colour becomes a specification, the next question is which red material is doing the work. In incense and lacquer alike, red has several routes into a product, and only one of them is this resin.

| Material | What it is | Where the colour sits | Behaviour with heat | Place in incense |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dragon's blood | Palm fruit resin, *Daemonorops draco* | Dracorubin-type pigments in the resin | Softens, bubbles and smoulders; aromatic smoke; pigment stays in the residue | Resin note and colour together |
| Red sandalwood | Heartwood of *Pterocarpus santalinus* | Santalin-type extractives in the wood | Smoulders as a wood; dry, woody smoke | Wood note and colour together |
| Cinnabar | A mineral, mercury sulfide | Mercuric sulfide | Must not be burned; heating drives off mercury vapour | Not an incense material |
| Formulated red dye | A colourant, not a botanical | Dye chemistry | Varies; may char, may volatilise, may leave a stain | Colour with no aromatic contribution |

Read the table by column rather than by row. The colour column shows that all four can look red. The heat column shows that they have almost nothing else in common. A resin that melts, a wood that smoulders, a mineral that must never be heated and a dye that may do any of several things are not variations on one theme; they are four different technologies of redness.

For a blender, the substitution logic follows from that. If a formula needs colour and a balsamic note, dragon's blood does both in one material. If it needs colour and a dry woody note, red sandalwood does both, and swapping one for the other changes the scent as much as the colour. If it needs colour alone, a dye will do it and contribute nothing to the aroma — which may be exactly what is wanted, or exactly the problem, depending on the brief.

Cinnabar deserves its own line because it is the one entry that is a hazard rather than an alternative. Mercury sulfide is not a craft colourant to be heated on a coal, and no traditional appearance of red makes it safe to burn indoors. That is a safety boundary, not a matter of taste.

The misconception worth naming here is simple, and it is editorial: a red incense stick does not prove that a red resin was used. It proves that someone chose red. The colour could come from this resin, from a wood, from a dye, or from a mixture of them. Colour is a design decision, not a certificate of contents.

## Why a photograph cannot grade this resin

> **Direct answer:** Colour photographs flatten exactly the variables a buyer needs — the interior of a lump, the evenness of the pigment, the presence of debris — and they flatter exactly the variable a seller can control.

A screen shows one surface under one light. The judgements that matter here happen on a broken face, under a consistent light, against a known sample. None of those conditions survives a photograph.

Three things are lost in particular. The first is depth: a lump photographed from outside may be uniform red on the surface and pale at the centre, and the camera records only the surface. The second is scale: pigment evenness is judged across a batch, and a close-up of the best piece says nothing about the sack. The third is the difference between colour and sheen — a resin can look vivid because it is rich in pigment, or because it is polished, damp or lit to look that way.

What a photograph is good for is identification at the level of "this is a red resin, roughly this form". That is a useful first filter and a poor final one. The rest of the assessment needs a sample in hand, a knife, a gentle heat source and a reference piece from a lot that is already trusted.

## Lumps, powder, and the cost of surface area

> **Direct answer:** Lumps keep their volatiles longer and show their structure when broken; powder blends evenly and colours strongly but loses aroma faster. The two forms are the same resin at different surface areas.

Grinding is not a neutral act. It multiplies the surface exposed to air, and the aromatic fraction is the part that leaves first. That gives the second mechanism chain on this page: grinding → surface area rises sharply → oxidation and evaporation proceed faster → the powder's scent declines sooner than the lump's → a powder bought in bulk and stored for a year is not the material that was tested on delivery.

| Form | Storage behaviour | Best suited to | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lumps | Slower volatile loss; interior visible on fracture | Blending by weight, pigment work, inspection | Uneven interior; debris hidden inside a good-looking lump |
| Powder | Fast blending and strong colour; aroma declines sooner | Incense mixtures, dye and pigment | Adulteration is harder to see; dust; caking in humidity |

Powder is where an inferior lot hides most comfortably. A vivid red powder presents no fracture to read, no interior to inspect and no structure to compare. Everything the buyer can see is the surface, and the surface is exactly what a blender controls. Lumps are not proof of honesty either, but they at least offer a broken face to argue with.

The practical response is not to avoid powder. It is to buy powder in the quantity that will be used within its aromatic life, and to keep a lump sample from the same lot as a reference. When the powder and the reference stop agreeing, the powder has told you something.

## Reading quality without a published ladder

> **Direct answer:** Dragon's blood has no standardized commercial grading ladder, so quality is read from species, colour, fracture, scent and residue. Those five signals answer different questions and cannot substitute for one another.

This is the section where a buyer has to build a framework rather than look one up. The absence of a ladder is not a gap in this page; it is a property of the trade.

**Species first.** Everything else is secondary, because a perfectly clean dragon-tree resin is still not 血竭. Ask for the binomial, not the trade name. If the answer is "dragon's blood", the question has not been answered.

**Colour second, on a fresh face.** Break a lump and look inside. Deep, even red throughout is the signal; a pale core, a brownish grain or a visibly different centre suggests either a mixed batch or a lump built around debris.

**Fracture third.** Good resin breaks cleanly and glassily. A crumbly, earthy break suggests a high proportion of non-resin material.

**Scent fourth, warmed not burned.** A gentle heat releases the sweet balsamic note. Flatness is a defect. Sharpness may be the heat source rather than the resin, which is why the test has to be done gently and consistently.

**Residue fifth.** A small pinch on a heat source leaves ash. How much, and how it looks, is a rough guide to how much non-volatile material the lot carries. This is a comparative test — it means something against a known sample, not in the abstract.

| Signal | Read it as | Do not read it as |
|---|---|---|
| Deep, even red | Strong pigment load and a clean-looking resin | Proof of species |
| Clean glassy fracture | Resin-rich lump, little included debris | Proof of freshness |
| Sweet balsamic vapour when warmed | Intact aromatic fraction | A prediction of how it burns on a coal |
| Low residue | Little non-volatile filler | A guarantee against colour adulteration |
| Binomial on the paperwork | Identity is specified | A guarantee that the paperwork matches the sack |

That last row is the uncomfortable one. Documentation specifies intent; it does not verify the contents. A species name on an invoice is necessary and not sufficient, which is why the physical checks still matter after the paperwork is in order.

## A decision table for buyers

> **Direct answer:** The specification follows from the use. An incense buyer and a pigment buyer are purchasing different fractions of the same resin and should write different requirements.

| If the use is | Specify | The failure being guarded against |
|---|---|---|
| Incense blending | Species, form, scent when gently warmed | A red, scentless resin that adds colour and no note |
| Lacquer, dye or craft colour | Species, colour depth on a fresh face, fineness, visible debris | A bright powder stretched with a cheaper red colorant |
| Resale under a Chinese name | 血竭 with the binomial on the label and on the lot | A dragon-tree resin shipped against a 血竭 order |
| Blending into a repeat formula | A retained sample from each lot, plus form and sieve | A new lot that behaves differently in a formula that has not changed |

The fourth row is the one that saves the most money over time and is the one most often skipped. A retained sample costs almost nothing. Without it, a reformulation problem six months later cannot be traced to the material, and the blame lands on the formula.

## Incense grade and pigment grade are different briefs

> **Direct answer:** The same resin gets graded twice — once for what it does in smoke, once for what it does on a surface. A lot that suits one brief can be mediocre for the other, and neither grade is the "real" one.

A blender buying for scent and a lacquer maker buying for colour are not competing for the same quality tier. They are asking different questions of the same sack, and a supplier who does not know which question is being asked will answer with a word like "premium" that settles nothing.

For incense work, the first criterion is what the resin does when warmed: sweet, balsamic, clean, and free of the sharp note that comes from charring. Colour is a secondary consideration, useful mainly because it indicates the resin has not been stretched with pale filler. A slightly duller lump with a clean scent is the better buy for this brief.

For colour work, the order reverses. Depth and evenness of red come first, fineness and freedom from visible debris second, and the scent barely matters — a material with a weak nose and a strong colour is entirely fit for purpose. That is not a lower grade. It is a different grade.

| Brief | First criterion | Second criterion | Acceptable compromise | Unacceptable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Incense blending | Scent when gently warmed | Clean residue | Paler colour | Flat, tarry or charred note |
| Lacquer, dye and pigment | Colour depth on a fresh face | Evenness and fineness | Faint scent | Brown, uneven or gritty colour |
| Resale under a Chinese name | Species on the paperwork | Consistent lots | Either colour or scent, if declared | A name-only label with no binomial |

The third row is where the two briefs meet, and where most disputes start. A reseller needs identity and consistency rather than excellence in either direction, because the customer may want either. A lot that is superb for pigment and poor for incense will disappoint half the customer base unless the listing says which it is.

The practical rule: write the brief before you write the order. "Good dragon's blood" is not a brief. "血竭, *Daemonorops draco*, strong balsamic scent when warmed, colour secondary" is a brief, and it can be checked on delivery by anyone with a knife and a heat source.

## 血竭 in the Chinese record

> **Direct answer:** 血竭 is recorded in the 本草綱目 (Compendium of Materia Medica, 木部) as a red resin with pigment, craft and materia-medica uses. In the incense and lacquer trades it was a colouring resin as much as a fragrant one.

The name itself is a description: 血竭 reads as "blood-exhausted" or "dried blood", a reference to the colour rather than to any ingredient. Chinese material names often work this way — the name records what the substance looked like to the people who traded it.

Three uses are documented in the tradition:

- **Incense.** A warm balsamic resin note, blended with other resins rather than burned alone.
- **Pigment and dye.** A red colourant for lacquer and craft work, where the colour was the point.
- **Materia medica.** An entry in the classical pharmacopoeia, described in the language of that tradition.

The third use needs a clear boundary. Statements in the 本草綱目 are historical documentation of what was believed and recorded, not clinical evidence by modern standards. This page treats them as traditional belief and makes no medical claim in either direction. That is not a dismissal of the tradition; it is a statement about what kind of document the source is.

The pigment use is the one most often forgotten in incense writing, and it explains a lot about the material's economics. A resin valued for colour can be sold into markets that never burn it. Demand from lacquer and craft work has always sat alongside demand from the incense burner, and the two markets grade the same lump by different criteria.

## Tradition, evidence, and where the line sits

> **Direct answer:** The botanical identity and the pigment chemistry are established; the incense and pigment uses are historically documented; the medicinal uses are traditional belief, not established clinical fact.

## How it behaves in a blend

> **Direct answer:** Dragon's blood is a resin note, usually a supporting one, adding balsamic warmth and a red tint to a mixture. It is rarely the top note and rarely burned alone.

Resins behave differently from woods in a blend. A wood smoulders and holds structure; a resin softens, flows and releases its volatiles in a burst before settling. Adding dragon's blood to a mixture therefore changes two things at once: the scent profile gains a sweet balsamic layer, and the burning behaviour shifts toward the resinous.

That second effect is easy to miss when a formula is judged only by smell in the jar. A blend that has been balanced by scent alone can behave differently once the resin proportion rises, because the physical character of the mixture has changed as well as its aroma. The correction is to test the blend as it burns, not only as it smells cold.

For colour, the effect is straightforward: a red resin tints a mixture, and the tint is visible in the finished stick or cone. That is a legitimate use and a traditional one. It is also a reason to be careful with pale binders, where a small change in resin proportion shows up visually before it shows up in the scent.

Two tests settle most of the blending questions, and neither needs equipment. The first is a burn test of the candidate blend alongside the current formula, side by side, in the same room and the same draught. The second is a cold-smell comparison of the two mixtures in identical jars, left closed for the same period. The first tells you what the resin does to the smoke; the second tells you what it does to the shelf. A resin can pass one and fail the other, and that is the argument for running both rather than trusting a single impression.

Colour adds a third variable that is easy to overlook in a formula review. A red resin shifts the appearance of the finished stick, and if the formula is a house style, that shift is a change to the product even when the scent is identical. Blenders who work with colour-bearing materials usually keep a physical reference of the finished piece, not only a written recipe, because the recipe records proportions and the reference records the result.

## Storage and handling

> **Direct answer:** Keep lumps and powder cool, dry and out of direct light, in a sealed container, and buy powder in quantities that will be used while the aroma is still intact.

Resin is more forgiving than a distilled oil and less forgiving than a wood chip. It does not evaporate in a week, but it does lose its aromatic edge, and powder loses it faster than lumps. The mechanism is the same one described above: surface area drives the rate.

Moisture is the other problem. Lumps that take up humidity soften, clump and become difficult to grind evenly, and a caked powder is a sign that the container has been breathing. A sealed jar in a dark cupboard is sufficient. Refrigeration is unnecessary and can introduce condensation each time the jar is opened.

Handling pigment-grade powder deserves a little care for a different reason: fine red dust travels. Work over a tray, keep the container closed between portions, and accept that a small amount of staining is normal in pigment work. That is a housekeeping note, not a health warning.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** Burning any incense produces smoke and particulate matter, and dragon's blood is no exception. Burn it in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep burning material away from children and pets.

The resin itself is not a food and should not be ingested on the strength of a traditional medicinal reputation. Pigment-grade powder should be kept out of the eyes and away from food preparation surfaces. None of this is unusual for an incense material; all of it is worth stating plainly. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general handling framework.

## FAQ

> **Direct answer:** These are the questions that come up first when a buyer or a burner meets the material; each answer is short and points back to the longer treatment above.

**What exactly is dragon's blood resin?** A dried resin that forms on the fruit of the rattan palm *Daemonorops draco*, native to Southeast Asia and classed in the Arecaceae. It serves two purposes at once: a sweet, woody, balsamic incense material and a red pigment. The Chinese name is 血竭.

**Do all resins sold under this name match?** No. At least two unrelated materials answer to it — the rattan-palm fruit resin 血竭 and the red resin of *Dracaena* trees such as *Dracaena cinnabari*. A shared colour and a shared English phrase are all they have in common.

**Does it produce red smoke?** No. The red sits in non-volatile pigments that remain in the residue after burning. What leaves in the smoke is the aromatic fraction, and that fraction carries no colour. A red plume means something other than this resin is alight.

**Is it a wood or a resin?** A resin. It gathers on the outside of a palm fruit, is scraped away and dried. The plant is a climbing palm rather than a timber tree, so the phrase "dragon's blood wood" names a product the plant never makes.

**How do I separate 血竭 from dragon-tree resin?** Through the botanical name rather than the look. Require the binomial on the lot, break a lump and compare the fresh face with a retained sample, and treat colour as the weakest test available. Both materials are red, and red proves nothing.

**Can it be burned indoors?** With ventilation. Every incense material produces smoke and particulate matter, so burn in a ventilated room, avoid concentrated smoke, and keep the source away from children and pets.

## Summary

Start with the binomial and most of the difficulty disappears. 血竭 is fruit resin from *Daemonorops draco*, a climbing palm of Southeast Asian forest, and it holds two kinds of value in one brittle lump — the balsamic note a blend wants and the deep red a lacquer wants. Treat those as two specifications, because they fail independently. The English name is shared with tree resins from *Dracaena* that have nothing to do with the palm, so identity comes before appearance. With no published grading ladder, the judgement rests on five separate readings: the species named, the colour on a fresh face, the way it breaks, the scent released by gentle warmth, and what remains after heating.

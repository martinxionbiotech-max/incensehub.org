> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf (桑叶, sāngyè) is the dried leaf of *Morus alba* — the white mulberry, a Moraceae tree whose foliage is the food of the domesticated silkworm. The scent is soft, green and tea-like with a little sweetness, and in Chinese practice the leaf belongs to tea and materia medica far more than to the incense burner.

Read that table as three different kinds of statement. The species, habit and leaf measurements are botany and can be checked against a flora. The origin and the historical role are records of cultivation and text. The last two rows are judgments about how the material behaves in trade and in a blend, and they are editorial — useful, arguable, and not the same thing as a measurement. Keeping those layers apart is the whole discipline of reading a material page, and it matters more than usual here, because mulberry carries several thousand years of cultural weight that has nothing to do with how the leaf smells.

## The plant behind the leaf

> **Direct answer:** *Morus alba* is a deciduous tree or large shrub of the fig family, reaching 3–10 m, with leaves 5–30 cm long that are ovate and irregularly lobed. Its fruit is a fused syncarp that colours from red to deep purple as it ripens, and Flora of China places the species as native to central and northern China, cultivated widely since.

The white mulberry is grown in rows and hedges as much as it is left to become a tree; pruning keeps the crown low so that leaves can be picked by hand. Left alone it forms a small tree with a rounded crown. The leaves are the variable part. Flora of China describes them as ovate to broadly ovate, 5–30 cm long and 5–12 cm wide, with irregular lobing — and irregular is the operative word, because a single plant can carry entire leaves on one shoot and deeply lobed leaves on another, especially on vigorous young growth. The margins are toothed, the surface is thin and soft, and the underside is paler. Anyone who has only seen a botanical drawing of a neat, five-lobed leaf has seen one possible shape rather than the rule.

Flowering is unremarkable to look at: small greenish flowers in catkins, wind-pollinated, easy to miss under the foliage. The fruit is the part most people recognise. It is a syncarp — a cluster of many small fruits fused into one fleshy mass — 1–2.5 cm long, red and sour when unripe, turning dark purple, purple, or in some forms greenish-white when mature. Two words split the tree: 桑葚 (sāngshèn) for the fruit, 桑叶 for the leaf. Both come from the same plant, and neither substitutes for the other — a point that returns below.

What made the tree economically central is not its fruit. It is the leaf's role as the only food of the domesticated silkworm, *Bombyx mori*. Sericulture turned mulberry into a crop with its own land, labour and varieties, and the tree's other products followed: Flora of China notes that the leaves feed silkworms, the bark fibre is used for textiles and paper, and the bark itself is used in medicine. A single species supplying food, fibre, paper and medicine explains why the mulberry sits so deeply in Chinese material culture — and why the leaf arrives at a buyer already carrying meanings that a neutral aromatic never has to carry.

The cultural shorthand 农桑 (nóngsāng), "farming and sericulture", pairs mulberry with grain as the two poles of the traditional agrarian economy — a compact piece of evidence for how central the tree once was. It also explains the tone of older writing about the leaf: a plant that fed an industry and appeared in the materia medica acquires a weight that a buyer handling a sack of dried leaf may not expect. That weight is real as history and irrelevant as aroma. Judging the material means setting the history aside long enough to smell the sample.

Other mulberries occur in China, and the binomial normally attached to the commercial leaf is *M. alba*. A buyer who needs species certainty should ask for the botanical name on the specification rather than rely on the Chinese common name, because common names travel and binomials do not. See [*Morus alba* in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200006379) for the full description.

## One tree, three materials

> **Direct answer:** 桑叶, 桑葚 and 桑白皮 are three distinct materials from one tree: the leaf, the fruit and the root bark. The part name is the specification, and a purchase order that says only "mulberry" names a tree rather than a material.

| Part | Chinese | What it is | Where it is used | Confusion it creates |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leaf | 桑叶 (sāngyè) | Dried leaf blade | Tea, sachets, light blends | Treated as interchangeable with "mulberry" |
| Fruit | 桑葚 (sāngshèn) | The compound fruit, fresh or dried | Food, preserves, traditional use | Ordered when the leaf was meant, or the reverse |
| Root bark | 桑白皮 (sāngbáipí) | Bark from the root | Materia medica | Assumed to be "bark of the same leaf material" |

The three are not grades of one another; they are different organs with different chemistry, different handling and different price structures. In cut or powdered form the visual difference narrows sharply, which is where part-level naming earns its keep. The point is not that substitution is routine — it is that a specification should make substitution impossible to argue about later. Write the part, the binomial, the form and the crop year, and the ambiguity disappears.

There is a second, subtler confusion: 桑叶 is sometimes discussed as if it were the tree's "main" product because tea made from it is familiar. Commercially, the leaf's biggest use has long been silkworm fodder, and fodder is bought on criteria that have nothing to do with fragrance. That gap between fodder grade and aromatic grade is one of the practical traps of this material, and no published ladder resolves it for a buyer.

## What the leaf smells like

> **Direct answer:** Mild, clean, green and tea-like, with a faint sweetness that reads closer to green tea than to any resin or wood. The dried leaf is quieter than the infusion, and both are far quieter than the smoke the leaf produces when it burns.

Smell a handful of dried leaf and the first impression is dusty-green, like a hayloft or a tea chest opened after a season. Crush a leaf between the fingers and the note jumps: greener, slightly sappy, a little sweet, with a hay edge that sits between cut grass and green tea. Steep it and the liquor is softer again, sweeter, with a faintly nutty tail. Three presentations, one material, three different aromatic results — and the difference is not poetry, it is volatility.

The green note of a crushed leaf is largely built from six-carbon aldehydes and alcohols that appear when leaf cells are ruptured and their contents mix. That is a general feature of green plant tissue, not a claim specific to mulberry. The practical consequence is what matters for anyone handling the material:

- **Crushing, cutting or rolling a fresh leaf → cell contents mix → green volatiles form quickly → the note spikes and then declines as those compounds oxidise.**
- **Drying slowly in cool, humid air → enzymes stay active for longer → hay, fermentation and cooked-fruit notes develop → the fresh tea-fragrance character is muted.**
- **Drying fast with gentle heat → enzyme action is cut short → a greener, brighter leaf survives → but the margin before scorching is narrow.**

That trade-off is the same one leaf-tea makers manage everywhere, and it explains why two samples of "dried mulberry leaf" can smell like different materials. Neither is adulterated. They were dried on different curves.

There is a further distinction worth making, because it is where most expectations go wrong. The tea-fragrance character is a **cold** character: it lives in the dry leaf, in the infusion, in a sachet at room temperature. Fire does not intensify it. Burning leaf tissue produces smoke, and smoke is a blunt, acrid, carbon-heavy smell that swamps delicate green volatiles rather than carrying them. A leaf that smells wonderful in a cup can smell like burning garden waste on a coal. That is not a defect in the leaf; it is a mismatch between the material's register and the delivery method.

## The tea-fragrance register, mapped

> **Direct answer:** 茶香 (tea-fragrance) is a real aromatic register — soft, green, leaf-like, faintly sweet — and it behaves differently from woods, resins and florals. It sits at the top of a blend, fades first, and needs a heavier material underneath to hold it.

| Register | Core character | Materials that typically sit here | Behaviour in a blend |
|---|---|---|---|
| 茶香 tea-fragrance | Green, leafy, soft, faintly sweet | Mulberry leaf, tea leaf, bamboo leaf | Top-note; lifts and fades; needs a base |
| 木香 woods | Dry, warm, structural, sometimes creamy | Sandalwood-type and other woody 香材 | Base and middle; slow, steady |
| 树脂 resins | Heavy, balsamic, sweet-resinous | Gum and balsam resins | Base; fixes lighter notes |
| 花香 florals | Sweet, lifted, petal-like | Sophora flower and other floral 香材 | Middle; can dominate if overdosed |
| 辛香 aromatics | Sharp, warm, diffusive | Spice seeds and barks | Middle to top; high impact at low dose |

The table is a working map, not a taxonomy of everything in the canon. Its purpose is to stop a common mistake: treating "green" as a material identity. Green is a colour word that covers at least three different things — the grassy green of a fresh leaf, the resinous green of certain gums, and the sharp green of a synthetic aldehyde. A brief that asks for "something green" has not specified a material, and a supplier who fills it with the cheapest green available has not necessarily misunderstood the brief.

Mulberry leaf occupies the softest end of the register. It has no projection to speak of, no resinous body, and no fixative quality. What it has is a clean, recognisable leaf note that reads as tea rather than as perfume. That is a narrow but genuine value, and it is why the material keeps appearing in sachets and in tea-fragrance compositions even though it is absent from the classical incense canon as a lead ingredient.

The register also explains the fixative problem. Light volatiles have high vapour pressure; they leave an open sachet faster than a resin leaves a sealed jar. **An open sachet of leaf → volatiles move into the headspace → the top note thins long before the base changes → what remains is the flat, hay-like residue.** A blend that depends on mulberry leaf for its character will change character as it sits. A blend that uses the leaf as a lift over a stable base will simply become a little quieter. That difference is a design decision, not a quality failure.

## Where a mild leaf sits in a formula

> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf is an assistant at best, never a chief. In the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) logic of Chinese formula thinking, a material with no projection and no fixative power can support a composition but cannot govern it.

The four-role frame is a useful discipline even for someone who does not work inside the classical tradition. 君 (chief) sets the character; 臣 (minister) supports and extends it; 佐 (assistant) modifies, softens or contrasts; 使 (guide) carries the blend and harmonises. Apply it honestly and most quiet leaves land in the third slot. Mulberry leaf can round off a sharp resin, add a clean top note to a sweet base, or give a sachet a tea-house impression. It cannot be the reason the blend exists, because nothing in its chemistry projects.

Two failure modes follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is over-dosing. A formulator smells the dry leaf, finds it pleasant, and assumes more will smell stronger. It does not. Past a certain proportion the leaf contributes a dusty, hay-like flatness and mutes the materials around it. The correct dose is the point where the top note is just perceptible, and that point is found by trial, not by a fixed percentage.

The second is processing loss. **Ground leaf folded into a damp incense paste → water and heat during drying → light green volatiles driven off → the finished stick smells of binder and base wood, with the leaf barely present.** Whole or coarsely cut leaf in a dry sachet keeps far more of its character, because it is never wetted and never heated. The form and the delivery method decide the outcome before the formula does.

There is an editorial judgment here that is worth stating plainly: much of the disappointment people report with "leaf" materials comes from asking a cold-register material to perform in a hot-register application. The leaf is not weak. It is being used against its grain. Change the delivery — sachet instead of stick, cool instead of lit — and the same leaf performs.

## What this leaf cannot do

> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf cannot fix a blend, cannot survive a wet paste, cannot stand in for tea leaf, and cannot supply the sweetness its own descriptor hints at. Knowing the limits is more useful than another paragraph about the aroma.

Start with the word "sweet". The sweetness here is a faint, tea-like impression in the top note, not a sugary body. A formulator who reads "slightly sweet" and expects the leaf to round off a harsh blend will be disappointed, because the effect is measured in nuance rather than in weight. Sweetness of the kind that fixes a composition comes from resins, balsams and heavy woods; the leaf offers a suggestion of it and nothing more.

Then the fixative question. A fixative slows the evaporation of lighter materials and holds a composition together over time. Mulberry leaf does the opposite: it is itself one of the lighter materials, and it leaves an open sachet sooner than almost anything else in the formula. Using it as a base is a category error. Using it as a top note over a base that already works is the correct move, and the difference shows up months later when the blend either holds or flattens.

Finally, substitution. Tea leaf and mulberry leaf share a register but not an identity: tea leaf carries its own tannin-and-aroma profile, and mulberry leaf is softer and more hay-like. A blend built around tea leaf will not be reproduced by swapping in mulberry leaf, even though both are green leaves with a tea-ish smell. The register is shared; the material is not.

## From branch to dried leaf

> **Direct answer:** The leaf is picked, dried, sorted, and then cut or milled. There is no distillation and no extraction: the product is whole leaf, and the drying curve is the only real processing decision.

The sequence is short, which is why the variables inside it matter so much:

1. **Picking.** Leaf is taken from pruned hedges or low crowns, usually several picks across the growing season. Young, thin leaf dries faster and carries a greener note; older leaf is coarser, more fibrous and flatter in scent.
2. **Withering.** Picked leaf is spread thin so that surface moisture leaves and the tissue becomes pliable. Pile it deep and it heats, sweats and starts to ferment.
3. **Stabilising (optional).** Leaf teas are often given a heat step to stop oxidation before the green character is lost. Whether a given mulberry-leaf product receives one is a producer decision, and it is a fair question to ask.
4. **Drying.** The decisive step. Gentle, fast drying preserves a green note; slow, cool drying pushes the leaf toward hay and cooked fruit; too much heat scorches the edges and leaves a toasted, bitter smell.
5. **Sorting.** Stems, grit and discoloured leaf are removed. Stem content is not dangerous, but it dilutes the material and adds a woody, flat note.
6. **Cutting or milling.** Whole leaf for sachets and tea, cut leaf for blending, powder for compounds that need the leaf distributed evenly.
7. **Storage.** Dried leaf is hygroscopic. **Damp leaf → mould → a musty, cellar-like note → the batch is finished, whatever its original quality.**

The drying step is where most of the aromatic outcome is decided, and it is also the step a buyer can least easily inspect. A supplier can show leaf colour and leaf size; the drying curve is invisible in the finished product except as a smell. That is why tasting or smelling a sample matters more than reading a specification sheet for this material — the sheet can describe the leaf, but only the nose can describe the dry.

## A working grading frame for dried leaf

> **Direct answer:** No published grading ladder exists for 桑叶, so the practical frame is a set of observable signals — leaf integrity, colour, stem load, aroma and moisture — each of which predicts how the material will behave rather than merely how it looks.

| Signal | What good looks like | What it predicts | Failure mode it catches |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaf integrity | Mostly whole or large-cut leaf | Slower aroma loss; cleaner sachet | Dust and fines that release fast and pack down |
| Colour | Even green to olive-green | Gentle drying; limited oxidation | Brown or black patches from scorch or fermentation |
| Stem load | Low; stems easy to see and remove | Higher aromatic leaf per gram | Woody, flat dilution |
| Aroma, crushed | Fresh green-tea note, faint sweetness | The register is intact | Musty, cellar, or dead-flat smell |
| Moisture | Brittle, snaps when bent | Stable in storage | Leathery, pliable leaf that will mould |
| Foreign matter | Clean; no grit or sand | Basic handling discipline | Contamination and off-notes |

This frame is editorial. It is not an industry standard, and it does not produce a grade name that two suppliers will agree on. What it does is convert a vague "good leaf" into five things a buyer can look at, smell and argue about. The order matters: aroma and moisture outrank appearance, because a beautiful leaf that has gone musty is worthless, while a slightly broken leaf that smells right will still do its job in a sachet.

One boundary worth drawing: leaf size is not a quality ladder in itself. Large whole leaf is not automatically better than cut leaf, because the right form depends on the application. Whole leaf suits sachets and tea; cut leaf suits even distribution in a blend; powder suits a compound that needs the leaf spread through a mass. Buying whole leaf for a powder application and milling it yourself adds a step without adding quality.

## Storage, and the arithmetic of decline

> **Direct answer:** Dried leaf declines from the moment it is dry, and the two accelerators are moisture and air. Whole leaf in a sealed, cool, dark container holds its note longest; powder in a warm cupboard holds it least.

Leaf is a low-density material with a lot of internal surface, which makes it a good absorber of whatever is around it. Three habits follow from that, and each one is cheap to adopt.

**Keep it dry.** Dried leaf that bends instead of snapping has already taken up water. In a warm room that moisture supports mould, and the musty note that results cannot be washed out or aired away. A sealed container with a desiccant packet is the simple answer; a paper bag in a kitchen cupboard is the wrong one. **Damp leaf → mould growth → musty, cellar-like off-note → the batch is compromised regardless of how good the leaf was on arrival.**

**Keep it dark and cool.** Light and heat both push the volatile fraction along. The green note that makes the material interesting is the part most sensitive to both, so a leaf stored in a sunny jar will keep its colour longer than its smell.

**Keep it away from strong odours.** Coffee, spices, soap and resin all migrate into dry leaf. A sachet material stored beside a bag of cloves will smell of cloves within weeks, and the tea-fragrance character is subtle enough to be overwritten completely.

Shelf life is a function of form as much as of storage. Whole leaf, sealed and cool, is the slowest to change. Cut leaf is next. Powder is the fastest, because every milled particle is an exposed surface. Buy the form that matches the rate at which you will use it, and mill late if you mill at all.

## Forms and what each one is for

> **Direct answer:** Whole dried leaf, cut leaf and ground powder are the three trade forms, with fresh leaf as a seasonal curiosity. Each has a different aroma-retention profile, and the finest form is not the best one.

| Form | Best use | Aroma retention | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried leaf | Tea, display sachets, long storage | Best; protected inside the leaf | Bulky; slow to blend evenly |
| Cut leaf | Sachets, loose blends | Good | More surface exposed; fades sooner than whole |
| Ground powder | Compounds, even distribution | Weakest; degrades fastest | Picks up moisture and off-notes |
| Fresh leaf | Seasonal tea, experimental work | Very short | Moulds within days; not a trade form |

The logic is simple surface-area physics. **Grinding leaf → many more exposed cells → faster exchange with the air → the top note leaves sooner and the powder begins to smell of the room it is stored in.** Whole leaf keeps its own atmosphere for longer. That is why a tea-fragrance material is usually bought whole and milled late, if it is milled at all.

## What it is not: a leafy confusion table

> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf, bamboo leaf, arborvitae leaf and sophora flower are all sold as "leaf" or "green" materials, but they come from different plants in different families, with different parts and different registers. Colour is not identity.

| Material | Plant and family | Part | Register | The confusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mulberry leaf (桑叶) | *Morus alba*, Moraceae | Leaf | Tea-fragrance, soft green | Treated as the default "green leaf" |
| Bamboo leaf (竹叶) | Bamboo grasses, Poaceae | Leaf | Fresh green, slightly grassy | Assumed interchangeable with mulberry leaf |
| Arborvitae leaf (侧柏叶) | *Platycladus orientalis*, Cupressaceae | Leafy spray | Green, woody, resinous | Bought as "cedar leaf" by mistake |
| Sophora flower (槐花) | *Sophora japonica*, Fabaceae | Flower bud | Sweet floral | Grouped with leaves because it is pale and light |

Three of these are leaves and the fourth is a flower, which is enough to show that "leafy green" is a sensory description rather than a botanical category. The practical consequence: a substitution made on colour will change the register of the blend even when the visual match is close. Arborvitae brings a woody-resinous edge that mulberry leaf does not have; bamboo brings a sharper grass note; sophora brings sweetness and lift. None is a drop-in replacement.

## The record and its limits

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records the tree 桑 in its tree section, 木之三, with 桑叶 among its parts. That is a historical record of tea and materia-medica use; it is not clinical evidence, and this page makes no health claim.

Three layers need separating, because they are constantly mixed in popular writing about this leaf.

- **Botanical identity — established.** *Morus alba* L. in Moraceae, described and delimited in floras, with a stable binomial and a published morphological description.
- **Historical record — documented.** Cultivation for sericulture over a long period, a tea tradition, and an entry in the *Bencao Gangmu* under 桑. These are facts about what people did and wrote.
- **Health claims — not established here.** Traditional materia-medica language describes uses and properties in its own framework. It is not a modern clinical finding, and repeating it as one would be a category error.

The middle layer is where most of the interesting material sits. A classical entry tells you that a plant was noticed, classified and used; it does not tell you that a modern trial would confirm the use. The distinction is not pedantry. It changes what a reader should do with the information: enjoy the history, use the leaf as a fragrance material if it suits, and do not convert an old sentence into a therapeutic promise.

| Layer | Status | What it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established | Species-level sourcing and specification |
| Traditional use | Historically documented | Cultural and historical context |
| Aroma character | Editorial observation | Blend design, not proof of anything else |
| Health claims | Not established | Nothing on this page |

## Sourcing checks

> **Direct answer:** Confirm the part, the binomial, the form and the crop, then judge the sample by smell and moisture. For a leaf with no grading ladder, the sample is the specification.

1. **Name the part.** 桑叶, not "mulberry". Say leaf explicitly, and put the Chinese character on the purchase document.
2. **Ask for the binomial.** *Morus alba* is the expected answer. A supplier who cannot give a binomial is selling a common name.
3. **Ask which form.** Whole, cut or powdered. The form changes the aroma-retention profile and the price logic.
4. **Ask the crop year.** Leaf is seasonal. Old crop smells flat, and no amount of careful storage restores a note that has already gone.
5. **Smell a crushed sample.** Green and tea-like is the target. Musty, cellar-like or dead-flat is a rejection, whatever the leaf looks like.
6. **Check moisture by hand.** Brittle leaf that snaps is stable; leathery leaf that bends is a mould risk in transit.
7. **Look for stems and grit.** Both dilute the material and both are easy to see in a cut sample.
8. **Ask about the drying method.** Shade-dried, sun-dried and heat-stabilised leaf are different products with different registers.

Supplier vocabulary is worth decoding, because the words are not standardized and they carry different weights for different materials:

| Term in a listing | What it can mean for leaf | What it does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| "Wild" | Grown without deliberate cultivation | Species, drying method, or age |
| "Hand-picked" | Selective harvest, less leaf damage | Whether the leaf was dried well |
| "Sun-dried" | A drying curve, usually slower and warmer | Whether the leaf scorched at the edge |
| "Shade-dried" | A cooler, slower curve, often greener | Moisture at packing |
| "New crop" | Recent harvest | Storage since harvest |
| "Food grade" | A food-safety framing | Aroma quality or leaf integrity |

None of these terms is dishonest; they are simply not specifications. Each one describes a practice, and a practice can be done well or badly. The move for a buyer is to accept the term as context and then ask the question behind it — which species, which drying curve, which crop year, which moisture at packing.

That list is deliberately about questions rather than thresholds. There is no number to hit, because there is no published ladder. What a buyer can do is make the supplier commit to statements that can be checked against the sample, which is a stronger position than accepting a grade name nobody defines.

A sachet that claims mulberry leaf deserves a closer look than a sniff at arm's length. The bag holds a blend, and the blend's smell is rarely the leaf's smell: a resin or a wood two layers down can dominate the headspace while the leaf contributes a soft top note nobody can isolate. The honest test is to open the bag, take out a pinch of the visible leaf, crush it, and smell that. **Sachet headspace → dominated by the most volatile and most abundant material → the named leaf is often the quietest thing in the bag → a claim about the leaf cannot be checked from outside the bag.** Ask what proportion of the fill is leaf, and expect an answer in words rather than a number, because few small producers measure it.

## Safety

Burning any plant material produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, do not inhale concentrated smoke, and keep lit material and loose dried leaf away from children and pets. Dried leaf is a fire risk near an ember and should never be left smouldering unattended. Sachet contents are not food and should be kept out of reach of children. This page is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice; see the [safety guide](/safety/).

## FAQ

**What is mulberry leaf (桑叶), and is it the same as 桑葚?** The dried leaf of *Morus alba*, the white mulberry, a Moraceae tree grown across China as silkworm fodder. It carries a mild green, tea-like scent and belongs to the tea-fragrance register, which is why it appears in tea, sachets and light blends rather than as a lead incense material. 桑葚 is a different part of the same tree — the compound fruit — and 桑白皮 is the root bark. All three are separate materials with separate uses, and the *Bencao Gangmu* entry for the tree 桑 in 木之三 does not make them interchangeable.

**What does mulberry leaf (桑叶) smell like?** Mild, clean and green, with a soft hay edge and a faint sweetness — closer to a green leaf tea than to a brewed cup of any named tea. The comparison is to the register, not to a specific drink. Crushed dry leaf is the strongest expression; the infusion is softer; the smoke is a different smell altogether.

**Can I burn mulberry leaf on its own?** You can, but the result is disappointing. Leaf tissue burns to smoke and ash, and the acrid, carbon-heavy side of combustion covers the delicate green note. The material performs far better in a sachet, in a tea, or as a small proportion in a blend with a stable base.

**Is silkworm-fodder grade the same as tea grade?** No, and this is the trap of the material. Fodder is bought for volume, freshness and freedom from mould, on agricultural criteria. A tea or fragrance buyer is buying aroma, cleanliness and a drying curve that preserved the leaf. The two supply chains can touch, but the grade names do not transfer.

**Why does my dried leaf smell musty after a few months?** Almost always moisture. Dried leaf is hygroscopic, and a sealed jar stored in a warm room can still let the leaf pick up enough water to support mould. Brittle leaf stored cool, dark and genuinely dry holds its note far longer than leaf that was already leathery when it arrived.

## Summary

Mulberry leaf (桑叶) is the leaf of *Morus alba*, a Moraceae tree native to central and northern China and cultivated throughout the country for sericulture. Its aroma is mild, green and tea-like, which places it in the 茶香 register — a cold, top-note register that lives in dry leaf and infusion, not in smoke. It is an assistant material at best, useful for lifting a sachet or softening a heavy blend, and it fails when asked to lead. The part name is the specification: leaf, fruit and root bark are three different materials from one tree. The classical record documents tea and materia-medica use; it does not establish a health claim, and this page makes none.

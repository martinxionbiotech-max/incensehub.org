> **Direct answer:** 香附子 (xiāngfùzǐ) is a dried sedge tuber — the underground store of *Cyperus rotundus*, purple nutsedge — and one of the least glamorous materials in the Chinese incense cupboard. Its job in 合香 (hé xiāng, blended incense) is weight: a dry, spicy, faintly smoky earthiness placed under sweeter materials, plus a long-recorded role as the cheaper stand-in for 甘松 (spikenard).

## Key facts

> **Direct answer:** A small, dense, dried tuber from a cosmopolitan sedge, woody-spicy-earthy when warmed, recorded in Song and Ming incense manuals, and commercially interesting precisely because it is common.

The table is short because the material is simple. A reader who wants the whole page in one line can take this: an unremarkable weed produces an unremarkable-looking tuber, and the tuber carries a useful, quiet, earthy note that a formula can lean on. Everything else — the botany, the names, the substitution economics — is detail hung on that fact.

## A sedge tuber, described plainly

> **Direct answer:** The plant is a perennial sedge: stolons thread through the soil carrying egg-shaped tubers, while solitary stems rise 15–90 cm from a swollen base. Its leaves are narrow, blue-green, 2–5 mm wide. The floristic record gives it an elevation band from sea level up to 2100 m and a flowering window of May to November.

The plant is easy to walk past. A sedge of the family Cyperaceae, it puts up solitary stems from a swollen tuberous base, with leaf blades 2–5 mm wide and a blue-green cast that reads as grass at a glance. The stems are 15–90 cm, so the plant is low enough to walk through without noticing and tall enough to stand clear of a mown lawn. The flowers and fruit run from May to November. The whole above-ground plant is, in effect, a seasonal appendage to the thing underground.

That underground structure is the incense material. Slender stolons run through the soil and carry ellipsoidal tubers, each one a package of stored reserves and a bud for the next season's growth. The tuber is a storage organ first and an aromatic organ second, which explains a good deal of how the material behaves: it is dense, it dries hard, and its scent is locked into tissue rather than gathered in a resinous exudate that can be tapped.

Compare that with the materials most readers know. Agarwood is a resin-soaked wood, and its value scales with how much resin the tree laid down. Frankincense is a dried exudate, harvested as tears. Cyperus is a whole organ — root, storage tissue, and skin together. You cannot grade it by resin content because there is no resin to measure. What you can do is look at the tuber and smell it, which is why this page spends more time on screening than on abstract quality tiers.

**Chain — why the plant is so hard to clear.** A tuber chain forms along the stolon → each link can produce a new shoot → cultivation breaks the chain into fragments but rarely removes all of them → each surviving fragment restarts the cycle → the plant returns within a season. This is the mechanism behind its reputation as an agricultural weed, and it is also the reason the raw material never runs short.

## A weed with a place in the cupboard

> **Direct answer:** Abundance is the defining commercial fact about cyperus. The same plant that farmers spend money to remove supplies an incense tuber that is cheap, familiar, and available in almost any market — the opposite of a rarity premium.

Most aromatic materials are sold on scarcity. A tree that grows on one island, a resin that takes a decade to form, a root that only one valley produces: scarcity is the story, and price follows the story. Cyperus inverts that. It grows through Africa and Asia, across Australia and the Pacific islands, into Europe, and through the Americas; it infests fields, and no conservation body has any reason to protect it. The material is therefore cheap, and it will stay cheap.

That inversion has a practical consequence that buyers should hold on to. In this material, low price is not a warning sign. A buyer trained on resins learns to read a low price as a hint of adulteration or a poor lot; with cyperus, the low price is simply the truth about supply. **In our reading, rarity is a poor proxy for quality here** — the useful questions are about freshness, cleanliness, and whether the lot is what the label says, not about provenance romance.

The second consequence is less comfortable. Materials with prestige get graded, sorted, argued over, and written into standards. Weeds get bagged. Cyperus sits in the second category: no trade ladder, no recognised grades, very little published screening. A buyer who wants consistency has to build that consistency through sampling, because the market will not supply it as a shorthand.

## The names, and the aconite trap

> **Direct answer:** The name 香附子 pairs 香 ("fragrance") with 附子, the aconite side-tuber the cyperus tuber resembles in outline. That is a shape metaphor, not a statement about content. The other names are descriptive too: 香附 shortens it, 莎草根 means "sedge root," and 雀头香 — "sparrow-head incense" — repeats the shape logic.

The etymology is worth unpacking because it produces one of the few genuine confusions around this material. 香 is fragrance. 附子 is the name of the daughter tuber of aconite (*Aconitum carmichaelii*), a toxic plant used in traditional medicine only after processing. The cyperus tuber is small, dark, and tapered in a way that resembles that aconite side-tuber, so the name attached itself by shape.

**The naming trap is this:** 香附子 and 附子 are different materials with different safety profiles, and a reader who drops the first character can cross from a bland incense tuber to a toxic medicinal root. Nothing about the two is interchangeable. The resemblance is an outline, and the name records the outline rather than any botanical or chemical relationship.

The other names are easier. 香附 is what people say in ordinary use, and it is the form that appears in materia-medica writing. 莎草根 translates literally as "sedge root" and points at the plant rather than the tuber. 雀头香 repeats the shape logic, and it is the most poetic of the set: a small, pointed tuber that someone once thought looked like a bird's head. The Song *Xiangpu* (香譜) records 香附子 under 莎草根 and 雀头香 in its second scroll, citing the older *Bencao*; that is the clearest evidence that the names were already entangled by the Song.

What the variety does not indicate is confusion about the plant. Unlike 木香 or 藿香, where one written name covers materials that are genuinely different, the cyperus names all point at one species. The multiplication is a sign of how widely the plant was known, not of uncertainty about what it was.

## What it smells like

> **Direct answer:** Dry, woody, and spicy, with an earthy base and a faint smoke-like edge. It is not sweet, not floral, and not loud. Warmed, it reads as the smell of a dried root in a warm room rather than as perfume.

The first impression of a cold tuber is disappointing. Held to the nose straight from the jar, a good tuber gives very little — a dusty, faintly woody breath and not much else. That is not a fault. The aromatic tissue is locked inside a dry, hard matrix, and at room temperature the volatiles barely leave it. Warm the tuber in the hand, or crush a fragment, and the character arrives: wood first, then a spice that sits somewhere between pepper and a dry ginger, then the earthy base that is the material's signature.

| State | Impression | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Whole dried tuber, cold | Faint, dusty, almost nothing | Aromatic tissue is locked in the dry matrix |
| Tuber warmed in the hand | Woody-spice lifts over an earthy base | Tissue is intact and the lot is not musty |
| Freshly ground | Sharper and drier, spice ahead of earth | Grinding has opened new surface |
| On a burning coal | Low, smoky-earth, slow to build | The smoke note belongs to the material |
| Damp or aged stock | Flat, cardboard, sometimes sour | Storage failure, not a variety difference |

The smoke note divides people. Some readers expect an incense material to smell sweet before it burns, and cyperus does not. The faintly smoky edge is part of the dry-down even before combustion, and on the coal it becomes the dominant register. That is the point of the material in a blend: it supplies the low, dry, slightly ashen floor that sweeter aromatics can sit on.

## Where it sits in a 合香 formula

> **Direct answer:** Cyperus is a body note. Read through the 君臣佐使 (jūn chén zuǒ shǐ, chief–minister–assistant–guide) hierarchy of classical compounding, it belongs in the assistant layer — supporting the main aromatic rather than announcing itself. That is an editorial placement, not a claim about any single historical recipe.

The 君臣佐使 frame is a way of talking about rank inside a formula: a chief material sets the character, ministers support and extend it, assistants fill out the body and correct faults, and guides carry the blend. It comes from the materia-medica tradition, and it is useful here as a description of function rather than as a rule with fixed proportions.

Cyperus does assistant work. It has no bright top note to lead with and no strong sweetness to compete. What it has is weight. Put a small amount under a sweet floral or a soft sandalwood and the blend stops feeling thin at the base; the same blend without it can read as if it is floating. That is a real, repeatable observation in compounding, and it is why the material survived in formulas for centuries despite having no glamour of its own.

**Chain — how a body note changes a blend.** A sweet chief note dominates the opening → the base reads thin because nothing sits under it → cyperus is added at a low proportion → the dry-down gains an earthy floor → the sweetness above it reads rounder and less sugary → the finished blend feels more complete without any single note being identifiable. The effect is structural, not additive: the cyperus is doing its job when nobody can name it.

Dose judgement follows from that. There is no useful percentage to quote, because the right amount depends on how much weight the rest of the formula already has. The working rule is to start below the level where the root announces itself, then raise the proportion until the blend stops feeling hollow, then stop. A blend where a listener can pick out "cyperus" is usually a blend with too much cyperus.

## Pairing: what cyperus does to four kinds of blend

> **Direct answer:** Cyperus dries and darkens whatever it is paired with. It works best under resins and florals, holds its own beside woods, and competes badly with loud spices. These are editorial readings from bench practice, not rules handed down from a manual.

| Partner | What cyperus contributes | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Resins — frankincense, benzoin, myrrh | Dries the sweetness and stops the smoke turning cloying | Too much buries the resin's brighter top under an ashy floor |
| Florals — osmanthus, rose, jasmine | Gives a sweet base something to stand on | An overdose flattens the flower; the blend smells of root first |
| Woods — sandalwood, cedar | Reinforces the dry middle without adding a new register | Both are quiet and low, so the result can read as one flat brown note |
| Spices — clove, cassia, cardamom | Bridges the spice and the earth; dry heat reads as depth | Both speak in the same register, so the blend turns sharp rather than deep |

The floral row is the one that surprises people. It seems counter-intuitive that a dry root helps a flower, and the effect is easy to overshoot. The reason it works at all is that sweetness without a floor reads as thin rather than rich — the same reason a cook salts a dessert. The reason it fails is that the root has a voice of its own, and once that voice is audible the flower is no longer the subject of the blend.

The spice row is the opposite trap. Cyperus and clove both occupy the dry-warm band, and stacking two materials in one band does not double the effect; it narrows it. A blend built this way smells emphatic and small. If a spice is the point, the cyperus should drop to a trace or leave entirely.

## The 建宁宫中香 record

> **Direct answer:** The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records 香附子 in the Han-dynasty 建宁宫中香 palace formula, alongside 檀香, 藿香, 零陵香, 白芷, and 甘松. The list is the best evidence for how the material was used: as one voice in a large, layered formula rather than as a solo incense.

The formula is a court recipe transmitted through a Ming compilation, and its ingredient list is long and mixed: a mint-family aromatic (藿香), a fragrant grass (零陵香), sandalwood, a warm pungent root (白芷), spikenard, and cyperus among others. That mixture is the interesting part. A palace incense of this kind was not built around one precious material; it was built as a composition, with each ingredient covering a register the others did not.

Cyperus's register in that company is the earthy middle. 檀香 supplies the creamy wood, 藿香 and 零陵香 the green-aromatic top, 白芷 a warm pungency, 甘松 the heavy sweet-earth base. Cyperus adds dry weight between the pungent and the sweet, and it does so at low cost — a useful property in a formula that had to be made in quantity for a palace.

One caution belongs here. **A list of ingredients is not a formula.** The *Xiangcheng* entry names materials; it does not, in the passage usually quoted, give the proportions that would let a modern maker reproduce the blend. Reading the list as a recipe and guessing equal parts would produce something that smells nothing like the original. What the list does support is a claim about role: cyperus was a working ingredient in high-status compounding, not a peasant substitute used only when nothing better was available.

## Cyperus and spikenard: substitution, not identity

> **Direct answer:** 香附子 and 甘松 are different plants with related earthy notes. Cyperus has served as a substitute for spikenard where the latter was scarce or costly, and that substitution is a cost decision, not a botanical equivalence.

| Question | 香附子 — *Cyperus rotundus* | 甘松 — *Nardostachys jatamansi* |
|---|---|---|
| Plant | Perennial sedge, Cyperaceae | High-altitude perennial herb |
| Part used | Tuber carried on a stolon | Rhizome and root mass |
| Range | Cosmopolitan weed, near sea level to 2100 m | Himalayan high country |
| Supply | Abundant, low cost, widely traded | Limited, costlier, source-dependent |
| Aroma direction | Dry, woody-spicy, earthy, faintly smoky | Earthy-sweet, heavier, root-rich |
| Role in a formula | Body and weight; stand-in for 甘松 | A distinctive base note in its own right |
| Substitution status | The substitute | The material being substituted |

The table makes the trade-off visible. A maker who wants spikenard's heavy, sweet-earth base and uses cyperus instead gets the weight but not the sweetness. The blend will hold together; it will not smell the same. That is acceptable when the substitution is declared and the formula is adjusted around it — the sweet top can be pushed a little, the earthy middle accepted as its own thing.

It is not acceptable when the substitution is hidden. **The line between a legitimate substitution and adulteration is disclosure**, and it is the buyer's line to police. A supplier who sells cyperus as cyperus, at a cyperus price, has done nothing wrong. A supplier who sells cyperus into a spikenard order has changed the material without changing the label, and the resulting blend will be wrong in a way that no adjustment downstream can fix.

Detection is mostly paperwork and nose. The label should name the species. The price should be consistent with an abundant weed rather than a restricted Himalayan root. The aroma should be dry and smoky rather than sweet and heavy. None of those checks requires a laboratory, and all three together are hard to fake for long.

## From tuber to powder

> **Direct answer:** The tuber is cleaned, dried whole, and either stored whole or ground. Whole tubers keep better; powder blends better. Nothing else is required, and nothing else should be added.

Processing is the shortest section on this page because the material asks for very little. Tubers are lifted, washed free of soil, sorted, and dried. Drying is the only step that can ruin a lot: dry too slowly and mould gets a start; dry unevenly and some tubers stay soft while others go hard; store before the moisture is down and the whole bag goes musty. A properly dried tuber is hard, slightly shrunken, and clean-smelling, with the aromatic note dormant until it is warmed or ground.

**Chain — what drying does.** Fresh tuber carries a high water fraction → water occupies the tissue and supports microbial growth → drying removes that water → the tissue hardens and the aromatic fraction is retained in a stable matrix → the tuber can be stored without spoiling → grinding later exposes fresh surface and releases the note on demand. The sequence explains why whole tubers are the better storage form: the aromatic surface is protected until the moment a maker needs it.

| Form | What it is good for | What tends to go wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Whole dried tuber | Long storage, sorting, slow release | Uneven drying; hidden mould in a damp sack |
| Coarse powder | Open-textured sticks and cones | Poor cohesion; burns fast and hot |
| Fine powder | Dense packs and slow smoulder | Traps moisture; cakes; can smother the ember |
| Old powder | Nothing much | Flat aroma, dust, uneven burn |

Forming sits inside the wider craft sequence, [the stick-making process](/blog/how-incense-is-made/); for the bench behaviour of ground material, see [powder handling](/blog/incense-powder-resin/). The one point worth carrying from this page into those is that cyperus powder is a thirsty material: it takes up moisture from the air quickly, and a bag left open in a humid room will cake before the aroma has had time to fade. Storage follows from the same fact. Whole tubers want a dry cupboard, a sealed jar, and darkness; ground powder wants a smaller jar and a shorter horizon. Neither wants the shelf above a kettle, and neither recovers once damp has got in.

## Quality: what can be checked

> **Direct answer:** Cyperus has no standardised grades, so quality is a matter of screening each lot: dense clean tubers, a clean cold smell, a clear woody-spice when warmed, and dry powder that flows. There is no origin story or grade name that can substitute for those checks.

The absence of a grading ladder is not a scandal; it is a consequence of the material's economics. Nobody builds a grade system for a weed. The practical response is a short checklist that a buyer can run on a sample bench in ten minutes.

| Check | What good looks like | Common fault | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size and heft | Small, dense, heavy for its size | Light, hollow, papery | Density tracks intact tissue |
| Surface | Clean, root hairs removed | Soil packed into crevices | Soil burns to ash and dust |
| Cold smell | Faint, clean, rooty | Musty, sour, mouldy | Storage failure is not reversible |
| Warmed smell | Woody-spice lifts clearly | Flat or acrid | Aromatic fraction has degraded |
| Moisture | Hard and dry through | Soft, cool, damp | Mould risk and caking |
| Powder | Free-flowing, even colour | Clumped, dull, dusty | Moisture or age, or both |

Two of those rows deserve emphasis. Moisture is the fault that destroys the most material, because it is invisible in a sealed bag and only shows up when the powder cakes or the tuber grows a bloom of mould. Smell is the fault that matters most for the final product, because a musty tuber can be clean and dry and still wrong — the mould has already done its work. Neither fault can be corrected downstream. Screening is the only control.

## Three trials worth running on a new lot

> **Direct answer:** Before a lot goes into a batch, run three short trials — a visual sort, a warm-hand smell test, and a micro-burn on a coal. Together they catch the faults that paperwork cannot: soil, must, damp, and a smoke that turns acrid.

The trials are cheap and they take an afternoon. What they buy is a decision made before the material is mixed into anything expensive.

**Trial one — sort a handful on white paper.** Spread fifty tubers on a sheet and look at them as a group rather than one at a time. Colour should be reasonably even, the surface free of caked soil, and the shapes consistent enough that you are looking at one harvest rather than a blend of lots. Broken fragments and fine dust at the bottom of the bag are not a disaster, but a high proportion of them says the material has been handled roughly or stored too long. Pick out anything soft and set it aside; softness at this stage is the earliest visible sign of the moisture fault.

**Trial two — warm and smell, then smell again cold.** Take three tubers, hold them in a closed hand for a minute, and smell. The woody-spice should arrive without effort. Then let them cool and smell again: the note should retreat but not vanish into a cardboard flatness. A lot that smells stronger cold than warm is suspect, because it suggests something on the surface rather than in the tissue — dust, storage residue, or a contaminant.

**Trial three — burn a pinch on a coal.** A small pinch on a lit coal tells you what the stick will smell like at the end, when everything else has burned away. Expect low smoke, a dry earthy note, and no sharpness. Acridity here is a hard stop: it will not improve in a blend, and it will sit under the finished product for its whole burn.

**Chain — why the trials work in that order.** Visual sorting removes the physical faults → the warm test establishes that the aromatic tissue is intact → the burn test shows what combustion adds → each stage rules out a class of failure before the next, more expensive test → a lot that passes all three can be blended with reasonable confidence. Skipping the first two and going straight to the burn wastes material; skipping the burn and trusting the nose misses the faults that only appear at temperature.

## What cyperus is not

> **Direct answer:** Three materials get confused with 香附子, and the confusions are different in kind: a toxic aconite tuber that shares part of its name, a Himalayan root that it sometimes replaces, and the general assumption that any dark aromatic root is the same thing.

| Name | What it actually is | Why the confusion arises | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 香附子 | *Cyperus rotundus*, sedge tuber | — | The material this page describes |
| 附子 | Aconite daughter tuber, toxic | Shared characters; similar outline | Never an incense material; not interchangeable |
| 甘松 | *Nardostachys jatamansi*, rhizome | Similar earthy note; recorded substitution | Different plant, different aroma, different price |
| "Sedge root" generically | Any of several sedges | Common English name | Species name on the label is the only check |

The second row is the one that matters for safety. Aconite is a plant that requires expert processing before any medicinal use and has no place in a censer. Nothing in this page should be read as softening that: 香附子 is not 附子, and a label that says 附子 is describing something else entirely. The last row matters for quality: a buyer who accepts "sedge root" as a description has accepted a category, not a species, and the category contains more than one plant.

## The traditional record and its limits

> **Direct answer:** The Song *Xiangpu* and the Ming *Xiangcheng* document 香附子 as an incense material, and the materia-medica tradition describes 香附 as regulating qi (理气). The first is a record of use; the second is **traditional belief**, and no clinical evidence stands behind it. Nothing on this page is a medical claim.

The documentary chain is short and clear. The Song *Xiangpu* (香譜) lists 香附子 under the names 雀头香 and 莎草根 in its second scroll, citing the older *Bencao*. The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) includes it in a Han-dynasty palace blend, 建宁宫中香. Those two citations are enough to establish that the tuber was a recognised incense ingredient with a name, a role, and a place in high-status compounding. They do not establish anything about its effects on the body.

That distinction is worth stating plainly because incense writing often blurs it. 理气 — "regulating qi" — is a technical phrase from the materia-medica tradition, and it belongs to that frame of reference. It is not a description of a measurable physiological action, and a modern reader should not translate it into one. **The medicinal uses of 香附 are traditional belief, held within a classical system, and they are not established clinical fact.**

There is a second boundary, specific to incense. Burning plant material releases smoke laden with fine particles, and inhalation is an exposure route, not a therapy. A page about an aromatic tuber should not imply that burning it delivers a benefit; the honest position is that the aroma is the point and the smoke is a risk to manage. That is why the safety section below is about ventilation and attention rather than about dosage.

## Buying: a decision frame

> **Direct answer:** Buy cyperus by the job it has to do. Whole tubers for storage and control, powder for production, and a declared species name in every case. The material is cheap enough that there is no reason to accept an unlabelled lot.

| If the job is | Choose | Check before you buy |
|---|---|---|
| Body in a sweet or floral blend | Whole tubers, ground in-house | Warmed aroma, moisture, absence of must |
| Reproducing a classical formula | Whole tubers with a named species | Label says *Cyperus rotundus* |
| Filling a spikenard role at lower cost | Whole tubers, and declare the substitution | Aroma is dry, not sweet; price reflects a weed |
| Production powder for sticks | Powder with a stated particle size | Flow, caking, lot-to-lot consistency |
| Teaching or display | Intact whole tubers | Clean surface, representative size |

The fourth row is where most commercial disappointment originates. Powder is where a supplier can hide a poor lot: a musty tuber ground fine still looks like powder, and the fault only appears when the batch is mixed. Buying whole tubers and grinding in-house costs a step and buys a great deal of certainty. A maker who cannot grind in-house should at least ask for the particle size and a sample from the actual lot, not from a reference bag.

The third row is the ethical one. Substituting cyperus for spikenard is a legitimate cost decision when it is declared and the formula is rebalanced. It becomes adulteration when it is silent. Buyers who never ask which species they are buying make the silent version profitable, which is the mechanism by which the practice persists.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** Cyperus tuber is burned for scent, not eaten or used as medicine. Burning releases smoke and fine particles, so keep the piece in moving air, on a holder that cannot tip, and never leave it burning alone.

Three points cover the practical risk. Smoke is the first: any burning aromatic puts particles into the air, and a small room with a smouldering stick concentrates them. Ventilation is not a formality. The second is fire: a burning stick or cone needs a holder that will not tip, and it should not be left while it burns. The third is material identity: 香附子 is not 附子, and a buyer who confuses the two is handling a toxic root rather than an incense tuber.

On ingestion, the position is simple. This is not a food material and it is not a medicine. The classical record describes internal medicinal use of 香附 within the materia-medica tradition; that record is not a recommendation, and nothing on this page should be read as one. Keep the material out of reach of children and pets, and treat the smoke as something to clear rather than something to breathe deeply.

## Summary

A weed supplies this material, and the weed's habits explain almost everything about it. The tuber is a storage organ rather than an exudate, so there is no resin grade to read and no vintage to chase; quality is a question of whether the tissue is intact, clean, and dry. Its recorded work is unglamorous — weight under a sweeter blend, a dry floor beneath a flower — and its commercial life is shaped by the fact that it costs very little and never runs short. The classical manuals name it, the materia-medica tradition gives it a therapeutic language, and this page keeps that language at arm's length: 香附子 is an incense material here, and its medicinal reputation is **traditional belief**, not clinical proof.

## FAQ

**What is cyperus (香附子)?** A dried underground tuber taken from *Cyperus rotundus*, the sedge known in English as purple nutsedge. The plant is a weed of disturbed ground on several continents; the tuber is what the incense trade uses. Expect a low, dry, spicy-earth smell rather than anything bright or sweet.

**Is cyperus the same as spikenard?** No, and the difference is not cosmetic. 香附子 comes from a sedge in Cyperaceae; 甘松 comes from *Nardostachys jatamansi*, a herb of the high Himalaya. Their root notes are cousins rather than twins, and the substitution between them has always been driven by availability and cost. Treat a swap as a re-formulation, not a rename.

**What does cyperus smell like?** Cold, almost nothing — a dusty breath of wood. Warm it and the order arrives: dry wood, then spice, then an earthy base with smoke at the edge. The absence of sweetness is the defining feature, and it is exactly why the material is useful underneath something else.

**Is cyperus a wood or a resin?** Neither one. It is a tuber: a swollen store of starch and tissue sitting on an underground runner. That single fact rules out most of the quality vocabulary borrowed from agarwood or frankincense — there is no resin load to measure, no grain, no tear shape — and leaves density, cleanliness, dryness, and smell as the working criteria.

**Why is 香附子 named after aconite?** Because the tuber's outline resembles the side-tuber of aconite, which is called 附子. The name is a visual analogy and nothing more: the plants are unrelated, and 香附子 contains no aconite. The risk runs the other way — a label reading 附子 alone describes a toxic medicinal root, not this incense material.

## Evidence & Sources

### Identity
**Source:** the Flora of China treatment of *Cyperus rotundus* and the GBIF backbone record for the species, both linked under Sources below. **Evidence class:** Established — morphology, elevation band, and range come from the floristic record.

### Use in the classical manuals
**Source:** a second-scroll entry in the Song *Xiangpu* (香譜) lists 香附子 under 雀头香 and 莎草根, citing the older *Bencao*; the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) gives it a place in the Han-dynasty 建宁宫中香 palace recipe. **Evidence class:** Historically documented — a record of use, not of clinical effect.

### Naming and substitution
**Source:** the recorded 香附子 / 甘松 substitution, and the separate 香附子 / 附子 naming distinction. **Evidence class:** Field screening.

### Combustion
**Source:** burning aromatic plant material releases smoke and fine particles; work in a room with moving air. **Evidence class:** Established.

**Direct answer:** Ginkgo (银杏, yínxìng) is the seed of *Ginkgo biloba*, the last surviving species of an entire plant division and a tree that has been cultivated in China for so long that its wild origin is uncertain. The seed — 白果 (báiguǒ, "white fruit") — is a mild, nutty, faintly bitter kernel eaten roasted or boiled, and it carries a documented safety file that matters more than any aromatic claim. It is not an incense material: it has no classical role in 合香 (hé xiāng, blended incense), its scent is a quiet food note, and this page records it as a food seed rather than inflating it into a 香材 (fragrant material).

## One tree, four names, two products

> **Direct answer:** Chinese distinguishes the tree (银杏), the seed (白果), an older name for the seed (鸭脚子), and the leaf (银杏叶). The seed is food; the leaf is the part behind the modern extract trade. Confusing the two is the most common error made about this plant.

The naming is precise once it is laid out, and the precision earns its keep at the point of purchase:

| Term | What it names | What it is for | Record |
|---|---|---|---|
| 银杏 (yínxìng) | The tree — "silver apricot" | Shade, seed, timber, ornament | Long cultivated |
| 白果 (báiguǒ) | The kernel — "white fruit" | Kitchen and materia medica | Documented food use |
| 鸭脚子 (yājiǎozǐ) | An older label for the same kernel — "duck-foot" | Historic synonym, not a different grade | Named in the *Bencao Gangmu* |
| 银杏叶 (yínxìng yè) | The leaf | Extract manufacture, never the kitchen | Modern, separate reputation |

A purchase order that says 银杏 is ambiguous, because a seller may read it as leaf, kernel, or extract depending on which department answers. The unambiguous word for the food product is 白果. That single substitution prevents most of the mismatches in this category.

### Seed against leaf

| Question | The kernel (白果) | The leaf (银杏叶) |

| How it is used | Cooked and eaten | Processed into an extract |

The two parts have separate trades, separate regulations, and separate literatures, and a sentence about one tells you nothing about the other. That is why this page keeps returning to the part: "ginkgo" without a part name is not a material, it is a tree.

## The botanical outlier

> **Direct answer:** *Ginkgo biloba* is the only living member of the division Ginkgophyta, a lineage that predates the flowering plants. It is a deciduous tree with fan-shaped, bilobed leaves that turn bright yellow in autumn, and it is dioecious — male and female structures sit on separate trees.

Botanical fame of this kind is unusual, and it is worth stating precisely what is and is not remarkable. The tree is not a "living fossil" because it is old in years; individual trees are long-lived but not extraordinary. It is a living fossil because its lineage is the sole survivor of a whole division: every other species in Ginkgophyta is extinct.

The reproductive biology is the detail most readers have never heard. Ginkgo is dioecious, meaning a given tree is male or female rather than both. Pollen moves by wind from male trees to ovules on female trees, and the male gametes are motile — flagellated cells that swim, a trait more familiar in ferns and cycads than in a large tree. That combination of wind pollination and swimming gametes is one of the reasons the species is studied as an evolutionary holdover.

The autumn colour deserves its own sentence, because it explains why the tree is planted where it is. As the season turns, the leaves stop making chlorophyll — the green pigment that masks everything else — and yellow carotenoids that were present all along become visible. Nothing is added; a mask is lifted. The display is short and dependable, which is exactly what a street planting wants, and it is a large part of why a tree with such an awkward fruit still earns its place in cities.

The practical consequences of dioecy are what a reader is more likely to notice on a street:

- Only female trees bear seeds, so a planting intended to produce 白果 must include females and a pollen source.
- Ripe seeds fall with their fleshy coat intact, and that coat smells rancid — a genuine nuisance in pedestrian areas.
- Because of that, municipal and landscape plantings often specify male trees, which is why many city ginkgos never fruit at all.

That last point explains a common experience: people who know ginkgo as a beautiful autumn tree with no fruit are usually looking at a male cultivar in a managed planting, not at a different species.

## The autumn problem

> **Direct answer:** When female ginkgoes drop ripe seed, the fleshy coat splits and releases a smell that most people describe as rancid or vomit-like. That single fact shapes how the tree is planted in cities and how the seed is collected for food.

The sequence is predictable every autumn: female tree bears ovules → wind brings pollen from a male tree nearby → fertilised ovules develop into seed → the fleshy coat ripens to yellow-orange → fallen seed bursts or is trodden on → the coat's odour spreads across pavement. The smell is not a sign of decay in the kernel; it is the normal state of a ripe sarcotesta.

For a city, the calculation is straightforward. A male tree delivers the autumn colour, the fan-shaped leaves, and the architectural silhouette with none of the fruit. That is why so many street and park plantings specify male stock, and why a reader may go years without seeing a ginkgo seed at all despite walking past the trees daily.

For a forager, the same fact is a warning and an instruction:

- **Gloves, not bare hands.** The coat stains and can irritate skin. Handle fallen seed with protection and wash up afterwards.
- **Avoid street collections.** Seeds from paved, trafficked ground carry road grime and whatever the municipality has sprayed; the food trade works from managed trees for good reason.
- **Process promptly.** The coat deteriorates quickly, and a bag of seed left in a warm car will smell far worse by evening.
- **Discard the doubtful.** Soft, discoloured, or mouldy seed is not worth the risk, because the hazard in this material is not one you can taste your way past.

That last rule is the one to keep. With most foods, spoilage announces itself and the cook can trim around it. With ginkgo, the toxin is invisible and odourless, so the decision has to be made on the look and feel of the seed rather than on the smell of the coat.

The odour does have one legitimate use, and it belongs to the collector rather than the cook. A coat that has just turned rancid signals that the seed is ripe and the kernel is fully formed; waiting longer means the coat breaks down into a slippery mess that is harder to remove and stains more aggressively. Collectors therefore read the smell as a clock, not as a quality grade. Once the coat is off and the kernel is clean, smell tells you almost nothing about whether the seed is good — that judgement belongs to firmness, colour, and the absence of mould.

## Why "living fossil" does not mean "incense material"

> **Direct answer:** Botanical rarity and aromatic usefulness are separate properties, and ginkgo is the clearest case in this encyclopedia. The tree's lineage is genuinely ancient; the seed's role in incense is genuinely absent. One fact does not imply the other.

This is the page's central correction, and it is worth spelling out as a chain, because the inflation happens in steps rather than all at once:

a lineage survives a mass extinction → the tree is described as a "living fossil" → the word carries prestige and a sense of the sacred → marketing language borrows that prestige → a reader assumes the material belongs in ritual or incense use → the assumption hardens into a claim that no classical text supports

Each step is small enough to seem reasonable. Together they produce a product story with no documentary basis. The honest position is narrower and more interesting: ginkgo is a botanical celebrity and a food seed, and the two facts coexist without needing a third.

What the classical record actually contains is a food and materia-medica entry, not an incense entry. The seed does not appear among the aromatic materials of the 香乘 (Xiāng Chéng) canon, and it is not worked into 合香 formulas as a fragrant ingredient. Its scent, described below, explains why: it is a mild nutty note that reads as food, not as fragrance.

The point is not to diminish the tree. It is to keep the tree's real distinction from being spent on a claim it cannot support. A reader who wants the botanical story has a remarkable one; a reader who wants an incense material should be told, plainly, that this is the wrong page.

## What would make a seed work as incense

> **Direct answer:** A seed becomes an incense material when it brings volatile aroma, suitable combustion behaviour, and a place in the blending tradition. Ginkgo seed fails every test, and naming the tests makes the absence legible instead of mysterious.

Four properties decide whether any plant part belongs in a burner:

1. **Volatile yield.** The material must release aromatic molecules at combustion or smouldering temperatures, in enough quantity to be perceived. A kernel that smells of nuts when hot is releasing Maillard products from cooking, not a stored aromatic oil.
2. **Resin or oil chemistry.** Woods and resins work because they carry terpenes, resins, and gums that volatilise in a controlled way. A starchy seed carries none of that in quantity.
3. **Combustion behaviour.** The material must smoulder steadily, hold an ash, and not produce a harsh or acrid smoke out of proportion to its scent. Dense starch tends to smoulder poorly.
4. **Cultural placement.** The material needs a documented role in a blending tradition — a reason to be in the formula beyond novelty — or it will always be an experiment rather than a recipe.

Ginkgo seed scores low on every line. Its aroma is heat-generated and faint; its chemistry is starch and protein rather than resin; its burn is a food burn rather than an aromatic one; and the classical record places it in the kitchen and the materia medica, not in 合香.

This framework is more useful than a simple yes-or-no, because it can be applied to the next unfamiliar material a reader meets. A seller offering a new "incense seed" can be asked which of the four properties it actually has. The answer is usually informative, even when it is evasive.

A bench test takes ten minutes and settles most of it. Put a small piece on a burning charcoal disc, away from anything else, and watch three things: whether it catches and smoulders without going out, what the smoke smells like at one minute and at three, and what residue is left. A resinous material gives a steady glow and a smell that develops; a starchy kernel chars, smells of toast and then of scorched starch, and leaves a crumbly black ash with no aromatic after-note. The difference is obvious in the nose, and it is the reason a formula book and a kitchen rarely share ingredients.

## What the seed smells and tastes like

> **Direct answer:** The kernel is mild and nutty with a faint sweetness and a starchy, slightly bitter finish — closer to a roasted chestnut or a broad bean than to any spice, resin, or flower. The aroma is a cooking aroma, produced mostly by heat, and it does not survive as a perfumery note.

Raw kernels are bland and slightly astringent. Heat changes them: roasting or boiling develops a soft, sweet nuttiness and softens the bitterness, while the texture becomes dense and slightly waxy, like a chestnut. Overcooking turns them mealy; undercooking leaves both the texture and the bitterness raw.

The bitterness is not a defect to be eliminated. It is part of the seed's character, and it sits at the edge of the sweetness rather than dominating it. Cooks usually balance it with something else — a sweet syrup in a dessert, a savoury broth in a stew — rather than trying to remove it.

For aromatic purposes, the honest assessment is that there is very little to work with:

- The note is **weak** relative to any material used in incense, and it is generated by heat rather than present in the raw seed.
- It is **food-shaped**: nutty, starchy, slightly sweet. It does not blend into floral, woody, or resinous compositions without reading as "food" in the middle of them.
- It is **transient**: what smells pleasant in a bowl of soup is gone by the time a stick has cooled.

That is not a criticism of the seed. It is a description of what kind of material it is. A chestnut is not a worse thing for not being sandalwood.

## From fallen seed to kernel

> **Direct answer:** Processing is culinary and sequential: collect the ripe seed, remove the fleshy, foul-smelling outer coat, crack the white shell, and cook the kernel. The coat is the part that stains and irritates skin, and the cooking step is the part that reduces — but does not eliminate — the seed's toxin.

The sequence matters because each step has a hazard or a quality consequence attached to it:

ripe seed falls or is collected → the fleshy sarcotesta softens and turns rancid → the coat is removed, usually with gloves → the white shell is cracked → the kernel is boiled or roasted → the cooked kernel is eaten in modest quantity

The first step is where most of the mess lives. The sarcotesta is yellow to orange-yellow when ripe and carries a strong rancid odour; it also stains hands and can provoke a skin reaction in people who handle it bare. Commercial processors remove it mechanically or with protected hands, and home cooks who gather fallen seed usually learn the glove lesson once.

The shell is hard and white with two or three longitudinal ridges, and it must be cracked without crushing the kernel inside. A common approach is a light tap along the ridge, or a brief boil to soften before shelling. Either way, the goal is an intact kernel, because a broken one cooks unevenly and turns mealy at the edges.

Cooking is not only a texture step. It is the step that makes the seed safe to eat at all, and even then the margin is narrower than most foods. The next section deals with that directly.

## Cooking with 白果

> **Direct answer:** The kernel is treated like a starchy nut: cracked out of its shell, blanched to remove the thin brown membrane, then roasted, simmered, or added to soups and desserts. The membrane is bitter and should go; the cooking time should be generous rather than minimal.

A workable home sequence looks like this:

1. **Crack the shell** along one of the longitudinal ridges with a light tap, keeping the kernel whole.
2. **Blanch** the shelled kernels briefly in boiling water. The thin brown skin loosens and can be rubbed off; leaving it on adds bitterness and a slightly gritty texture.
3. **Cook properly** — simmer in a soup or syrup, or roast in salt — until the kernel is tender through, not al dente.
4. **Season around it**, not on it. The kernel is bland and slightly sweet, so it takes on the flavours around it.

Roasting in coarse salt is the street-food method and the one that develops the nuttiest aroma: the salt bed distributes heat evenly and keeps the kernels from scorching. Boiling is gentler and is the usual route when the seed will go into a soup or a sweet syrup.

Pairings follow the starch logic. In Cantonese sweet soups the kernel sits with tofu skin, barley, and rock sugar, where its mildness balances the sweetness. In savoury cooking it goes into chicken or pork stews and braised dishes, where it absorbs the sauce and adds body rather than flavour. Japanese cooking treats it as a beer snack — grilled, salted, eaten by the handful — which is exactly the pattern of consumption that calls for portion discipline.

Storage is unglamorous. Keep whole seed dry and cool, shell only what you will cook, and refrigerate or freeze surplus kernels. Cooked kernels are perishable and should be treated like any cooked starch. The seed does not improve with age; the bitterness sharpens and the texture dries.

## The safety file

> **Direct answer:** Ginkgo seeds contain ginkgotoxin (4′-O-methylpyridoxine), and poisoning has been documented after people ate raw or insufficiently cooked kernels — with children at particular risk. Cooking reduces the hazard but does not reliably remove it, so the seed is a food to prepare properly and eat in modest quantity, never a snack to eat freely.

This is the part of the page that deserves the most care, because the gap between "traditional food" and "safe in any quantity" is where harm happens.

| Hazard | Where it sits | What it does | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ginkgotoxin (4′-O-methylpyridoxine) | The kernel | Associated with neurological symptoms in documented poisoning cases | Cook thoroughly; keep portions modest; do not give to children casually |
| Ginkgolic acids and related irritants | The fleshy outer coat | Contact dermatitis in sensitive people | Remove the coat with gloves; wash hands and tools |
| Rancid, mouldy coat | Outer coat and stored seed | Spoilage, off-flavours, mould risk | Discard discoloured or soft seed; dry and store properly |
| Choking and texture risk | Whole kernel | Dense, slippery food for small children | Cut kernels; supervise |

Three framing points keep this precise:

**Cooking is risk reduction, not elimination.** Boiling or roasting lowers the toxin load, and traditional cuisines have always cooked the seed. It does not make the kernel toxin-free, which is why quantity and frequency matter and why "it is a traditional food" is not by itself a safety argument.

**Children are the group to watch.** Documented poisoning cases cluster among children who ate seeds raw or lightly cooked. A household rule that keeps 白果 away from young children, or limits them to a taste within a properly cooked dish, is a reasonable precaution rather than an alarmist one.

**The leaf is a different product with a different literature.** Leaf extract safety questions do not transfer to the seed, and seed cooking advice does not transfer to leaf extract. Keep the part attached to the claim.

None of this is medical advice, and none of it is a diagnosis. Readers on medication, pregnant readers, and anyone managing a health condition should treat the seed as a food to discuss with a clinician rather than as a remedy. What this page can say with confidence is the shape of the hazard: it is dose-related, it is reduced by cooking, and it is concentrated in the kernel.

A few kitchen habits carry most of the practical protection:

- **Cook from raw, not from habit.** A kernel that is merely warmed through has not had the treatment the seed needs. Boil or roast until tender all the way through.
- **Keep the raw seed away from grazing.** A bowl of shelled kernels on a counter is an invitation, and the people most at risk are the ones least likely to read a label.
- **Label leftovers.** Cooked kernels kept in the fridge should be identifiable as 白果, with the cooking date, so they are not mistaken for a neutral nut and eaten cold by the handful.
- **Separate the tools.** The coat's irritant and stain transfer to boards, cloths, and hands; wash them rather than wiping them down.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary discipline any kitchen applies to a food with a known hazard, and it is more useful than memorising a number.

One more boundary. The usual incense-smoke warning applies here only in an unusual case — a blend that includes ginkgo seed as a novelty. If someone does burn it, the smoke is the exposure, exactly as with any plant material, and the [safety guide](/safety/) covers ventilation and distance. The far more likely risk with this material is on a plate, not on a burner.

## Judging quality as a food seed

> **Direct answer:** 白果 is graded as food: kernel fill, freshness, absence of mould, and clean husking. There is no incense grade, because there is no incense use — a "ginkgo grade" for burning would be a category invented by the seller.

| Check | What good looks like | Common failure | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kernel fill | Plump, heavy for its size, no hollow sound | Shrivelled, light, hollow | Thin kernels cook to mush and taste bitter |
| Colour | Even ivory to pale yellow | Grey, green-tinged, or dark patches | Ageing or mould, not character |
| Smell | Faint nutty sweetness, nothing sour | Rancid, sour, or chemical | Spoilage that cooking will not fix |
| Husking | Coat fully removed, shell clean | Sticky residue, fragments of coat | Residue carries the irritant and the smell |
| Moisture | Dry shell, no condensation in the pack | Sweating, clumping | Mould risk in storage |
| Damage | Intact kernels, few cracks | Cracked, crushed, broken | Uneven cooking, faster staling |

The order is deliberate. Kernel fill and freshness are the quality questions; colour and smell are the evidence. A buyer who only looks at size will accept large but hollow seed, which is the most common disappointment in this trade.

There is a commercial reason to grade by fill rather than by count. Seed is sold by weight, and a lot of large but light kernels can look impressive and yield less edible material than a smaller, denser lot. For a food buyer the useful question is how much sound kernel a kilo delivers, not how photogenic the shells are.

## Forms in the food trade

> **Direct answer:** 白果 moves as whole seed in the shell, as shelled kernels, as cooked or frozen kernels, and in prepared dishes. Every form is culinary; none of them is an incense form.

| Form | What it is | Where it goes | Handling note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole seed in shell | Intact shell with kernel inside | Retail and home cooking | Store dry and cool; crack before cooking |
| Shelled kernel | Kernel with coat and shell removed | Kitchens, food service | Use quickly; the kernel stales faster than the shell protects |
| Frozen kernel | Blanched and frozen | Year-round supply | Do not refreeze after thawing |
| Cooked, ready to use | Boiled or roasted kernels | Desserts, congee, dishes | Treat as perishable |
| Canned or vacuum-packed | Shelf-stable kernels | Asian grocery trade | Check for off-odour on opening |

The form determines the shelf life more than the harvest date does. A whole seed in its shell is protected by the shell and can keep for a season under good conditions; a shelled kernel is exposed and declines quickly. That is why the same harvest can be sold at very different prices depending on how much work has been done to it — and why a buyer should ask about form before comparing quotes.

A simple decision flow covers most of what a home buyer needs:

is the seed still in its shell? → yes: store it dry and cool, crack only what you will cook → no: is the kernel firm and pale, with no sour smell? → yes: cook it properly and eat it soon → no: discard it without tasting

That flow is deliberately blunt at the last step. There is no culinary rescue for a soft, sour, or mouldy kernel, and the cost of throwing away a handful is trivial compared with the cost of being wrong.

## Reading a "ginkgo" label

> **Direct answer:** Four questions separate the products sold under this name: which part, whether it is food or extract, how it was prepared, and what the safety line says. Most confusion disappears once the part is named.

| Question | Clear answer | Vague answer | What it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Which part? | 白果 (seed) or 银杏叶 (leaf) named | "ginkgo" alone | Whether it is food or an extract product |
| Which category? | Food, or supplement/extract | "natural ginkgo" | Which safety file applies |
| How prepared? | Roasted, boiled, frozen, raw | no preparation stated | Whether the cooking step is still yours to do |
| What safety text? | Cooking and portion guidance | none | Whether the seller understands the hazard |

A label that says only "ginkgo" is not hiding anything sinister; it is simply not specific enough to be useful. The word names a tree, and a tree is not a product. The same is true in the other direction: a claim about the leaf's extract should not be read as a claim about the kernel on a plate, and a warning about eating raw seed should not be transferred to a packaged extract.

For a buyer, the useful habit is to ask for the part before discussing anything else — price, origin, grade, or use. Every other question depends on that answer, and a supplier who cannot give it is not yet describing a material.

## The seed category: food versus incense

> **Direct answer:** Three seeds sit near each other in this database and play three different roles. Cypress seed is a working incense material; ginkgo and torreya are food. The family, not the word "seed", predicts which is which.

| Seed | Source plant | Family | What it is for | Does it carry aroma into a burner? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cypress seed (柏子) | *Platycladus orientalis* | Cupressaceae | Incense — the 柏子香 tradition | Yes: resinous and woody |
| Ginkgo (白果) | *Ginkgo biloba* | Ginkgoaceae | Kitchen and materia medica | No: a faint food note at most |
| Torreya (香榧) | *Torreya grandis* | Taxaceae | Kitchen and materia medica | No: rich and oily, but not aromatic |

The order is deliberate: the one incense material comes first, and the two foods follow. Read that way, the category stops looking like a list of similar objects and starts looking like a lesson in plant chemistry. Resin-rich families put something into the air when they smoulder; starchy seeds put something on a plate.

For a reader, the practical significance is that "seed" describes a shape, not a function. A catalogue grouping 白果, 香榧, and 柏子 under one heading is sorting by morphology. A formulator sorting by behaviour would file 柏子 with the resins and the other two with the pantry.

## The traditional record

> **Direct answer:** The documented record is culinary and materia-medica. 白果 is eaten roasted or boiled, and the *Bencao Gangmu* lists 银杏 under 白果 and 鸭脚子 with a Song-dynasty tribute origin for the name. Medicinal uses belong to traditional belief, not to established clinical fact.

The record here is thin in one direction and rich in another, and the shape of that asymmetry is the story. Food is documented abundantly; incense is documented not at all.

On the culinary side, the kernel has been eaten roasted or boiled for as long as the tree has been cultivated, appearing in everyday dishes rather than only in festival food. That is the best-attested use by a wide margin, and it is the one a modern buyer is actually participating in.

On the naming side, the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) lists the seed in its fruit section under 白果 and 鸭脚子, and explains the name 银杏 — "silver apricot" — as a court-era coinage tied to the tree's entry into tribute during the Song dynasty. The detail is a record of how a name travelled, not of how the seed was used.

The tribute detail is worth pausing on, because it explains the name rather than the plant. A name that enters the record through tribute tells you about court fashion and regional offerings; it does not tell you that the seed was burned, prized as an aromatic, or used in ritual. Readers sometimes infer ceremonial status from the word "tribute", and that inference is not supported by anything in the entry.

On the medicinal side, 白果 appears in traditional Chinese medicine. This page treats that as traditional belief and the language of materia medica, not as clinical evidence, and it makes no therapeutic claim of any kind.

On cultivation, the tree has been tended for so long that its wild origin is uncertain; Flora of China records it as perhaps native to northwestern Zhejiang, at Tianmu Shan, and widely cultivated elsewhere. A plant that has travelled with people for centuries is a poor candidate for a clean "native range" story.

What is missing from the record is just as informative. There is no entry in the classical incense canon, no formula tradition, no trade description of the seed as an aromatic. For a material with this much cultural visibility, that silence is not an oversight. It reflects the seed's actual behaviour in a burner.

## FAQ

**Is ginkgo nut the same as ginkgo leaf?** No. 白果 is the seed, a food with its own safety profile built around ginkgotoxin. 银杏叶 is the leaf, the part behind the extract trade. They come from the same tree but belong to different product categories, different regulations, and different safety literatures.

**Are ginkgo seeds safe to eat?** They are a traditional food that must be cooked, and even then they are not a free snack. The kernels contain ginkgotoxin, poisoning has been documented after raw or undercooked seeds were eaten, and children are at particular risk. Cook thoroughly, keep quantities modest, and treat the seed as food rather than as a remedy.

**Is ginkgo a classical incense ingredient?** No. It does not appear in the 香乘 canon and has no documented role in 合香. Its scent is a mild, heat-generated food note rather than an aromatic material, which is consistent with its absence. The tree's botanical celebrity stands; its aromatic role does not.

**Why is ginkgo called a living fossil?** Because it is the only living member of the division Ginkgophyta — a lineage older than the flowering plants, with every other species in its group extinct. The phrase describes the lineage's isolation, not the age of any individual tree.

**Can ginkgo seed be burned as incense?** It can be burned, in the sense that any dry plant material can, but it makes a poor incense: the note is faint, food-shaped, and generated by cooking heat, and it does not survive as a room scent. There is no classical or commercial tradition of burning it.

## Summary

A tree that outlived its entire division, and a kernel that belongs in a soup pot — that is the pairing this entry records. *Ginkgo biloba* survives as the only ginkgophyte: a dioecious, wind-pollinated tree whose fan-shaped leaves turn gold in autumn, bearing a seed of 2.5–3.5 × 1.6–2.2 cm inside a fleshy coat that smells foul and is stripped away before the white-shelled kernel can be used. That kernel, 白果, is food — starchy, mild, faintly bitter, roasted or boiled, named in the *Bencao Gangmu* beside 鸭脚子 and linked by name to Song-dynasty tribute. Its safety file is the part worth remembering: ginkgotoxin in the seed, documented harm from raw or undercooked kernels, and children at particular risk. Its incense file is empty, and that emptiness is a finding about the material rather than a gap in the research.

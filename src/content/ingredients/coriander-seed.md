> **Direct answer:** Coriander seed (芫荽籽, yánsuī zǐ) is the dried fruit of *Coriandrum sativum*, an annual umbellifer native to the Mediterranean and western Asia and now grown across China. It smells warm, citrusy, and faintly woody, and it works in incense as a bright spice accent rather than a base. The leaf of the same plant — cilantro, 芫荽 or 香菜 — is a different aromatic altogether, and the two should never be treated as one material.

Two facts organise everything else on this page. The first is that the thing sold as coriander seed is a fruit, not a seed in the botanical sense. The second is that the plant produces two aromatics that could hardly be more different. Get those straight and the material stops being ambiguous.

## The fruit that trade calls a seed

**Direct answer:** What the spice trade sells as coriander seed is the whole dried fruit of *Coriandrum sativum* — a small, round, ridged schizocarp that normally holds two mericarps. Each mericarp carries oil ducts in its wall, and those ducts are where the aroma lives.

The distinction is not pedantry. A true seed sits inside the fruit; the coriander "seed" is the fruit itself, ridges, oil canals, and all. That anatomy explains the crush test used across the spice trade: the aromatic oil is held in channels within the fruit wall, so the whole fruit smells mild until the wall is ruptured. Crush it and the oil ducts break; the linalool-rich fraction escapes at once.

| Structure | What it is | Why it matters to a buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit | The whole dried schizocarp, usually two joined mericarps | The traded unit; intact fruit keeps aroma longest |
| Ridges | Raised ribs running along the fruit | Intact ridges signal sound handling; rubbed-down fruit has lost surface oil |
| Oil ducts | Channels in the fruit wall holding the essential oil | The reservoir the crush test releases |
| Mericarp split | The two halves separating in storage or handling | Split fruit dries out faster and loses aroma sooner |
| Calyx remnant | The small stem scar at one end | A clean scar suggests careful harvesting; grit and stem fragments do not |

*Coriandrum sativum* is an annual, which shapes the supply. There is no perennial root or slow-growing heartwood to wait for; the crop is sown, grown, flowered, and harvested within a season, and the fruit is dried and sold. That makes coriander a volume commodity rather than a rarity, and it explains why the material is judged on freshness and handling rather than on age or provenance mystique.

Flora of China records the species as cultivated across China and notes the dual use directly: the stem and leaves serve as a culinary herb, while the fruit is used as a spice. One botanical entry, two products, two supply chains. A buyer who reads only the species name has read half the specification.

## Two aromatics from one plant

**Direct answer:** The fruit and the leaf of *Coriandrum sativum* are opposite materials. The fruit is warm, citrusy, and woody, built around linalool; the leaf is green, sharp, and polarising, built around aldehydes that many people read as soapy. Only the fruit has an incense role.

| | Fruit — 芫荽籽 | Leaf — 芫荽 / 香菜 |
|---|---|---|
| Plant part | Dried ripe fruit (schizocarp) | Fresh green leaf and stem |
| Dominant chemistry | Linalool and related alcohols | Green aldehydes |
| Scent direction | Warm citrus, woody, faintly floral | Fresh, grassy, sharp |
| Common human reaction | Pleasant, spice-like | Divided; a soapy note for some tasters |
| Kitchen role | Spice for blends, pickles, curries, baking | Garnish and fresh herb |
| Incense role | Fresh spice accent in 合香 | None |
| Shelf life | Months to years when whole and dry | Days, refrigerated |
| Failure mode | Flat, dusty, musty | Yellowed, slimy, lost |

The chemistry is the reason the contrast is so complete. Linalool is a rounded, sweet, floral-citrus alcohol; the leaf's aldehydes are sharp, green, and volatile. They do not blend into a family resemblance — they pull in different directions.

For a formulator, this has a hard consequence. A recipe that calls for coriander seed cannot be made with cilantro, and a dish that wants fresh cilantro is not improved by ground seed. The names collapse in English ("coriander" can mean either in some dialects) and the plant part is the only reliable separator.

There is also a perceptual point worth stating plainly: the soapy reaction to cilantro is real for the people who experience it, not a joke or a failure of taste. It is a useful reminder that "aroma" is not a property of a material alone — it is an interaction between molecules and a nose. Coriander seed sits on the friendly side of that divide for most people; the leaf does not, for a substantial minority.

## Reading the Chinese name

**Direct answer:** 芫荽籽 is a transparent compound: 芫荽 names the plant, 籽 names the seed, and the label separates the spice from the leaf by construction. The older name 胡荽 carries the 胡 prefix that marks a foreign introduction, the same marker found in 胡椒 and 葫芦巴.

Chinese plant names often do taxonomic work that English leaves undone. Here the suffix 籽 does the work of the English phrase "seed" — and unlike "coriander," it cannot be mistaken for the leaf. A supplier who writes 芫荽籽 has named the part; a supplier who writes only 芫荽 has named the plant and left the part open.

The 胡 element is a historical layer. It appears in names for plants that entered China from the west — 胡椒 for pepper, 葫芦巴 for fenugreek — and it functions as a provenance tag rather than a description. Coriander's route into Chinese kitchens came from the same direction, and the old name preserves that memory. Modern usage prefers 芫荽, with 香菜 ("fragrant vegetable") as the everyday word for the leaf.

| Name | Characters | What it names | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 芫荽 | yánsuī | The plant | Standard modern name |
| 芫荽籽 | yánsuī zǐ | The fruit/seed | The precise spice term |
| 胡荽 | húsuī | The plant, older name | 胡 marks western introduction |
| 香菜 | xiāngcài | The leaf herb | Everyday culinary name |

For anyone building a specification, 芫荽籽 is the term to insist on. It is unambiguous in a way that "coriander" is not, and it survives translation better than most spice names.

## What the fruit contributes to a blend

**Direct answer:** Coriander seed adds a bright, citrus-woody lift that sits above heavier materials and fades before they do. In 合香 it works as an accent alongside fennel and citrus peel, not as a structural material. Its job is to open a blend, not to hold it together.

Think of a blend as having a front, a middle, and a floor. The floor is resins, woods, and roots — materials that release slowly and persist. The middle is where most spices live. The front is the volatile fraction that arrives in the first minutes. Coriander seed belongs at the front and the upper middle: it gives the blend a citrus edge at the opening, then steps back.

That placement is why the material is dosed cautiously. A bright accent that overwhelms the opening leaves the blend smelling of coriander rather than of the composition. A small proportion does the work; a large one turns a formula into a single note. The exact proportion depends on the other materials and on the format, and no page can give a number that survives contact with a real recipe.

In the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) vocabulary, coriander seed reads as an assistant — 佐 — at most. It supports the chief material, adds a facet the chief lacks, and does not compete for attention. That reading is editorial judgment rather than a classical ruling; the old formulas were built around very different material sets, and importing the vocabulary into a modern blend is an analogy, not a citation.

What coriander seed does not do is carry a blend. It has no resinous weight, no deep wood base, and no long tail. A formula that relies on it for structure will smell thin once the top note lifts, which happens quickly.

## Heat, volatility, and the disappearing top note

**Direct answer:** The compounds that make coriander seed smell like coriander are volatile, and volatile compounds leave first when a blend is heated. On a burning coal the citrus lift is the earliest casualty; what remains is a duller, woodier trace. This is a property of the chemistry, not a defect in the material.

The chain is short and unforgiving: the fruit is ground or blended → surface area rises sharply → linalool and its companions evaporate and oxidise → the bright top note weakens → heat from the coal drives off what is left before it can be appreciated → the blend's opening flattens.

Grinding is the first step in that chain, and it happens before the incense is ever lit. A whole fruit protects its oil behind the fruit wall; a milled powder exposes every ruptured cell to air. That is why ground coriander smells wonderful for a week and dull for a year, while whole fruit in a sealed jar still has a future.

Heat accelerates the rest. In a smouldering blend, the front of the coal reaches temperatures far above anything the fruit experienced in storage, and the aromatic molecules at the surface are decomposed rather than gently evaporated. Some of that decomposition produces pleasant toasted notes; much of it produces the acrid, ashy character that makes badly built incense smell like burning paper.

The practical conclusion is not that coriander seed is unsuitable for incense — it appears in traditional blends — but that its contribution is brief. A formulator who wants the citrus note to survive has to protect it: keep the fruit whole until blending, add the ground spice late in the process, and accept that the opening will be the only place it shows.

## Grading at the bench

**Direct answer:** Coriander seed is graded on the crush, not on appearance alone. Sound fruit is round, dry, ridged, and strongly citrusy when broken; tired fruit is pale, dusty, and flat. There is no useful grade above "fresh and clean," because the material has no rarity tiers.

| Check | Sound fruit | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Round to slightly oval, two mericarps joined | Flattened, broken, or mostly split halves |
| Surface | Intact ridges, even pale brown to tan colour | Rubbed, bleached, or greyish surface; dust in the bag |
| Feel | Dry, hard, heavy for its size | Soft, light, or spongy fruit |
| Crush aroma | Immediate warm citrus, woody, faintly floral | Flat, dusty, hay-like, or musty |
| Cleanliness | Minimal stem, soil, or stones | Grit, stems, insect holes, webbing |
| Off-notes | None beyond the spice itself | Musty cellar note, paint-like sharpness, rancid edge |

The crush test is the centre of the method, and it needs a little discipline. Take a few fruits, press them between finger and thumb or under a spoon, and smell immediately — the oil is most legible in the first seconds. A good lot releases a smell that is recognisably citrus before it is recognisably spice. A poor lot releases dust.

Colour is a weak signal on its own. Fresh coriander fruit varies from pale tan to light brown depending on variety and drying, and a darker lot is not automatically older. What matters is uniformity and the absence of grey bloom, which usually points to damp storage. Uniformity matters because a mixed lot means mixed handling somewhere upstream.

Mustiness is the one defect that should end the evaluation. A cellar-like note means mould has been at work, and no amount of airing fixes a mouldy spice. The same applies to a sharp, solvent-like smell, which suggests contamination rather than age.

## Forms, and how each one ages

**Direct answer:** Coriander moves as whole fruit, as ground powder, and — separately — as fresh leaf. Whole fruit is the reference form and keeps longest; powder is convenient and short-lived; the leaf is a different product with a different shelf life and no incense use.

| Form | Aroma at its best | Keeps for | Best use | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole fruit | Full citrus-woody profile, released on crushing | Long, in a sealed jar away from heat | Blending, sachets, evaluation | Slow release until crushed |
| Cracked fruit | Stronger immediate lift, slightly shorter life | Moderate | Blends ground at home | Uneven particle size |
| Ground powder | Loudest initial aroma, easiest to disperse | Short | Immediate use, pastes, doughs | Rapid volatile loss, hides identity |
| Fresh leaf | Green, sharp, entirely different | Days | Cooking only | Spoilage; no incense role |

The whole-to-ground trade-off is the same one that governs every aromatic: surface area buys intensity and spends time. What makes coriander a clean example is that the fruit wall is a genuine barrier. Whole fruit can sit for a season and still open with a clear citrus note; the same fruit milled will be noticeably flatter within weeks.

For incense work, the practical sequence is to buy whole, store whole, and crush only what a batch needs. A small electric mill or a mortar does the job; the goal is a coarse, even grind rather than a flour, because a fine powder releases everything at once and clogs the blend.

The fresh leaf deserves its own line only to be excluded. It is a culinary herb, it spoils in days, and its aroma has no place in a blend. Including it in the same table is a reminder that "coriander" as a shopping word covers two products that share a plant and nothing else.

## Storage and the slow decline

**Direct answer:** Store coriander seed whole, sealed, cool, dark, and dry. The enemies are the usual four — heat, light, air, and moisture — and grinding is the event that converts a stable material into a fragile one. A well-stored jar of whole fruit holds its citrus note far longer than any powder.

The decline chain: whole fruit → sealed jar → low oxygen and stable humidity → oil stays in the ducts → aroma survives; open the jar repeatedly and the cycle reverses: fresh air in → oxygen attacks the surface oil → volatiles migrate out → the headspace fills with the aroma the spice has lost.

Temperature matters because it sets the rate. A jar above a stove runs the reaction faster than a jar in a cupboard. Light matters because the same oils that smell good are chemically reactive, and ultraviolet light accelerates their breakdown. Moisture matters because a damp spice can support mould long before it looks wrong.

There is one counterintuitive point about refrigeration. Cold storage slows chemistry, which sounds ideal, but a jar moved in and out of a fridge condenses water on the fruit each time it warms. For a material sold dry and used dry, a stable cool cupboard is usually the better answer than an interrupted cold chain.

The useful habit is small and repeated: keep the working portion in a small jar, keep the reserve sealed, and smell the working jar before each use. When the crush stops giving citrus, the material is finished for aromatic purposes even if it is still fine in a stew.

## A buying sequence for the fruit

**Direct answer:** Buy coriander seed by naming the part, checking the crush, and refusing lots that smell musty. The sequence is short because the material is a commodity: there is no vintage to chase and no rarity to pay for. What separates a good lot from a bad one is freshness and cleanliness.

| Step | Action | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Name the part | Specify 芫荽籽, the dried fruit — not 芫荽, not the leaf | The single most common specification error |
| 2. Ask the form | Whole fruit unless a batch needs powder now | Powder sold as a default shortens the buyer's shelf life |
| 3. Crush and smell | Break several fruits and smell within seconds | Flat lots, musty lots, and admixture of caraway or fennel |
| 4. Look for cleanliness | Stem fragments, stones, dust, insect holes | Poor handling upstream; a sieving problem, not a scent problem |
| 5. Check uniformity | Even colour and size across the sample | Mixed lots that blend two harvests or two sources |
| 6. Buy small and often | Order to the season's use rather than to a discount tier | Stale stock at the back of the cupboard |

Step six is an editorial recommendation rather than a rule of the trade, and it follows from the material's economics. Coriander is a high-volume field crop with no scarcity premium to capture, so a year's supply bought at once saves little and costs freshness. The freshness lost in long storage outweighs any saving on the invoice.

Step three is where most of the value sits. Admixture is the quiet failure: a lot with a noticeable caraway or fennel fraction will look plausible in a bowl and smell wrong only when crushed. Because the fruits are harvested and bagged in similar conditions, the mixing happens before the buyer ever sees the sack. The crush test is the only cheap defence.

There is a limit to what a buyer can check without a laboratory, and it is worth stating plainly. Appearance and aroma will catch stale stock, mould, and gross admixture. They will not catch pesticide residue, heavy metals, or a mislabelled species that happens to smell close. For those, the answer is documentation and testing, not a better nose.

## Substitution: what to reach for instead

**Direct answer:** Coriander seed has no true substitute, only neighbouring materials that move a blend in a similar direction. Fennel adds a sweeter licorice note, orange peel adds a juicier citrus, cumin adds earth and weight. The choice depends on which part of coriander's character the formula actually needs.

| Stand-in | What it shares | What it adds | What it loses | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fennel | Umbellifer sweetness; a citrus-adjacent lift | Anethole — a rounder, licorice-like sweetness | The dry woody edge; the fine citrus top | The blend wants sweetness rather than lift |
| Orange peel | Citrus brightness | Juicier, more fruity, more volatile | The woody, spicy base | The top note is the point |
| Cumin | Warm spice weight | Earthy, heavier, slightly smoky | The clean citrus character entirely | The blend needs depth, not lift |
| Dill seed | Green, sharp, umbellifer family | A grassier, more angular top | The round sweetness | A greener, more herbaceous profile is wanted |
| Anise | Sweet spice, similar family | Stronger, sweeter, more liquorice | The subtlety; anise dominates easily | The formula is sweet-spice led |

The matrix is a decision aid, not a licence to swap freely. Each stand-in changes the balance of the blend, and a substitution that solves one problem usually creates another. Fennel, for instance, will cover for coriander's sweetness but not for its dryness, and a blend built on fennel reads sweeter and heavier than the same blend built on coriander.

There is a practical rule underneath the table: substitute by function, not by name. Ask what the coriander was doing — opening the blend, bridging the spices, adding a dry citrus edge — and then choose the material that does that job. A name-for-name swap is how blends end up unbalanced.

For buyers, the matrix also explains why substitution is a decision about function rather than about price. Each stand-in has its own supply chain and its own cost, and none of those costs tells you whether the material belongs in the blend. Fennel, cumin, and anise can each cost more or less than coriander depending on the season and the origin; the question that matters is which job the formula needs done. Treating the cheapest option as the safe option is how a blend loses the note it was built around.

## Telling the umbellifer seeds apart

**Direct answer:** Coriander seed shares a family, a shape, and a shelf with several other umbellifer spices, and the dried fruits are easy to mix up at a glance. Caraway is crescent-shaped and smells of rye; fennel is longer and greener with a licorice note; cumin is longer, darker, and earthier. The nose settles in seconds what the eye cannot.

| Spice | Shape and colour | Dominant smell | Kitchen signature | Incense temperament |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coriander | Round, ridged, pale tan | Warm citrus and wood | Blends, pickles, baking | Bright, brief top note |
| Caraway | Crescent, darker brown | Sharp, rye-like, slightly minty | Bread, cabbage dishes | Pungent, short-lived |
| Fennel | Longer, greener, ridged | Sweet licorice | Sausage, fish, digestifs | Sweet, heavier, slower |
| Cumin | Long, dark, rough | Earthy, oily, warm | Curries, chili, stews | Heavy, base-leaning |
| Dill | Thin, light brown, winged | Green, sharp, grassy | Pickles, fish | Green, angular, brief |
| Anise | Small, pear-shaped, pale | Sweet licorice, strong | Baking, liqueurs | Sweet, dominating |

The confusion has a commercial edge. A lot of "coriander" sold in bulk markets can contain caraway or fennel admixture, because the fruits are harvested, dried, and bagged in similar ways and the visual difference is modest once the bag is open. A buyer who checks only colour and size will miss it; a buyer who crushes a few fruits will not.

Caraway is the most instructive comparison, because it is the one most often mistaken for coriander in a kitchen. Its aroma is dominated by carvone rather than linalool, which gives it a rye-bread sharpness that coriander never has. Once you have smelled both side by side, the difference is unmistakable — and that is the argument for building a reference set rather than relying on descriptions.

Fennel is the other near neighbour, and the difference is one of weight. Fennel's anethole is sweeter and slower; coriander's linalool is drier and faster. In a blend, fennel sits lower and lasts longer. Substituting one for the other changes not just the smell but the timing of the blend.

Cumin is easy to separate by smell — earthy and oily rather than citrus — but its dark colour also makes it the one that visibly contaminates a pale lot. Dill and anise are less common in incense work but appear in spice blends, and both have distinctive shapes that survive a visual check.

## The traditional record

**Direct answer:** Chinese sources record 芫荽, also 胡荽, as a culinary herb and aromatic in the 本草綱目 菜部. That is a historical record of use, not evidence of efficacy, and this page treats it as tradition. The kitchen role is well documented; the medicinal language is not a clinical claim.

| Layer | What it covers | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Culinary record | 芫荽 / 胡荽 as a herb and spice in Chinese cooking | Well documented |
| Materia medica | The 本草綱目 菜部 entry treating the plant as an aromatic | Historically documented |
| Traditional belief | Medicinal uses attributed to the plant in the classical literature | Traditional belief; no clinical claim |
| Chemistry | Linalool as the dominant aromatic compound in the fruit | Established |
| Incense use | The fruit as a fresh spice accent in some 合香 blends | Documented practice, limited role |

The 菜部 placement is worth noticing. It files coriander with vegetables and kitchen herbs rather than with the aromatics of the 草部 or the incense materials of the 香乘 tradition. That classification matches the material's actual standing: coriander is a food spice that occasionally crosses into scent work, not a canonical incense ingredient.

The medicinal language attached to the plant in later texts is best read the way one reads any historical pharmacology — as a record of what practitioners believed and prescribed, not as a result set. This page repeats none of it as advice. What it does repeat is the documentary fact: the plant was known, named, and used, and the names themselves carry history.

The 胡 prefix is part of that history. It marks the plant as an import, and it sits in the same naming layer as 胡椒 and 葫芦巴. A reader who learns to notice the prefix gains a small tool for reading the whole materia-medica vocabulary: names encode routes, not just species.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Coriander seed as a dried spice is a low-risk kitchen material, and the risks that matter here belong to incense and to concentrated oil, not to the fruit itself. Burning any incense produces smoke and particulate matter; coriander essential oil is a concentrate with its own handling rules.

- **Smoke is the main exposure.** Any smouldering material generates fine particles and carbon monoxide. Burn in a ventilated room, avoid inhaling directly from the plume, and do not treat a pleasant smell as evidence of clean air.
- **Keep blends away from children and pets.** Whole fruit looks like food; a sachet or a bowl of blending stock is a chewing hazard and a choking hazard.
- **Treat the essential oil as an industrial material.** It is far more concentrated than the spice, it oxidises in storage, and oxidised linalool is a recognised skin sensitiser. Store it sealed, dark, and cool, and follow the supplier's safety data.
- **Do not apply oil to skin neat.** Dilution and patch-testing are the ordinary precautions for any aromatic oil, and they are not optional.
- **Discard mouldy fruit.** A musty smell or visible bloom means the lot is spoiled; heat does not make it safe.
- **No medical use is implied.** Nothing on this page is a treatment, a dose, or a recommendation for internal use.

If you are pregnant, nursing, or managing a health condition, ask a clinician about any botanical before using it, and do not rely on an incense page for health guidance. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general rules on smoke and ventilation.

## FAQ

**What is coriander seed?** The dried ripe fruit of *Coriandrum sativum*, an annual umbellifer native to the Mediterranean and western Asia and cultivated across China. The spice trade calls the whole fruit a seed; botanically it is a schizocarp that usually holds two mericarps. It smells warm, citrusy, and woody, and it is used as a spice and as a fresh accent in some incense blends.

**Are coriander seed and cilantro the same thing?** One plant, two products, and the difference is the whole point. The fruit is warm, citrusy, and woody, with linalool as its main aromatic compound. The leaf is green, sharp, and polarising, with aldehydes that some people perceive as soapy. In the kitchen they are used differently, and in incense only the fruit has a role.

**What does coriander seed smell like?** Warm citrus first, then a dry woody note, with a mild spice prickle and a faint floral edge. The impression is closer to a peeled citrus rind dried in wood than to the green leaf. Crushing the fruit releases the smell; the intact fruit is comparatively quiet.

**Is coriander seed the same as caraway?** No. They are different species in the same family, and the aromas diverge sharply: caraway is dominated by carvone and reads as rye-like and slightly minty, while coriander is linalool-led and citrusy. The fruits also differ in shape — caraway is crescent-shaped, coriander is round and ridged.

**Why does cilantro taste soapy to some people?** The leaf's green aldehydes are perceived differently from person to person, and for a substantial minority the result reads as soap. That is a real sensory difference, not a failure of taste. The fruit does not carry the same aldehydes, which is why someone who dislikes cilantro may still enjoy ground coriander.

**Can I use ground coriander in a sachet?** You can, but it is the weakest form for that job. Powder releases its aroma quickly and then declines, and fine particles sift through loosely woven cloth. Whole or lightly cracked fruit gives a slower, longer release, and a lined pouch is the better container if powder is the only form available.

**How should coriander seed be stored?** Whole, sealed, and away from heat and light. The fruit wall protects the oil, so intact fruit keeps far longer than powder. Smell a crushed sample before each use; when the citrus note is gone, the aromatic value is gone with it.

**Does heating coriander seed improve it?** Dry-toasting is a culinary technique that pushes the aroma toward nutty and reduces the citrus lift. That can be useful in cooking and is usually counterproductive in a blend where the citrus top note is the reason the spice was included. Heat moves the profile; it does not create more aroma.

## Key takeaways

- Coriander seed is the dried fruit of *Coriandrum sativum*, an annual umbellifer grown across China and native to the Mediterranean and western Asia.
- The "seed" is a schizocarp; its oil ducts hold a linalool-rich oil that the crush test releases.
- The leaf is a different aromatic with a different chemistry — green aldehydes, a soapy note for some people, and no incense role.
- In 合香 the fruit is a fresh spice accent, not a structural material; it opens a blend and fades early.
- Grinding and heat both spend the top note; buy whole, crush small, and blend late.
- Grade on crush aroma and cleanliness, not on colour alone; mustiness is a discard signal.
- 芫荽籽 is the unambiguous name to specify, and 胡荽 records the plant's western introduction.
- Medicinal language around the plant is traditional belief, not clinical evidence.

> **Direct answer:** 荔枝壳 (lìzhī ké) — litchi husk — is the dried wall of the fruit, the leathery red casing left once the aril has been eaten. It is one of the few fruit peels with a named role in the classical incense literature: the Ming compendium 香乘 preserves a 荔枝香 made from it and calls it the clearest of the peels. Treat it as a light, fruit-forward top-note ingredient, judged on condition rather than on grade.

Two facts organise everything else on this page. The first is that the material is a **byproduct**: the husk is what remains after a fruit grown to be eaten has been sold. The second is that it is a **top note**: light, volatile, and short-lived, which is why the classical record praises it for blending rather than for burning alone. A reader who holds those two facts together will make better sense of the quality signals, the storage advice, and the commercial reality than a reader who treats the husk as a cheap kind of wood.

## What the husk is, precisely

> **Direct answer:** The husk is the fruit wall — the pericarp — that surrounds the edible aril and the seed. It is thin, leathery, red when fresh, and rough with small protuberances; it is not a wood, a resin, a root, or a flower.

A litchi fruit has three parts that matter to a buyer. The outer wall is the pericarp, differentiated into a tough outer layer and a thinner inner membrane. Inside sits the aril, the translucent fleshy structure that people eat. At the centre is the seed. What incense uses is the outer wall, dried. The aril is food, the seed is a separate article in traditional pharmacy, and the wall holds the aromatic character described in the classical texts.

The red colour of a fresh husk comes from anthocyanins, the same broad family of pigments that colours berries and autumn leaves. Those pigments are unstable: they fade and brown as the husk dries and ages, partly through the action of polyphenol oxidase, the enzyme that turns a cut apple brown. This is a useful processing fact and a warning about quality signals. A brown husk is not automatically old or badly handled, and a red husk is not automatically fresh. Colour tells you something about pigment chemistry, not about the volatile fraction that produces the smell.

The physical structure explains the behaviour. A pericarp is thin, lightweight, and high in surface area relative to its mass. It has no dense resinous core to melt and no woody matrix to smoulder for a long time. When it burns, the flame finds a large surface quickly, which is why a peel reads as a bright, fast top note rather than as a base. That is a material property, not a matter of recipe, and it is the reason a formulator reaches for a binder or a denser companion material.

## Husk, peel, shell, pericarp: the words matter

> **Direct answer:** "Husk", "peel", "shell", and "pericarp" all point at the same tissue in everyday speech, but only one of them is precise. The botanical term is pericarp, the fruit wall; the culinary terms describe how it is used, not what it is.

English is loose here in a way that Chinese is not. A banana has a peel, a nut has a shell, a coconut has a husk, and a litchi has all three words applied to it by different writers. The tissue in each case is the fruit wall, and the differences in vocabulary come from kitchen habit rather than from anatomy. A buyer who reads "litchi shell" in a catalogue has not learned anything about the material; a buyer who reads "pericarp" has learned which part of the fruit is being sold.

The distinction has commercial consequences. "Peel" suggests a kitchen byproduct, which is accurate but invites the assumption that any fruit peel will do. "Husk" suggests something tougher and more protective, which fits the leathery litchi wall. "Shell" suggests a hard casing, which the litchi does not have. Suppliers choose the word that sounds most valuable, and the choice is a small signal of how carefully the material has been described.

Chinese is more disciplined because the part is built into the name. 壳 is the shell or husk, and 荔枝壳 cannot be mistaken for the flesh or the seed. That precision is worth carrying into English: when a specification matters, use "pericarp" once, then use the common word consistently. A contract that alternates between "peel", "husk", and "shell" is a contract with three possible materials in it.

## Litchi or lychee? A spelling note

> **Direct answer:** "Litchi" and "lychee" name the same fruit. Litchi is the older romanisation and the form used in botanical nomenclature; lychee is the common trade spelling in English. Neither spelling says anything about the material's quality.

The split is a transliteration accident, not a botanical distinction. Both forms descend from 荔枝, and both circulate in English-language trade. *Litchi chinensis* is the scientific name, so "litchi" appears in every flora and taxonomic database; "lychee" appears on menus, in supermarkets, and in most consumer writing. A buyer who searches for "lychee husk incense" and a buyer who searches for "litchi husk incense" are looking for the same thing.

The practical consequence is search behaviour. A supplier may list the material under one spelling and not the other, and a catalogue search that returns nothing under "lychee" may still have the goods under "litchi". For a buyer, the species name is the reliable key: *Litchi chinensis* is unambiguous in a way that either common spelling is not.

There is no quality hierarchy hidden in the spelling. A lot described as "lychee peel" is not a different grade from one described as "litchi pericarp"; the difference is marketing register. The precise description is the one that names the part and the species, not the one that chooses the more familiar spelling.

## The plant and where it comes from

> **Direct answer:** A soapberry-family evergreen, *Litchi chinensis* usually stays below 10 m but can exceed 15 m, bearing pinnate leaves in two to four pairs and a dark-red fruit 2–3.5 cm across. Its native ground is Hainan and the Xuwen district of Guangdong; from there cultivation spread through southern China, with Guangdong and southern Fujian becoming the classic growing regions.

Flora of China gives the botanical frame: an evergreen tree, usually below 10 m but capable of reaching 15 m or more, with greyish-black bark and leaves made up of two or three pairs of leaflets, occasionally four, each 6–15 cm long and 2–4 cm wide. The fruit is a dark-red globe, 2–3.5 cm in diameter, wrapped in its rough, leathery wall, the material that incense uses. Blooming comes in spring and the fruit ripens in summer, so the husk supply exists in a narrow window of the year.

Flora of China narrows the native range to Hainan and the Xuwen area of Guangdong. Orchards carried the species through southern China, and Guangdong and southern Fujian emerged as the classic producing regions. The litchi has been grown in China for more than a thousand years, and the fruit's place in southern Chinese food culture is old enough that the husk's second life as an aromatic is not a modern recycling idea. It is a recovery that the classical incense literature noticed and named.

That history matters for how the material is traded. A litchi orchard is not growing husk; it is growing fruit. The husk appears at harvest, in volume, and must be dealt with in the same few weeks as the fruit. A buyer is therefore not ordering from a dedicated aromatic crop but from the edge of a food supply chain, with all the seasonality and variability that implies. The Flora of China entry is the reference used for the description above: [Flora of China, *Litchi chinensis*](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200013205).

## The name 荔枝壳: a lesson in part precision

> **Direct answer:** 荔枝壳 says exactly which part is meant: 荔枝 is the litchi and 壳 means husk. The name points to the pericarp, not to the fruit flesh, the seed, or the tree — and that precision is the first quality check.

Chinese material names often encode the part, and the encoding is worth reading carefully. 荔枝肉 would be the flesh; 荔枝核 would be the seed; 荔枝壳 is the shell. A supplier who says "litchi" without specifying the part has said almost nothing about what will arrive. The difference is not academic: flesh is wet, sugary, and perishable, the seed is a different article with a different traditional use, and the shell is the only one of the three that is a plausible incense material.

Naming the part also protects against a common substitution. Dried fruit peels look alike to an untrained eye, and a buyer who orders "litchi peel" may receive a mix of peels from the same processing shed. Knowing that the order is specifically for the pericarp of *Litchi chinensis* gives the buyer a question to ask and a basis for rejecting a lot that contains flesh fragments, seeds, or another species.

There is a further distinction inside the husk itself. The outer surface is rough and red; the inner surface is smoother and paler. Both are part of the pericarp, and both are normally included in a dried whole husk. A lot that has been scraped down to the inner membrane may look cleaner and may even smell sweeter, but it has lost part of the tissue that carries the aroma. "Clean" is not the same as "complete".

## The classical record: 荔枝香 in the 香乘

> **Direct answer:** The 香乘, a Ming dynasty incense compendium compiled by 周嘉胄, records a 荔枝香 made from the husk and quotes an earlier 香谱 describing the husk as the peel that gave the clearest, most fragrant blend. The record is a blending judgment, not a medical claim.

The line preserved in the 香乘 is short: 取其壳合香最清馥 — "use its shell in a blend; the result is clearest and most fragrant." Two words in that sentence carry the whole case. 合香 names the blended-incense tradition, not solo burning. 清馥 is a judgment about clarity and lift, the quality a light peel contributes to a mixture. The text is praising a role, not ranking a raw material on a universal scale.

The attribution matters as much as the quote. The 香乘 is a compilation, and it preserves earlier material from the 香谱 tradition rather than inventing a recipe. That gives the reader a chain of transmission: an earlier incense manual described the use, and a later compendium thought it worth keeping. It does not give a modern supplier a license to claim a specific farm, a specific cultivar, or a specific formula. The classical record is evidence of use, not a certificate of origin.

What the record does not say is equally useful. It does not claim that litchi husk cures anything, that it should be burned alone, or that there is a grade ladder from common to imperial. Those are modern marketing additions. The historical claim is modest: a fruit peel, normally discarded, was found to make a clear and fragrant contribution to a blend. That is enough to justify the material's place in an encyclopedia, and it is all the justification the text provides.

## What it smells like

> **Direct answer:** The smell is fruit-forward and honeyed, with a soft floral edge and a mild woody shadow. It sits lighter on the nose than citrus peel and lacks the prick of spice, which is why it behaves as a top note rather than a base.

The first impression is sweetness without heaviness. Unlike a resin, the husk does not push a dense cloud of aroma; unlike a spice, it does not prick the nose. The fruit note is recognisably litchi-adjacent but drier and more diffuse, closer to a handful of dried fruit than to the fresh aril. Underneath the sweetness there is a faint floral lift and a soft woody shadow that keeps the note from becoming cloying.

That combination is why the material is described as "clear" in the classical language. A clear note is one that opens space in a blend rather than filling it. Litchi husk does that by adding a bright, sweet top edge that sits above denser materials without competing with them. The effect is easiest to hear when the husk is burned alongside a woody or resinous base: the base supplies weight, and the peel supplies the opening of the burn.

Describing the note to another person is its own skill. "Fruity" is too broad to be useful, because it covers everything from citrus to banana; "sweet" says nothing about weight; "floral" could mean a dozen different flowers. The useful vocabulary is relational: sweeter than citrus peel, lighter than a resin, with none of spice's prickle, closer to dried fruit than to the fresh aril. A formulator who can place the note against those references can brief a supplier or evaluate a sample without a laboratory.

Alone, the same material reads thin. A single peel burns fast, releases its volatiles in a short burst, and leaves little behind. That is not a defect in the material; it is a description of its role. A top note is supposed to be brief. The formulator's job is to decide what remains after the top has gone, and the husk's job is to make the opening worth attending to.

## Why a peel behaves differently from a wood or resin

> **Direct answer:** A peel is thin, low-density, and high in surface area, with a volatile aromatic fraction rather than a resinous or oily core. It ignites quickly, releases its scent early, and needs a binder or a denser companion to burn evenly in a formed incense.

The difference is structural before it is chemical. A sandalwood chip is dense; heat has to penetrate it, and the aromatic oils are held in a woody matrix that releases slowly. A resin grain melts and bubbles, feeding a steady stream of aroma. A dried peel is a thin sheet. Heat reaches the whole thickness almost at once, the volatile fraction leaves in a hurry, and the remaining tissue chars. That is why a peel-heavy stick can smell wonderful at the tip and empty halfway down.

The practical consequence is a formulation decision:

**Peel ground fine → surface area rises further → volatiles leave at a lower temperature → the top note flashes early → the middle of the burn reads thin → the formulator adds a denser material or a coarser cut to slow the release.** The chain runs in one direction, and no amount of extra peel fixes a structure that has no body behind it.

Low density also changes handling. A kilo of dried husk occupies far more volume than a kilo of wood chips, so packing, shipping, and storage costs are driven by bulk rather than weight. In a workshop, the material is light and dusty when ground, which means losses to the air and a need for gentle handling. None of this is a quality defect. It is the behaviour of a peel, and a buyer who expects wood-like behaviour will misjudge both the lot and the burn.

## Drying: the step that decides the material

> **Direct answer:** Drying is the whole process. The wall is pulled off the fruit, rinsed free of sugar, and dried until it snaps; how fast and how hot that drying runs determines how much of the sweet top note survives and whether the lot moulds.

The steps are simple enough to describe in a sentence: separate the husk from the aril and seed, wash off fruit residue, spread the pieces to dry, turn them so air reaches both faces, and store the brittle result in a dry container. Nothing is distilled, solvent-extracted, or pressed. The material is a dried peel, and its quality is decided at the drying rack.

The trade-off is real. Slow drying in shade or in moving air keeps the tissue cooler and preserves more of the volatile fraction, which is why a carefully dried husk smells brighter. Fast drying in strong sun or in a heated shed removes water quickly and reduces the window for mould, but it also drives off some of the molecules the buyer is paying for. Neither route is universally correct; the point is that the choice leaves a signature in the smell.

Moisture is the other half of the decision. A husk that is still flexible and leathery has not reached a safe storage state. Stacked while damp, it heats up in the pile:

**Wet husk → sugar and moisture in the tissue → a warm, dense pile → microbial growth and fermentation → sour, musty, or alcoholic off-notes → a lot that no blend can rescue.** This is the most common way a cheap raw material becomes an unusable one, and it is invisible in a photograph.

The colour changes during the same window. Anthocyanins fade and browning enzymes act, so a dried lot ranges from reddish-brown to dark brown. That is normal. What is not normal is a sour smell, visible mould, or a surface that feels damp against the lip of the jar. Those are stop signals, not cosmetic issues.

## The freshness clock: three things that fade at different speeds

> **Direct answer:** A dried husk carries three clocks — moisture, volatiles, and pigment — and they run at different rates. Moisture is measured in hours, aroma in months, colour in weeks to seasons, which is why colour is a poor proxy for smell.

The moisture clock starts the moment the fruit is opened. A fresh pericarp is wet, sugary, and warm from the orchard, and a pile of it is a microbial habitat. Drying has to begin the same day; a delay of a day in hot weather can decide whether a lot dries clean or ferments in the pile. This is the clock that decides whether the material exists at all, and it is the one a buyer never sees.

The volatile clock starts when the husk is dry. From that point the aromatic fraction declines slowly, and the rate depends on heat, air movement, and light. A sealed jar in a dark cupboard slows the decline; an open basket near a window accelerates it. This is why "aged" is not a compliment for a peel. A two-year-old husk may be perfectly sound tissue, but it is tired aroma, and no blending trick restores what has evaporated.

The pigment clock is the most visible and the least useful. Anthocyanins fade and brown over weeks to seasons, and the browning enzymes keep working slowly even in storage. A dark brown husk can smell fresh, and a reddish one can be stale. Colour is a record of pigment chemistry, not a freshness certificate.

Holding the three clocks apart changes how a lot is judged. Moisture is a snap test and a squeeze; aroma is a warm-hand test; colour is context, not evidence. A buyer who ranks lots by colour is reading the slowest and least relevant clock.

## Why peel materials stayed a minor register

> **Direct answer:** Fruit peels are a minor register in classical incense because they are light, seasonal, and short-lived, while the canon prizes materials that store well and burn slowly. The peel's weakness is also its use: it does what a dense material cannot.

A canon is shaped by what a material can do over time. Woods and resins keep for years, travel well, and hold their character through storage; a peel does none of those things. It appears for a few weeks, loses its aroma within a season or two, and crushes easily in transit. Those are not qualities a trading tradition rewards. The fact that a few peels still earned named places is a comment on how distinctive their contribution must have been.

The contribution is an effect of contrast. A dense base supplies weight and duration but can read heavy or closed at the opening. A light peel supplies the first minute of a burn, the part that tells the nose what kind of experience is beginning. Without a top note, a blend can feel like it starts in the middle. That is why a small amount of peel does a job that a larger amount of wood cannot do: it changes the shape of the experience rather than the substance of it.

The category is also small because the raw material is inconvenient. A peel must be dried quickly, stored dry, and used within a reasonable window, and none of those requirements fits a trade that prefers durable goods. A producer who has a fruit business already has the husk; a producer who does not has to source a perishable byproduct and build a drying operation around a short season. The barrier is not secrecy or rarity. It is logistics.

That combination — real sensory value, poor logistics, seasonal supply — explains the peels' position: respected in the recipe record, minor in the market, and easy to misdescribe. Anyone who has internalised the logistics will ask better questions than one who has been told a romantic story about an ancient fruit.

## Forms and how form changes the burn

> **Direct answer:** The material reaches the market in three forms: whole, coarse-cut, or powdered. Whole husk stores best and burns slowest; coarse cut is the blending compromise; powder releases fastest and is the easiest to over-dose.

| Form | Release | Burn behaviour | Storage | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried husk | Slowest | Burns unevenly, often needs a binder | Best — least exposed surface | Small-batch blending; visual inspection |
| Coarse cut | Moderate | More even than whole; still needs support | Good | General 合香 work |
| Powder | Fastest | Flashes at the top; can smother a smoulder | Poorest — absorbs moisture and odours | Incense powders and formed sticks |

Whole husk has one advantage no other form matches: it can be opened. Break a piece and look at the inner face. A pale, clean surface with no webbing or dark patches is a good sign; a fuzzy edge or a sour smell at the break is not. Powder hides all of that, which is why a buyer who cannot inspect whole material is buying on trust. A workshop that uses powder should keep a whole-husk reference sample from the same lot, so the grist can be checked against the material it came from.

The table is a set of trade-offs rather than a ranking. Whole husk is the easiest to inspect and the hardest to burn neatly. Powder is the easiest to dose by weight and the easiest to ruin by grinding too fine. Coarse cut sits between the two and is the form most likely to end up in a working blend.

Grind size is also a dose-control decision. A fine powder distributes evenly through a mixture, which is good for consistency and bad for restraint: the same weight of powder presents far more surface than the same weight of torn husk, so the aromatic hit is stronger and shorter. A formulator who changes the grind without changing the weight has changed the formula, even though the recipe looks identical on paper.

There is no universally correct ratio of husk to binder, and no classical figure to quote. The material is an accent, and the right amount depends on the base, the intended burn time, and the form. What can be said with confidence is that the husk should not be the structural backbone of a formed incense; it does not have the density or the binding behaviour for that job.

## Quality screening before you buy

> **Direct answer:** Screen for identity, aroma, moisture, and cleanliness, in that order. A clean, dry, unbroken husk with a honeyed fruit smell when gently warmed passes; a musty, damp, or crumbly lot fails regardless of how it looks.

| Check | Pass signal | Fail signal | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Part and species | Intact pericarp pieces with the rough outer face | Flesh fragments, seeds, or smooth unfamiliar peel | Confirms 荔枝壳, not fruit trimmings or another species |
| Aroma, cold | Faint sweet dried-fruit smell, no off-note | Sour, musty, or cook-like smells | Off-notes survive into the burn |
| Aroma, warmed | Honeyed fruit with a floral lift | Flat, hay-like, or acrid | Shows how much volatile fraction remains |
| Moisture | Brittle; snaps when bent | Leathery, pliable, damp at the jar lip | Damp material moulds in storage |
| Cleanliness | No grit, sand, or foreign matter | Visible soil or debris | Poor washing cannot be fixed later |
| Colour | Uneven red-brown to brown | Uniformly pale or patchy grey | Colour is a weak signal, but grey suggests mould |
| Storage history | Neutral smell, dry container | Smells of the warehouse or of another cargo | Peels absorb odours readily |

The order matters. Identity first, because a beautiful lot of the wrong material is still the wrong material. Aroma second, because the nose is faster than any certificate at detecting mould or volatile loss. Moisture third, because a damp lot is a future failure even if it smells fine today. Cleanliness last, because it is the easiest to see and the easiest to over-weight.

A buyer can run most of this check with a sample, a warm palm, and a few minutes. Break a piece: a dry husk snaps, a damp one bends. Warm a fragment in the hand and smell it: the volatile fraction shows up quickly. Look at the broken edge: grit and soil collect in the folds. Ask for the harvest year and the drying method; a supplier who knows the material will have an answer, and a supplier who does not will change the subject.

## Sourcing questions that discriminate

> **Direct answer:** Ask about harvest year, drying method, storage, and purity of part. The answers discriminate far more than a certificate, because they reveal whether the supplier handles the material or merely resells it.

A short list of questions covers most of the risk:

1. **Which year's fruit?** A peel is not a wine. The current or most recent harvest is the default expectation, and an evasive answer is itself information.
2. **How was it dried — sun, shade, or heated?** The method predicts volatile retention and mould risk, and a supplier who dries in-house will know the answer without checking.
3. **How has it been stored since drying?** Sealed, dark, and dry is the good answer. "In the warehouse" is not an answer.
4. **Is the lot pure litchi pericarp?** Ask directly about longan husk, because the two are close relatives and easily mixed.
5. **Whole, cut, or ground?** The form changes the dose and the storage life, and a supplier who offers only one form may be repacking rather than producing.
6. **Can you send a sample with a lot reference?** A lot reference is the minimum traceability for a material with no grading ladder.

The pattern in the answers matters more than any single reply. Specific, boring answers — a month, a drying method, a container type — signal a handler. Poetic answers about ancient trees and mountain mist signal a marketer. Neither answer proves quality, but only one of them gives a buyer something to verify.

## The confusion set: litchi, longan, chenpi, pomelo

> **Direct answer:** Litchi husk is easily confused with longan husk, and loosely grouped with aged mandarin peel and pomelo peel. Each is a separate species with its own aromatic register, and none is a drop-in substitute for another.

| Material | Family | Part | Aroma register | Substitute for litchi husk? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Litchi husk (荔枝壳) | Sapindaceae | Pericarp | Honeyed fruit, floral, light-bodied | The reference material |
| Longan husk (龙眼壳) | Sapindaceae | Pericarp | Darker, honeyed, heavier, less floral | No — same family, different weight |
| Tangerine peel (陈皮) | Rutaceae | Aged citrus peel | Rounded, mellow, bittersweet-warm | No — moves the blend from fresh to aged |
| Pomelo peel (柚皮) | Rutaceae | Citrus peel with pith | Bitter-sweet, floral and woody, thick-pith | No — pith adds bitterness and changes the burn |

The longan row is the dangerous one, because the two fruits are close relatives and the dried husks look similar in a sack. Litchi husk has a rougher, more tuberculate outer face; longan husk is smoother and leatherier. The smells diverge more clearly than the shapes: longan reads darker and more honeyed, with less of the bright floral lift that makes litchi husk useful as a top note. A mixed lot is not a disaster in a rustic blend, but it is a misdescribed lot.

The citrus rows are a different kind of confusion. 陈皮 and 柚皮 belong to the rue family, not the soapberry family, and their aromatic logic is built on aged peel and bitter pith rather than on fresh fruit. A formulator who reaches for 陈皮 because it is "also a fruit peel" has changed the register of the blend. The result may be pleasant, but it is no longer the formula that was designed.

The citrus side has separate entries: [pomelo rind (柚皮)](/ingredients/pomelo-peel/) and [aged mandarin peel (陈皮)](/ingredients/orange-peel/). A floral-fruity note from a different register is [osmanthus](/ingredients/osmanthus/).

## Blending role: where it sits in 君臣佐使

> **Direct answer:** In the 君臣佐使 grammar of 合香, litchi husk behaves as an assistant or guide rather than as a chief material. It brightens and lifts a formula; it cannot carry one, and treating it as the chief produces a thin, short-lived blend.

The classical hierarchy is a way of talking about function, not a ranking of worth. A 君 (chief) material sets the character of the blend; 臣 (minister) materials support and extend it; 佐 (assistant) materials modify or balance it; 使 (guide) materials carry and harmonise. A light fruit peel belongs in the assistant or guide slot because its contribution is an effect rather than a foundation. It makes the chief smell more like itself, and then it leaves.

That role has practical consequences. If the brief is a fresh, fruit-forward opening, the husk can take a larger share of the top note, supported by a denser material underneath. If the brief is a warm woody blend, the husk should be a trace — enough to lift the opening, not enough to announce fruit. The mistake in both cases is the same: adding husk to fix a weak structure. A top note cannot supply body, and a peel cannot rescue a base that was never there.

Pairing follows the same logic. The husk sits naturally with florals that need a fresh edge, with woods that need a brighter opening, and with resins that need a little air before they settle. It sits less naturally with heavily spiced formulas, where its sweetness can read as confusion, and with very smoky bases, where its delicacy is simply buried. These are tendencies, not rules; the only reliable test is a small trial burn.

## Troubleshooting a blend that smells wrong

> **Direct answer:** Most litchi-husk failures come down to four causes: too much husk, too fine a grind, damp material, or a base that cannot support a top note. Diagnose by symptom before changing the recipe.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| The fruit note vanishes in minutes | Powder too fine, or husk dosed too high | Coarser cut, lower dose, denser base |
| Smoke is acrid and scratches the throat | Damp peel, or too much binder | Dry the lot, reduce binder, cure the formed incense |
| A musty, cellar-like smell | Mould or odour transfer in storage | Discard the lot; change container and location |
| Sweet but flat, with no lift | Old husk or overheated drying | Fresher lot; add a brighter floral accent |
| The blend smells like fruit jam | Husk overdosed relative to the base | Cut the husk, rebuild the top note |
| One side burns faster, the stick tunnels | Uneven particle size or poor mixing | Grind uniformly, sieve, remix |

The table is a diagnostic order, not a recipe. Start with the cheapest change — dose — then the form, then the material itself. A taster who changes three variables at once learns nothing, because the result cannot be attributed. Keep a control stick from the previous batch, change one thing, and record the lot and the change together. That is ordinary workshop discipline, and it is what turns a variable byproduct into a predictable ingredient.

One failure deserves a separate note because it is not the husk's fault. A formula with no middle and no base will lose its top note no matter how much peel is added. The symptom looks like "the litchi husk is weak", and the cause is a structure that asked a top note to do a base's work. More husk makes the opening louder and the finish emptier.

## What the records do and do not show

> **Direct answer:** The historical recipe literature documents the incense use; the medicinal record for litchi belongs to traditional belief, not clinical evidence. Species identity is confirmed, and this page asserts no health claims.

The line between the second and third rows is the one readers blur most often. A plant can have a recorded incense use and a traditional medicinal use, and the two records have different evidentiary weight. The incense record is a statement about practice: people made 荔枝香, and a compendium preserved the fact. The materia medica record is a statement about a tradition of use, which is not the same as a demonstrated effect.

That is why this page describes the material's role and its handling without repeating medicinal claims. The classical texts are worth reading as history and as craft documentation. They are not a source of dosage advice, and they do not turn a fruit peel into a remedy. Readers who want the general safety picture for combustion should see the [safety guide](/safety/).

## Safety and storage

> **Direct answer:** Burning incense releases smoke and fine particles, so use a room with moving air and keep your face out of the plume. Discard mouldy or damp husk, store the rest dry and dark, and keep all aromatic materials out of reach of children and animals.

The safety issues for litchi husk are ordinary ones, and they are easier to manage than the exotic risks. Smoke is the first: any burning aromatic produces fine particles, and a small room with a smouldering stick concentrates them. Ventilation and distance matter more than the material.

Mould is the second. A peel that was dried badly or stored damp can carry mould, and burning mouldy material is a bad idea even if the smell seems to improve. The visible signs are grey patches, a musty smell, or a surface that feels clammy. The right response is to discard the lot, not to sun it and hope.

Storage is the third. Dried peels are hygroscopic and they absorb odours, so a sealed container in a dark cupboard is better than a pretty jar on a windowsill. Heat drives off the volatiles the material is valued for, and light accelerates the fading of the pigments. A cool, dry, dark place slows both processes.

The container matters less than the headspace. A tin with a tight lid beats a decorative jar with a loose cork, and a full container beats a half-empty one because every opening exchanges dry air for humid air. Label the container with the lot and the drying date; a peel that has sat unlabelled for three years is not improved by becoming mysterious.

The fourth is household access. Dried peels look like food, and a child or a pet may treat them as such. Keep the working stock out of reach, and do not store aromatic materials in unlabelled food containers. None of this is specific to litchi husk; it is the baseline discipline of a workshop that handles plant material.

## The byproduct economy

> **Direct answer:** Litchi husk is a seasonal byproduct of the fruit trade, produced in volume for a few weeks and traded on condition rather than grade. Its unit value is low, its variability is high, and freshness is the specification that matters.

The supply chain starts with fruit. Litchi is harvested in summer, sold fresh or processed, and the wall is stripped off wherever the fruit is eaten or processed. That means the raw material appears in a short window, in the same places where fruit is handled, and in quantities set by the fruit crop rather than by incense demand. A bad fruit year is a bad husk year, and no aromatic buyer can do anything about it.

The economics follow from that. A byproduct has low unit value, which means there is little incentive to invest in careful drying, clean storage, or detailed grading. The best lots come from producers who understand that a little care at the drying stage protects the value of the whole batch, and those producers are not the cheapest. A buyer who optimises only on price is buying the variance.

There is no published grading ladder for litchi husk, and this page will not invent one. What exists is a set of informal signals — freshness, cleanness, intactness, aroma on warming — that experienced buyers use. The absence of grades is not a gap in the market that a supplier can fill with a label; it is a reason to inspect the actual lot.

For a small workshop, the practical answer is to buy in quantities that can be used within a season, store them well, and re-inspect before each use. For a larger buyer, the answer is to build a relationship with a supplier who can describe the drying method and the storage history, and to treat each lot as its own specification. Neither answer requires a certificate, and neither is served by a romantic story about ancient orchards.

## Editorial view: a byproduct that earns its place

> **Direct answer:** Litchi husk is interesting precisely because it is not rare. It is a discarded peel that the classical record noticed, one whose worth comes from freshness and judgment rather than from scarcity — and that makes it a useful corrective to the rarity story that dominates aromatic marketing.

Three editorial judgments follow.

**Recovery is a form of craft.** The classical canon is full of resins, woods, and cut flowers that are harvested on purpose. A fruit peel is different: it is what the food chain leaves behind, and someone had to notice that it made a clear, sweet incense. That noticing is a small act of material intelligence, and it deserves more respect than a "byproduct" label usually gets.

**Condition is the specification that matters.** A resin keeps for years and a dense wood may improve with age. A peel cannot. Its aromatic fraction is volatile, its tissue is hygroscopic, and its colour is unstable. What separates a sound lot from a poor one is mostly how soon after harvest it was dried, and how well it has been kept since. That makes the material a test of a buyer's discipline rather than a test of a supplier's mystique.

**A light material should be judged by light-material standards.** Judging litchi husk by density, resin content, or age is a category error. It has none of those things, and it is not trying to. The right questions are about lift, clarity, and freshness — the qualities the classical text praised — and a buyer who asks those questions will get a better result than one who asks whether the husk is "old" or "thick".

Our reading is that litchi husk exposes a corner of the trade that modern sourcing rarely describes well: incense made from fruit peel. It is cheap, seasonal, variable, and genuinely classical. Its value lies in honesty about all four.

## Summary

荔枝壳 is the dried wall of the fruit of *Litchi chinensis*, a soapberry-family tree that originated in Hainan and the Xuwen district of Guangdong, then spread through southern China's orchards over more than a millennium. The 香乘 preserves a 荔枝香 made from the husk and quotes an earlier 香谱 calling it the clearest of the peel materials — a judgment about its role in 合香, not a claim about rarity. The note is honeyed and fruit-forward with a floral edge, a short-lived top accent that belongs in a blend; what separates a good lot from a poor one is freshness, clean handling, and competent drying, not a grade. A byproduct, it earned a name in the classical record, and it should be judged on condition.

## FAQ

**What is meant by litchi husk incense?** The material is the dried outer wall of the fruit of *Litchi chinensis*, taken off the flesh and seed, rinsed, and dried. It is then used whole or milled into a blend. The 香乘 preserves a 荔枝香 made this way and quotes an earlier 香谱 praising the husk as the clearest and most fragrant of the peel materials.

**How should a buyer describe the smell?** Honeyed fruit comes first, then a soft floral lift and a mild woody shadow. The note carries less weight than citrus peel and none of spice's prickle, and it behaves as a top note: bright at the opening, quiet by the middle of the burn. The dried-fruit character is closer to a handful of dried litchi than to the fresh aril.

**Is litchi husk the same as longan husk?** No. Both are Sapindaceae pericarps, and dried pieces can look similar, but longan husk is smoother and smells darker, more honeyed, and less floral. Litchi husk has a rougher outer face and a brighter top note. They are related materials, not swaps for one another.

**Can I make litchi husk incense from fresh fruit?** You can dry the husk from fruit you eat, but the result will be a home-dried peel of unknown moisture and variable aroma. Separate the husk from the flesh, wash off the sugar, dry it until it snaps, and store it dry. Do not expect the intensity of a properly dried lot; freshness of drying matters more than freshness of fruit.

**Why does litchi husk usually go into a blend?** Because it is a top note with little body. Burned alone, it releases a sweet burst and then fades, leaving a thin, short burn. In a blend it lifts the opening and brightens denser materials, which is exactly the role the classical text praises. Using it as the chief material produces a blend that opens well and finishes empty.

**Is litchi husk a fruit peel or a wood?** It is a fruit peel — the pericarp, the wall of the fruit. It is not a wood, a resin, a root, or a flower. That matters for how it burns, how it stores, and how it should be judged: by freshness and lift rather than by density or age.

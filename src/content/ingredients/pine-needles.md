> **Direct answer:** Crush a pinch of dried pine foliage and the air turns to a cold forest: green sap, resin, and the edge of winter. That scent belongs to 松针 (sōngzhēn), the leaf of a *Pinus* tree. It reaches the market as whole dried needles, serves mainly in cloth sachets, and is constantly mistaken for the resin of the same species.

## Key facts

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Chinese name | 松针 (sōngzhēn), literally "pine needle"; the tree is 松 (sōng) |
| Botanical source | *Pinus* species, family Pinaceae — the leaves, not the wood or resin |
| Part | Needles, ideally whole and freshly dried |
| Aroma | Piney, conifer, fresh, green, with a resinous edge |
| Traditional standing | A 香囊 (xiāngnáng) sachet herb, with a separate materia-medica record |
| Same tree, other parts | 松香 / 松脂 (resin), 松节 (node), 松实 / 松子 (seed), 松心 (heartwood) |
| Failure mode | Stale, musty, or heat-damaged needles that have lost the green top note |

Those rows are almost boring, which is exactly the point. A leaf, a low price, and one dominant risk — freshness. Difficulty arrives only when the single word "pine" is made to stand for several materials at once.

## What the name 松针 fixes

> **Direct answer:** 松针 is a part-name: 松 (pine) plus 针 (needle), the needle-shaped leaf. That precision is the most useful thing about the term, because the same tree supplies resin, wood, seeds, and pollen under different names and with different aromatic behaviour.

Chinese material names often work by specifying the part, and this one does exactly that. A supplier who is asked for 松 could reasonably send resin, wood, or needles. A supplier asked for 松针 has been given a much narrower instruction. The same logic runs through the materia medica, where the pine is entered once as a tree and its parts are then described separately.

| Part name | English | What it is | Aromatic behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 松针 / 松叶 | Pine needle | The evergreen leaf | Green, sharp top note; volatile; short-lived |
| 松香 / 松脂 | Pine resin | Oleoresin exuded by the tree, refined as rosin | Warm, balsamic, tenacious; base note |
| 松节 | Pine node | Knotty wood sections | Mild, woody, faintly resinous; structural or fuel use |
| 松心 | Pine heartwood | The durable inner wood | Dry woodiness; negligible top note |
| 松实 / 松子 | Pine seed | The edible seed | Nutty, oily; a food, not an incense note |

Two rows in that table are worth a second look. The first is 松节, which is wood rather than an aromatic in the perfumer's sense — it appears in the classical record as a durable material, and its presence in the same entry is a reminder that old materia medica catalogued the whole useful tree rather than a shelf of incense ingredients. The second is 松实, the seed: it is food, and treating it as an aromatic because it comes from the same tree would be a category error.

Calling this pedantry misses the commercial point. "Pine" is generic in every language, and a single shipment from one forest can contain resin, needle, and wood. The part-name is the only line in the order that actually specifies what will be delivered.

## A leaf at the top, an exudate at the base

> **Direct answer:** 松针 and 松香 are the same tree's two aromatic extremes: the needle is a light, volatile, green top note, and the resin is a heavy, warm, tenacious base. They are not interchangeable and they are not substitutes.

The contrast is the clearest example in this encyclopedia of a principle worth carrying into any formula: botanical identity does not determine aromatic function; the part does.

fresh needles on the branch → resin ducts in the leaf tissue hold light, volatile terpenes → those molecules evaporate readily at room temperature → the needle reads as bright and green → the impression fades within weeks of drying.

resin tapped from the trunk → heavier, less volatile acids and alcohols dominate the exudate → the material gives up its aroma slowly and at higher temperatures → the resin reads as warm and balsamic → the impression deepens with heat and lasts.

The two chains start from the same species and end in opposite places. A blender who wants the fresh forest opening reaches for the needle; a blender who wants the lingering base reaches for the resin. Using one where the other belongs produces a formula that is either hollow at the top or muddy at the base.

| Property | 松针 (needle) | 松香 (resin) |
|---|---|---|
| Volatility | High | Low |
| Position in a blend | Top note | Base note |
| Heat response | Loses character fast | Opens and deepens |
| Cost profile | Low, renewable leaf crop | Higher, slower-forming exudate |
| Typical form | Dried whole needle, ground foliage | Lumps, powder, refined rosin |
| Failure mode | Staleness and must | Overheating and scorching |

The cost rows are not incidental. A leaf that regrows every season is a different kind of commodity from an exudate that accumulates over years, and the price difference shapes how each material is handled. Needles are gathered in bulk and judged on freshness because the raw material is abundant; resin is graded, cleaned, and blended with more care because each kilogram represents a longer biological investment.

## A by-product of forestry, not a planted crop

> **Direct answer:** Pine needles are a by-product of forestry and pruning rather than a purpose-grown crop, which is why supply is abundant, cheap, and uneven in quality. The economics explain both the material's virtues and its variability.

A pine plantation is not managed for its needles. The trees are grown for timber, pulp, or resin, and the foliage is what gets left when a branch is cut or a stand is thinned. In parts of China, needles are also raked from the ground under planted pines, a practice that produces a mixed lot — older brown needles, twigs, soil, and whatever else the rake collects.

This origin shapes the trade in three ways. Supply is seasonal, following pruning and thinning schedules rather than demand. Quality is inconsistent, because the material is a by-product that no one is paid to handle delicately. And the price stays low, which caps how much cleaning and sorting any lot can economically receive.

None of that makes pine needles a bad material. It makes them a material to inspect rather than to assume. A well-gathered, well-dried lot from a careful supplier is excellent for sachets; a raked lot sold by weight is a gamble. The difference is visible in colour, smell, and the amount of brown needle and grit in the bag.

## The tree behind the leaf

> **Direct answer:** Pines are evergreen pioneers that keep several years of needles on the branch and defend themselves with resin. Both facts explain the material: the leaf is a long-lived but replaceable organ, and the resin that shares the tree is a wound response, not a constituent of the leaf.

A pine holds its foliage through winter, and individual needles live for several seasons before they brown and drop. That strategy is why a pine can be stripped of a few branches without harm, and why the fallen needle layer under a stand is thick enough to rake. It also explains the quality spread in gathered material: a bag collected from the ground mixes needles from different years, while a bag cut from living branches can be selected for the current season's growth.

The resin has a different origin. It is a defence, released when the bark is wounded, sealing the injury and carrying antimicrobial compounds into the breach. Tapping a tree exploits that response, which is why resin is a slower and more labour-intensive commodity than foliage. The needle contains resin ducts of its own, but the quantities are tiny compared with what flows from a tapped trunk.

A third detail is the fascicle sheath, the papery collar at the base of each needle bundle. It is a small thing with large practical value: it distinguishes a true pine from other evergreens at a glance, and it survives drying well enough to be checked in a purchased sample. For anyone handling the material, the sheath is the cheapest identification tool available.

## Which pine? The species question

> **Direct answer:** "Pine needle" is a leaf type shared by well over a hundred *Pinus* species, not a single botanical entity. For most aromatic purposes the genus is enough, but a buyer who cares about a specific character should ask which species and where it grew.

The genus spans the Northern Hemisphere and has a long list of Chinese representatives. In the south and centre, masson pine (*Pinus massoniana*) supplies both resin and needles in quantity. Elsewhere, Chinese white pine, Korean pine, Chinese red pine, and lacebark pine occupy different regions and altitudes, and every one of them sheds foliage that could, in principle, be collected.

The quickest field character is the number of needles in each fascicle, the little bundle that emerges from a papery sheath on the twig. It is not a full identification, but it sorts the genus into groups at a glance.

| Fascicle type | Examples in China | Needle character | Practical note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two needles per bundle | Masson pine (*P. massoniana*), Chinese red pine (*P. tabuliformis*) | Long, slender, often twisted | The common bulk material in southern and northern China respectively |
| Three needles per bundle | Lacebark pine (*P. bungeana*) | Medium, stiffer | Distinctive mottled bark makes the tree easy to recognise |
| Five needles per bundle | Chinese white pine (*P. armandii*), Korean pine (*P. koraiensis*) | Softer, often longer | Upland and northeastern species; softer foliage |

Two cautions belong with this table. First, bundle count is a sorting aid, not an identification: cone shape, bark, and location do the rest. Second, the table says nothing about which species smells best. Aroma varies with species, season, and drying, and the differences are subtle enough that a buyer is usually better served by judging the lot than by chasing a species name.

The species question matters most in one situation: when a supplier makes a specific claim. If a listing names a species, the name should be checkable against the needles in the box — bundle count, length, and stiffness are visible. If a listing says only "pine needle," that is an honest generic description, and the material should be judged as a generic green note.

## The scent: green, sharp, and short-lived

> **Direct answer:** The smell is a winter forest compressed into a small space — green, resinous, and clean, with a bright edge that cuts through heavier woods. That brightness is also the material's weakness: it is volatile, strongest when fresh, and steadily lost once the needle is dry.

The aroma has two layers. The first is the immediate, almost sharp greenness of crushed foliage, the smell of a branch snapped in cold air. The second is a softer resinous warmth underneath, which becomes more prominent as the sharp top fades. That second layer is the resin fraction making itself felt, and it explains why old needles smell more like weak resin than like fresh forest.

The volatile fraction is dominated by light terpenes, with pinene the molecule most people associate with the smell of pine. These compounds are small and mobile; they leave the tissue at room temperature, which is precisely why the material works in an open sachet and precisely why it does not last.

This is the second mechanism, and it governs most practical decisions about the material:

whole dried needle in an open sachet → air moves across the needle surface → light terpenes evaporate continuously → the headspace stays pleasantly piney for a while → the volatile reserve declines → the sachet goes flat, while the needle still looks unchanged.

The visible material outlives the aromatic material. That mismatch is the single most common disappointment with pine needles: a sachet that looks perfectly good but smells of nothing. The remedy is not more material but fresher material, or mechanical action — crushing a handful of needles releases a fresh burst from cells that were still intact.

## Harvest, drying, and the two ways to lose a lot

> **Direct answer:** Pine needles are gathered and air-dried, and the two failure modes are mould from a wet heap and volatiles lost to heat or long storage. Drying fast in thin layers preserves the green note; drying slowly in a pile destroys it.

Needles leave the tree with a high moisture content, and a heap of damp foliage is a small compost pile. The sequence is predictable:

damp needles stacked in a sack → microbial and plant enzymes generate heat → the interior of the mass stays wet → mould and fermentation proceed → the lot acquires a musty, swampy, sour note → the green top note is gone and cannot be recovered.

The alternative is thin layers, shade, and moving air. Heat speeds drying but drives off the very terpenes that make the material worth drying; strong sun also bleaches colour, turning green needles toward a dull olive-brown. The goal is a needle that is dry but still green, and those two objectives pull in opposite directions, which is why drying is the step where a good lot is most often ruined.

There is a harvest-side judgment too. Needles from the current season's growth are softer and carry the brightest scent; older needles on the same branch are tougher, more lignified, and duller. A gatherer who strips a branch indiscriminately mixes the two. A buyer who opens a bag and finds a high proportion of dark, stiff, older needles is looking at material that was collected without that distinction.

## Storage: the loss that continues after drying

> **Direct answer:** Dried needles are hygroscopic, so storage extends the drying problem rather than suspending it. A sealed, dark, cool container slows the loss of volatiles; a bag left open in humid air reverses the drying step and invites mould.

The leaf that leaves the dryer is not chemically inert. Its tissue is mostly cellulose and lignin with a small residue of volatile terpenes, and both fractions respond to the room. Cellulose takes up water from humid air and releases it again in dry air; the volatile fraction escapes whenever the container is opened. Neither process stops, but both can be slowed.

The moisture side matters more than most buyers expect. A lot that tests dry in the warehouse can feel clammy after a week in a humid climate, because the tissue equilibrates with the air around it. Once moisture climbs, the sequence that spoils a wet heap restarts at a lower speed:

dry needle → tissue reabsorbs water from humid air → moisture rises inside the leaf → mould spores, present on plant material and in the air, begin to grow → musty, cellar-like notes develop → the green top note is masked and then lost.

The volatile side is simpler. Each time the bag is opened, the headspace exchanges with room air and carries away the molecules that had collected there. A large bag opened daily loses its top note faster than a small sealed packet opened once, which is why repacking into smaller units is worth the trouble for anyone who uses the material slowly.

Three rules follow. Keep the material sealed between uses, because the container is the only barrier between leaf and room. Keep it dark, because light accelerates breakdown of the green fraction. Keep it cool but dry, and treat refrigeration with caution: a cold bag brought into warm air condenses water on the leaf, which is the opposite of what drying achieved.

## Forms: whole, cut, powdered, distilled

> **Direct answer:** The traditional form is the whole dried needle, used in sachets. Cut and powdered forms release faster and fade faster, and distilled pine needle oil is a separate modern product rather than a stronger version of the leaf.

| Form | How it is made | Release behaviour | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried needle | Gathered and air-dried, cleaned | Slow, steady, low dust | 香囊 sachets, pillow inserts, display |
| Cut needle | Chopped to short lengths | Faster initial release, shorter life | Blending, sachet refills |
| Powder | Milled dried foliage | Immediate burst, very short life, dusts heavily | Minor accent in a blend |
| Pine needle oil | Steam or solvent distillation (modern) | Concentrated, sharp, dose-sensitive | Perfumery and fragrance compounding |

The oil deserves a caution rather than an introduction. It is not "concentrated needles" in any simple sense: distillation selects for the volatile fraction and leaves behind the green, waxy, and bitter notes that give the dried leaf much of its character. A formulator who tries to reproduce a sachet by adding a few drops of oil to dried foliage will get a sharp, thin, oddly synthetic-smelling result, because the oil is not a like-for-like substitute.

The powder has its own trap. Grinding increases surface area, so the first impression is stronger than whole needles — and then the material goes flat faster, because there is more exposed surface for the volatiles to leave from. Powder also sifts through cloth and coats the inside of a jar. For a sachet, whole needles are better on every count except the initial intensity.

## Sachet versus burning: two release regimes

> **Direct answer:** A sachet releases aroma by evaporation at room temperature; a burning blend releases it by pyrolysis. Pine needles work well in the first regime and poorly in the second, because heat destroys the green note before it can travel.

The distinction is mechanical, not a matter of taste.

needle warmed gently by the body or the room → terpenes evaporate without decomposition → the green note reaches the nose intact → the sachet performs as intended.

needle in contact with a burning ember → the tissue heats far beyond evaporation temperature → terpenes crack and oxidise, and cellulose smoulders → the smoke carries acrid, tarry, scorched notes → the fresh forest character is replaced by the smell of burning garden waste.

This is why pine needles are described here as a sachet herb first. In a burning blend they can still contribute, but the contribution is small and conditional: the needle must be a minor part of the mass, the blend must burn cool, and the formula must not depend on the needle for its top note. A stick or cone that is largely pine needle will smell of combustion, not of forest.

| Application | Release regime | What the needle contributes | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 香囊 sachet | Evaporation | Fresh green opening, slow fade | Staleness after a few weeks |
| Pillow or drawer sachet | Evaporation, low airflow | Soft conifer background | Dust and staining |
| Loose incense blend | Partial pyrolysis | A green edge if proportion is low | Scorched, ashy note if proportion is high |
| Stick or cone | Pyrolysis | Minor top-note lift at best | The green note is destroyed |

The practical rule is proportion. A pinch of needle in a mostly resin-and-wood blend lifts the opening; a heap of needle in the same blend turns the smoke harsh. There is no universal number, because burn temperature and particle size vary, but the direction is reliable: less needle, cooler burn, better result.

## The 香囊 in practice

> **Direct answer:** A 香囊 is a small cloth bag of dried aromatic herbs, and its performance depends on airflow, fill, and placement rather than on any mysterious property of the recipe. Pine needles suit the format because they are light, bulky, and release slowly when whole.

The construction is simple enough to explain in a sentence: a cloth bag, loosely filled with dried plant material, closed at the neck, and hung or carried where air moves across it. The cloth acts as a membrane. Air diffuses in, picks up volatiles from the dried tissue, and carries them out; the tighter the weave, the slower the exchange.

That gives three practical levers. A loose fill leaves space for air and lets the needle move slightly, which keeps the surface exposed. A dense pack compresses the material into a mass and slows release. A coarse cloth passes more aroma than a tight one but also lets dust through, which matters with powdered ingredients and less with whole needles.

Placement does the rest. A sachet in a drawer sits in still air and releases slowly; one worn against the body is warmed continuously and gives up its scent faster. Neither is wrong, but a sachet that seems weak in a drawer may simply be cold. Warming it briefly between the hands, or crushing the bag gently, restores a burst of green.

The refresh cycle is short by the standards of resins. A pine sachet is at its best in the first weeks and then declines steadily, even though the cloth and the needle look unchanged. Changing the fill, rather than adding more of the same, is the only real remedy once the volatiles are gone.

## Quality: a freshness-first grading frame

> **Direct answer:** Pine needle quality is freshness, cleanliness, and dryness, in that order. There is no formal commercial grading ladder, so a three-tier working frame based on colour, aroma, and foreign matter is more useful than an invented standard.

| Grade (editorial frame) | Colour | Needle condition | Aroma | Foreign matter | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh | Vivid green to blue-green | Whole, flexible, clean | Sharp, resinous, clearly conifer | Twigs and cones minimal | Suitable for sachets and accents |
| Sound | Dull green to olive | Mostly whole, some breakage | Faint pine, some hay | Some twig and dust | Usable, lower intensity |
| Stale | Brown, grey, or mottled | Brittle, crushed, dusty | Musty, flat, or sour | Heavy dust, possible mould | Reject |

Colour is the fastest screen and the least reliable one on its own. Needles darken with age, but they also darken with hot drying, and a steamed or sun-baked lot can be brown while still carrying some aroma. Smell is the better test, and the correct way to take it is to crush a small pinch between the fingers and smell immediately. A fresh lot releases a clear conifer sharpness; a stale lot releases dust and a faint, sweetish hay.

The foreign-matter column matters more than it looks. Twigs add weight and little aroma. Cones and seed cases are a different material entirely. And because needles are gathered from the ground or from pruned branches, grit and insect fragments are common. A clean lot is evidence of care somewhere upstream, and that care usually shows in the drying as well.

### What the bag says before you smell it

A sample can be read in the hand as well as in the nose. The visible faults are not equal: some point to the drying, some to the gathering, and one points to a lot that was never properly dried at all.

| What you see or feel | Likely cause upstream | What it means for use |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform brown, brittle needles | Old stock, or hot drying that fixed colour while driving off volatiles | Weak top note; at best a minor accent |
| Bright green but faint smell | Colour retained, volatiles lost — long storage or heat | Appearance is not evidence; reject for sachet work |
| Musty or sour edge on opening | Damp heap during drying, or later moisture pickup | Reject; the note cannot be washed or aired out |
| Heavy twig and cone fraction | Raked ground collection with no sorting | Paying for weight rather than aroma; uneven release |
| Soft, clammy feel | Under-dried, or stored in humid conditions | Will mould in a sealed bag; dry it or reject it |
| Fine dust through the weave | Crushed material sold as whole needle | Faster opening burst, much shorter working life |

This is a diagnostic, not a grade. Two lots can look equally brown for different reasons — one old, one overheated — and both will disappoint, though the overheated lot may still carry a trace of resinous warmth where the old one rarely does. That distinction matters when a buyer is deciding whether to return a lot or simply use it faster.

## What the classical record says

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) enters the pine under 松 in its first tree section and distinguishes the parts: needles and seeds for consumption, nodes and heartwood for durability, resin as the tree's essence. The aromatic sachet use sits alongside that record rather than inside a formal incense canon.

The line usually quoted from the 木之一 entry runs: "松葉、松實，服餌所須；松節、松心，耐久不朽。松脂則又樹之津液精華也." The sense is a division of labour within one tree — the foliage and seeds belong to consumption, the nodes and heartwood to lasting structures, and the resin is the tree's essence. Read as a whole, the passage is a taxonomy of usefulness, not a recipe.

That framing is useful for a modern reader in two ways. It confirms that the needles were treated as a distinct part with their own uses, and it shows that the classical interest in the pine was broader than fragrance. The sachet tradition, in which dried aromatic herbs are packed into small cloth bags, is the context in which pine needles most plausibly served an aromatic purpose: a fresh green note among other herbs, worn or hung rather than burned.

The pine also carries a symbolic load that has nothing to do with scent. As an evergreen that holds its needles through winter, it stands for endurance and long life in Chinese art and poetry. That symbolism is worth knowing because it explains why pine appears so often in objects that are not primarily aromatic — a pine-motif box, a pine-scented sachet given at New Year, a painting of weathered branches. The meaning and the smell travel together in culture even though they are separate facts.

## Safety: know the tree before you trust the needle

> **Direct answer:** The first safety issue with pine needles is botanical misidentification — not every evergreen needle belongs to a safe pine — and the second is smoke from combustion. Neither is dramatic if handled sensibly, and both are easy to ignore until they matter.

Pines hold their needles in fascicles of two, three, or five, wrapped at the base by a small papery sheath. That bundle is the single most useful field character, because it separates *Pinus* from the conifers that do not bundle their needles. Yew (*Taxus*) is the one to know: its needles are flat, soft, single, and arranged along the twig rather than clustered in a bundle, and the plant is seriously toxic. A sachet made from a misidentified evergreen is not a minor error.

The practical rules that follow are simple:

- Gather only from trees you can identify with confidence, and check the fascicle sheath.
- Avoid roadside and industrial-edge collections, where dust and exhaust residue settle on the foliage.
- Do not assume that a decorative conifer in a garden is a pine; many are not.
- Keep dried needles away from food preparation areas, and wash hands after handling.

No plant material becomes harmless because it smells pleasant. Burning produces smoke and fine particles whatever the leaf, and pine is no exception. Work in moving air, do not draw concentrated smoke into the lungs, keep flames and embers clear of children and pets, and never burn a musty lot indoors. The [safety guide](/safety/) sets out the broader precautions.

There is also a distinction worth making about pine needle oil. A concentrated essential oil is not interchangeable with dried foliage: it is far stronger, it is not intended for casual internal use, and it should be kept out of reach of children. This page describes the dried needle as a sachet and blending material, and makes no therapeutic claim for either form.

## Sourcing checklist

> **Direct answer:** A buyer should confirm the part, the species claim, the freshness, and the drying history. For a low-cost leaf, the useful questions are about care rather than pedigree.

1. **Part.** Is it 松针 (needle), and not 松香 (resin), 松节 (node), or a mix?
2. **Species claim.** If a species is named, do the fascicles match? Two-, three-, or five-needle bundles are visible on inspection.
3. **Colour.** Green to blue-green, or brown and grey? Some browning is normal at the tips; a uniformly brown lot is old.
4. **Aroma.** Crush a pinch. Clear conifer sharpness is the pass signal; must, dust, or sourness is the fail.
5. **Moisture.** Dry and springy, not soft or clammy. Damp material will mould in the bag.
6. **Foreign matter.** Twigs, cones, grit, and insect fragments are deductions, not details.
7. **Storage.** Was the lot kept sealed and dry? Needles reabsorb moisture readily in humid weather.

For a material this inexpensive, the cost of a bad lot is not the money — it is the wasted batch it goes into and the false conclusion that pine needles "don't smell of much." They do, when they are fresh.

## Three misconceptions worth correcting

> **Direct answer:** The common errors are treating pine needles as a cheap filler, assuming more material means a stronger note, and reading green colour as proof of freshness. Each mistake leads to a predictable disappointment.

**The filler misconception.** Because needles are abundant and inexpensive, they get treated as bulk — something to add volume to a sachet or a blend. That reading misses the material's actual function. A filler is aromatic dead weight; the needle is a genuine top note, just a fleeting one. The right conclusion from its low price is that you can afford to use good material generously, not that the material is unimportant.

**The quantity misconception.** Doubling the needle does not double the scent. Volatiles leave the surface at a rate set by temperature and airflow, and a thicker layer mostly adds more surface area deeper inside the mass, where air does not move. In a sachet, an overfilled bag can smell weaker than a loose one. In a burning blend, more needle means more combustion products and a harsher smoke, not a brighter forest note.

**The colour misconception.** Green is a useful first screen, but it is not proof of freshness. Needles can be dyed, or dried hot enough to fix a green colour while cooking off the volatiles. Conversely, a naturally dried lot may be dull olive and still smell clean. The test that actually works is the crush test: bruise a pinch and smell it immediately.

What follows from these three corrections is a single habit — judge the material by behaviour rather than by appearance or price. That habit transfers to almost every aromatic raw material, which is why the pine needle is a good teaching material despite its modest standing.

## FAQ

**What is 松针 in the incense trade?** The dried foliage of a *Pinus* tree — the leaf, not the resin, wood, or seed. It is sold whole rather than milled, and its main outlet is the small cloth sachet. Buyers judge it on freshness first, because the note it carries is volatile and short.

**How does 松针 differ from 松香?** They are parts of one tree with opposite behaviour. The needle is light, volatile, and fleeting; the resin is heavy, warm, and persistent. A formula that needs a forest-green lift reaches for the first, one that needs a base for the second. Treating them as interchangeable is the most common buying error in this corner of the trade.

**Why has my sachet gone quiet while the needles still look green?** Colour is fixed in the tissue; scent is not. Light terpenes leave the leaf continuously at room temperature, so the visual material and the aromatic material age on different clocks. Crushing a pinch buys a short revival from cells that stayed intact. Only fresh fill restores the note for long.

**Can the needle carry a burning blend?** Only as a minor voice. Pyrolysis arrives before the green note can travel, so a stick or cone built around pine foliage smells of scorched vegetation. Keep the proportion low, keep the burn cool, and let resin or wood do the structural work.

**Does a named species guarantee a better lot?** No. Species sets a range of possibilities; drying, storage, and the share of current-season growth decide what actually arrives. A generic lot handled well beats a named lot that sat in a humid warehouse. Check the fascicle bundle to confirm the genus claim, not to rank quality.

## Summary

松针 is a fresh leaf with a short aromatic career. It is not the resin, the wood, or the seed, and the part-name is the only dependable way to keep those four commodities apart on a purchase order. Whole and loosely packed, it gives a sachet a green conifer opening; ground fine or heaped thick, it gives dust and a scorched note. Freshness, cleanliness, and dryness decide whether a lot is worth using, and extra material never repairs a stale one.

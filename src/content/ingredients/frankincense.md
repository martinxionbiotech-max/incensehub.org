**Direct answer:** 乳香 (rǔxiāng) is the hardened exudate that seeps from a cut in a *Boswellia* tree's bark — a resin that softens in warmth and releases a citrus-and-balsam scent, carried along trade routes for more than a thousand years.

Most descriptions of frankincense stop at "resin from a tree used in churches", which is true and nearly useless. The material has a physical structure that dictates how it must be handled, a species map that explains why two lumps with the same name can smell different, a Chinese record that is older and more specific than most English-language pages admit, and a chemistry that separates what the resin contains from what the smoke actually carries.

This page works through those layers in the order a buyer or maker needs them. It starts with what the lump in your hand is made of, moves to the species and grade questions that determine what you are buying, and ends with the practical handling and safety points that decide whether the material performs. Where a claim is traditional rather than established, the page says so. Where the trade uses a word loosely, the page marks the looseness.

The row that determines everything downstream is the material class. Frankincense is not a wood, not a dried sap in the sense that maple syrup is a sap, and not a "gum" in the sense of the gum arabic used as an adhesive. It is a composite exudate, and each of its three fractions behaves differently when you heat it, grind it or store it.

## What the lump is made of

**Direct answer:** Frankincense is an oleo-gum-resin with three fractions — resin acids and diterpenes, a water-soluble polysaccharide gum, and a small volatile oil — and the proportions vary by species, season and tapping.

The tree produces the material as a wound response. *Boswellia* species carry resin canals in the bark, and when the bark is cut the canals are severed and the contents flow out. That is a different physiology from the pine resin most people know: conifers produce resin in resin ducts throughout the wood as well as the bark, and the material is dominated by diterpene acids with a large monoterpene fraction. Frankincense is closer to a suspension of resin in a gum solution, with the volatile oil dispersed through both.

The three fractions explain three different behaviours. The resin fraction is the part that melts, smokes and leaves a hard residue; it carries the diterpenes and the triterpenoid acids that laboratory work has studied. The gum fraction is water-soluble and swells when it meets moisture, which is why a resin powder can turn sticky in a humid workshop and why a water drop on a tear can raise a milky bloom. The volatile fraction is small by weight and large by influence: it holds the citrus, pine and green notes that make fresh frankincense smell alive.

Follow the material from tree to tear: bark is scored → resin canals are cut → the exudate flows and coats the wound → the volatile fraction begins to evaporate at ambient temperature → the resin oxidises and thickens → the droplet hardens into a tear. Every step loses something. The tear you buy is not the fluid the tree produced; it is that fluid after days of exposure, and its aroma is the aroma of a partially evaporated material.

This is the reason a fresh tear and a two-year-old tear from the same tree do not smell alike, and the reason two harvests of one species can diverge. It is also the reason the oil and the resin cannot be swapped: distillation lifts a concentrated volatile fraction and leaves the heavy matter in the still, so the bottle holds the opening and the lump holds the body.

## Three products from one exudate

**Direct answer:** The same lump becomes three commercial products — whole resin, distilled oil and solvent-extracted absolute — and each holds a different slice of the original material.

Whole tears are the material with nothing taken away: picked over, freed of bark and grit, sold as they dried. All three fractions are still present, so the lump melts, clings and smells the way it did on the tree. No fraction has been removed or concentrated, which makes this the reference against which the oil and the absolute are judged.

Steam distillation takes the material apart. Resin is charged into a still with water, heat is applied, and the volatile fraction rides out with the steam, condenses and separates as oil. What stays behind is the gum and the heavy diterpene resin — the body of the material. The oil is therefore not "liquid frankincense"; it is the opening of frankincense, bottled. Yields are low because the volatile fraction is small, which is why a gram of oil costs many times a gram of tears.

Solvent extraction takes a different route and produces a concrete, then an absolute. The result is fuller and darker than the distilled oil, closer to the resin's warmth, and it is used where a perfumer wants the balsamic body without the water-soluble gum. It is also the route that carries more of the resin's heavier molecules into a pourable product.

| Product | Process | What it holds | Where it earns its keep | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole tears | Sorting and cleaning | Resin, gum and oil together | Heating, ritual, tasting a species | Dissolve evenly into a fine blend |
| Essential oil | Steam or hydrodistillation | Mostly monoterpenes | Perfumery top notes, scenting a space | Reproduce the resin's body |
| Absolute | Solvent extraction | A broader, heavier slice | Fine fragrance, resinous accords | Behave like a simple resin |
| Powder | Milling and sifting | The whole material, divided | Sticks, cones, pastes | Stay free-flowing in humidity |

Follow the still: resin plus water plus heat → volatile molecules vaporise → steam carries them to the condenser → they liquefy and separate → the gum and heavy resin remain in the vessel → the bottle on the shelf holds a fraction, not the whole. Once that is clear, the pricing and the substitutions stop being mysterious. A buyer comparing oil and resin by weight is comparing two different products that happen to share a name.

## The species map

**Direct answer:** Five or six *Boswellia* species supply nearly all traded resin, each with its own scent signature, so a bag labelled only "frankincense" could hold any of them.

| Species | Main origin | Trade handle | Aroma character | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *B. sacra* | Oman, Yemen | Hojari (for the pale Omani grade) | Bright citrus-resinous, slightly green, the reference "church" note | Slow-growing; the prestige origin in Western markets |
| *B. carterii* | Somalia, Ethiopia, Sudan | Somali frankincense | Clean citrus, softer balsam, slightly sweeter | Largest commercial volume; treated by some botanists as conspecific with *B. sacra* |
| *B. frereana* | Somaliland, Somalia | Maydi | Peppery-citrus, resinous, less sweet, distinct from the church profile | Valued in Gulf chewing-gum and fragrance uses as well as incense |
| *B. papyrifera* | Ethiopia, Sudan, Eritrea | Mader | Softer, sweeter, more balsamic, less citrus | Common in blended commercial material |
| *B. serrata* | India | Salai | Drier, more balsamic, less bright | The Indian gum-resin, used in ayurvedic and incense contexts |
| *B. rivae*, *B. neglecta* | Kenya, Ethiopia, Somalia | Often sold as "frankincense" without a species name | Variable, generally softer | Minor trade, frequently blended |

Two cautions sit under that table. The first is taxonomic: the boundary between *B. sacra* and *B. carterii* is disputed, and some authorities treat the Somali populations as part of *B. sacra* while others keep them separate. A supplier who insists on a hard species line may be repeating a trade convention rather than a botanical finding. The second is that trade names are not species names. "Hojari" describes a grade and appearance associated with Omani material; it is not a botanical category, and it can be applied optimistically to material from elsewhere.

What the species map does buy you is a prediction about behaviour. The bright, almost lemon-and-pine opening that most people associate with frankincense is strongest in *B. sacra* and *B. carterii*. *B. frereana* reads more peppery and less sweet, which is why it performs differently in a blend even though it is a fine material. *B. serrata* is drier and more balsamic, closer to the resinous heart than to the citrus top. If a formula was built on one of these and the supply switches, the top of the composition changes before anything else.

## 乳香: the Chinese name and its record

**Direct answer:** The two characters 乳 (milk) and 香 (fragrance) describe the pale fluid that beads from a wounded tree; the name reached Chinese texts as a label for an import, and the material was usually discussed alongside [没药 (mòyào, myrrh)](/ingredients/myrrh/).

The name is a description, not a mystery. 乳 is milk; 香 is fragrance; the compound names the appearance of the fresh exudate. That kind of descriptive naming is typical of materials that arrived through trade rather than through an inherited Chinese category — the name explains the object to a reader who has never seen it.

Dating the arrival is easier than dating a single text. Aromatic cargo moved by sea and by caravan, and by the Tang period 乳香 was established enough in Chinese writing to be given a pharmaceutical entry rather than a traveller's description; the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草綱目) later carries it as a known import. Nothing in that record suggests a native plant, and nothing suggests a recent arrival either.

What stays constant across the record is the company the material keeps. 乳香 and 没药 are named in the same breath so often that they read as a formula rather than two entries — a bright resin and a dark one, both from Burseraceae, prescribed and burned as a pair. The same double act appears in the eastern Mediterranean, where the two were traded and offered together. Companionship in a text is not equivalence, though; it marks a division of labour, the way salt and pepper divide one.

Chinese medical writing describes 乳香 with the language of 活血 (huóxuè, "invigorate the blood") and 止痛 (zhǐtòng, "relieve pain"), and those phrases belong to the traditional theoretical frame rather than to modern pharmacology. They are worth recording because they explain how the material was used and why it appears in so many formulas. They are not clinical claims, and this page does not convert them into any.

For incense specifically, 乳香 belongs to the stream of blended incense that the Ming compendium 香乘 (Xiangcheng) documents — a tradition in which imported resins were combined with native woods, flowers and gums under the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) logic borrowed from medicine. In that frame a bright resin usually supports rather than leads: it lifts the composition and fills the upper register while a wood carries the body. The assignment is a compositional judgment, not a fixed rule, and the same resin can be chief in one blend and assistant in another.

## Why a resin became ritual

**Direct answer:** Resins earned their place in temples and rites for practical reasons — slow release, portability, visible smoke — long before anyone wrote a theology of incense.

Start with the map. The trees grow on the dry escarpments of Dhofar in southern Oman and across Hadhramaut in Yemen, and the resin moved north by camel through Sabaean and later Nabataean hands: Shabwa, Petra, Gaza, Alexandria, then the Mediterranean. Pliny the Elder complained about the cost of the Arabian monopoly in the first century, which is a useful reminder that the material was already a luxury good with a long supply chain before the Roman Empire existed.

A caravan good has to survive the journey. Tears dry hard, resist rot, tolerate heat and cold, and deliver a large effect from a small weight — a kilogram scents a courtyard and can be divided without a scale. Those are the properties that made frankincense worth carrying across a desert, and they are also the properties that made it suitable for offering: a material that is scarce at the point of use, stable in storage and generous in effect fits the economics of ritual almost perfectly.

The ceremonial use follows the physics. A resin that volatilises slowly scents a space for the length of a ceremony; a visible plume marks the room as different from the one next door; the smell settles into cloth and hair and travels home with the participant. Egyptian embalming, temple offerings and church liturgies drew on those effects without needing a theory of why smoke is pleasing.

In China the same physical logic met a different ceremonial frame. Aromatics arrived by the overland routes and, increasingly, by sea through ports such as Guangzhou, where maritime trade administration supervised the cargo; by the Tang and Song periods, imported resins were part of a recognised category of 香药 (xiāngyào, aromatic goods) handled by physicians and perfumers alike. 乳香 took a place among the resins used in blended incense, not as a native plant but as a dependable import.

The loop that formed is worth stating plainly: a resin that volatilises slowly → scent persists through the ceremony → the smell becomes associated with the occasion → repetition binds them → the material is expected at the rite → demand supports the route → the route keeps the price high → the price keeps the material special. Behaviour created the association, and the association protected the price.

## Why heat, not flame

**Direct answer:** Frankincense gives up its scent by melting and evaporating, and a flame burns the delicate top notes before they can reach you.

The physical sequence is short and decisive. The resin softens somewhere around 60–100 °C, well below the temperature of any flame. As the melt pool forms, the monoterpenes at the surface evaporate and carry the citrus-pine top. Raise the temperature further and the resin begins to pyrolyse: the molecules crack into smaller, harsher fragments — phenols, aldehydes, soot precursors — and the smell shifts from bright resin to acrid smoke.

| Method | Rough temperature at the material | What happens | Aroma result | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wax warmer or gentle plate | 60–90 °C | Slow melt, volatiles released gradually | Sweet, rounded, low projection | Small rooms, long sessions |
| Electric resin heater | 150–220 °C | Steady volatilisation, minimal pyrolysis | Bright citrus top, clean resin heart | Tasting a species, blending trials |
| Charcoal disc | 500–700 °C at the surface | Flash volatilisation plus pyrolysis at the contact point | Intense, smoky, "church" character, shorter | Ritual, large spaces, atmosphere |
| Direct flame | Above 800 °C | Combustion of the volatiles, soot formation | Acrid, tarry, thin | Nothing aromatic |

The table explains a fact that surprises people who have only met frankincense in a church: much of the smoke in that setting comes from the charcoal, not from the resin. A charcoal disc runs hot enough to pyrolyse part of the gum-resin on contact, and the resulting phenolic, tarry notes are read as "sacred" because they are inseparable from the ritual setting. Heat the same resin on an electric heater and the citrus top reappears; the material has not changed, the temperature has.

Follow the chain: solid resin → heat above the melt point → melt pool forms → monoterpenes evaporate from the surface → scent rises → if the temperature keeps climbing, pyrolysis takes over → phenols and soot dominate → the top notes are gone. Every step is a temperature threshold, which is why the heat source matters more than the quantity of resin.

There is a practical corollary for anyone burning on charcoal. The first thirty seconds are mostly volatilisation and the smoke is sweet; after that the contact point chars and the profile turns tarry. If you want the bright phase to last, keep the resin off the hottest part of the disc, or use a heater and accept a smaller, cleaner scent.

## The aroma, layer by layer

**Direct answer:** Frankincense reads as a citrus-pine top over a resinous-balsamic heart and a soft creamy-woody base, and the balance shifts with species, age and heat.

| Layer | Main contributors | What you smell | What destroys it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | Monoterpenes — α-pinene, limonene, α-thujene; octyl acetate in some species | Lemon peel, pine needle, green resin | Overheating, long storage, fine grinding |
| Heart | Heavier terpenoids and resin acids | Balsam, warm resin, faintly medicinal | Pyrolysis on a hot disc |
| Base | Resin acids and the non-volatile matrix | Creamy, woody, slightly sweet residue | Nothing much; it outlasts the rest |

The descriptor "lemony" is a shorthand for a group of compounds rather than a single note. Octyl acetate gives a waxy-citrus impression in some species; limonene and α-pinene give the sharper peel-and-pine edge; α-thujene adds a green, slightly camphoraceous quality. Because the mix differs by species and harvest, two materials that both smell "like frankincense" can behave very differently in a blend — one will lift a composition, the other will sit on it.

In a blend, frankincense works as a brightener and a bridge. It brightens heavy woods and dark resins by adding a volatile top that reads as clean rather than sweet. It bridges citrus and resin because it contains both characters in one material, which is why it appears in compositions that would otherwise need two separate ingredients to make the transition. What it does not do well is fix. Its resin matrix is far less persistent than labdanum, benzoin or oakmoss, and a formula that relies on frankincense to hold a base will lose shape faster than expected.

That last point is an editorial correction to a common claim. Frankincense is often listed as a fixative because it is a resin, and resins are assumed to fix. In practice its volatile fraction leaves quickly and its residue is soft; it supports a base rather than anchoring it. A perfumer who wants hold reaches for a heavier material and uses frankincense for lift.

## Grading: what a tear can and cannot tell you

**Direct answer:** Tears are graded by size, colour, clarity and cleanliness, but those signals describe selection and handling rather than species, chemistry or likely performance in a blend.

| Signal | What it usually indicates | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Large, whole tears | Careful collection and handling | Species identity |
| Pale, translucent colour | The high grade the Omani trade prizes | Higher volatile-oil content |
| Greenish tinge | A characteristic of some hojari material | Authenticity on its own |
| Uniform colour across a lot | Sorting and cleaning | Absence of blending |
| Bark and dark fragments present | Less cleaning | Poorer aroma |
| Dust and small chips | Handling loss | Lower quality as powder |

The grading vocabulary is real and old, and it is also narrower than buyers assume. Size and colour tell you how the material was collected, sorted and cleaned. They do not tell you which species produced it, how much volatile oil remains, or whether it was stored well. A bag of large pale tears from a poorly stored lot can smell flatter than a bag of small dark tears from a fresh harvest.

What follows depends on the job. Whole heating rewards large clean pieces: they melt at an even rate, throw no dust, and look the way the buyer hopes. Milling erases that advantage in seconds, so a sifted grade with live aroma beats a showpiece that has already lost its opening. Treating powder as inferior misunderstands the format — it is the same resin with a different cost structure.

There is no single international grading standard for frankincense. The categories are trade conventions that vary by origin and buyer, and a specification written in one market may mean nothing in another. That is not a reason to ignore grades; it is a reason to ask what a grade means in the specific supply chain you are buying from.

## Adulteration and label screening

**Direct answer:** The recurring problems are species substitution, added gum or pressed dust, and diluted oil, and field checks can screen for them without proving anything.

| Check | How it is done | What failure looks like | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Species claim | Ask for harvest region and species; compare against a reference sample by nose | A "sacra" price with a Somali or Ethiopian aroma profile | Sensory comparison needs experience |
| Added gum | A drop of water on a broken surface | Milky bloom and stickiness beyond normal | Some gum is native to the resin |
| Pressed or reconstituted tears | Look for uniform shapes, seams, dull surfaces | Too-perfect pieces that crumble or dissolve quickly | Small pieces are hard to judge |
| Diluted oil | Evaporation on a warm plate; residue check | Thin, sharp, solvent-like opening; oily residue | Needs a reference |
| Heavy weighting | Feel and density | Unusually heavy pieces with dull sound | No reliable field test |

These checks are screening tools, not analysis. A water drop can reveal added gum, but frankincense contains its own gum and will react to water anyway; the test is about degree, not presence. A visual inspection can catch crude reconstitution, but a well-made pressed tear is designed to pass a glance. The only definitive methods are laboratory ones — gas chromatography for the volatile profile, microscopy or spectroscopy for structure — and they cost more than most buyers want to spend on a small lot.

What a buyer can do without a lab is demand specificity. A supplier who names the species, the region, the harvest season and the grade is making claims that can be checked against the material and against other suppliers. A supplier who says only "frankincense, premium grade" is making a claim that cannot be wrong, which is a different and less useful thing.

## Frankincense against the other great aromatics

**Direct answer:** Two of these materials are exudates that liquefy on a hot surface; the other two are timber that has to smoulder, and that physical split predicts almost everything about how each one is sold and used.

| Material | Class | Source | Aroma | Heat behaviour | Chinese standing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frankincense (乳香) | Oleo-gum-resin | *Boswellia* bark | Citrus-pine over balsam | Melts and volatilises | Imported aromatic, classical record |
| Myrrh (没药) | Oleo-gum-resin | *Commiphora* bark | Bitter, balsamic, medicinal | Melts, smokes heavily | Imported aromatic, paired with 乳香 |
| Agarwood (沉香) | Resin-impregnated wood | *Aquilaria* | Deep, sweet, woody, animalic | Smoulders as wood | Central to the canon |
| Sandalwood (檀香) | Aromatic wood | *Santalum* | Creamy, milky, soft woody | Smoulders as wood | Central to the canon |

The table's most useful column is the class column. Frankincense and myrrh are exudates; agarwood and sandalwood are woods. That difference decides how each is prepared, how much heat it needs and how it fails. An exudate melts before it burns, so it can be used on a heater; a wood has to smoulder, so it needs a coal or a binder. Substituting one class for another in a formula is not a swap of aromas, it is a swap of physical behaviour.

The frankincense-myrrh pairing is the oldest in the trade and the easiest to misunderstand. They are not two versions of the same smell. Frankincense is bright, volatile and clean; myrrh is darker, heavier and slightly bitter, with a medicinal edge that reads as ancient rather than fresh. Used together they cover a wide band — the bright top and the dark base — which is why the pairing survived in both Chinese medicine and Mediterranean religion. For a deeper comparison, see [frankincense vs myrrh](/blog/frankincense-vs-myrrh/).

Set beside the timbers, 乳香 behaves like a brightener rather than a foundation. Layered over agarwood or sandalwood it thins and illuminates them; asked to do their job, it fails, because it has no fibre to smoulder. The [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) maps the whole field, and the [resinous aroma family](/blog/resinous-aroma-family/) places the balsams relative to one another.

## Boswellic acids: what is in the resin is not what is in the smoke

**Direct answer:** The boswellic acids are heavy triterpenoid compounds locked in the resin fraction — too large to rise with the smoke — and the medical interest in them has not translated into established clinical proof.

The boswellic acids — a group that includes KBA and its acetylated form AKBA — are pentacyclic triterpenoid acids. They sit in the resin fraction and they are large, heavy molecules by the standards of aroma chemistry. That size is not a detail; it is the reason they behave the way they do.

Volatility falls as molecular size rises. The compounds that reach your nose from a warm lump of resin are small monoterpenes and a few oxygenated derivatives, all light enough to leave the surface at 150–200 °C. The triterpenoid acids are an order of magnitude heavier, and they stay in the residue. What rises is the volatile fraction plus, at higher temperatures, the products of pyrolysis — phenols, aldehydes, furans and particulate matter. None of those is a boswellic acid.

| Question | What the chemistry says |
|---|---|
| Are boswellic acids in the resin? | Yes, in the resin fraction |
| Do they evaporate when incense is heated? | Very little, if any — they are not volatile at those temperatures |
| Is the smoke a delivery route for them? | Not in any meaningful quantity; the plume is dominated by terpenes and combustion products |
| Does laboratory interest equal clinical proof? | No; research interest is not established efficacy |

The chain runs: resin heated → volatile monoterpenes leave first → the plume carries those plus pyrolysis products → the heavy acid fraction remains in the residue → inhalation exposure to boswellic acids is minimal and unquantified. Anyone who argues that burning frankincense delivers the studied compounds has skipped the volatility step.

Put the two halves together and the marketing collapses. A sentence that mentions boswellic acids beside a burning tip implies the smoker receives them; the volatility gap says otherwise. Equally, the therapeutic claims made for the resin — joints, breathing, swelling — sit in the traditional-use and early-research pile. Old practice is not proof, and a censer is not a dosing device.

## The vocabulary of the trade

**Direct answer:** Most confusion around frankincense comes from trade words that look like species names but describe grades, regions or appearances instead.

| Term | What it actually means | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Luban | The Arabic word for frankincense as a substance | Gulf and Arabian trade language |
| Luban dhakar | "Male" frankincense — the harder, paler Omani grade | Omani sorting vocabulary |
| Hojari | A pale, translucent, sometimes green-tinged Omani grade | Dhofar trade usage |
| Maydi | The Somaliland name for *B. frereana* resin | Somali trade usage |
| Mader | An Ethiopian trade name associated with *B. papyrifera* | Ethiopian trade usage |
| Salai | The Indian name for *B. serrata* gum-resin | Indian market usage |
| Tears | Whole droplets of dried exudate | Universal trade term |
| Siftings | The small fragments left after sorting | Universal trade term |
| Bakhoor | A Gulf incense mixture, not a synonym for frankincense | Gulf domestic practice |

The table is worth keeping in mind whenever a supplier uses a word as though it settled a question. "Hojari" answers a question about appearance and reputation, not about botany. "Maydi" answers a question about species and origin, not about grade. "Luban" answers nothing at all beyond "this is frankincense", which is the word a buyer is trying to get past.

English adds its own ambiguity. "Frankincense oil" may be steam-distilled, solvent-extracted, or a compounded fragrance that only smells of resin, and the price gap between those three is wide. A line that names the material but not the route leaves the decisive question unanswered.

## A closer look at the volatile fraction

**Direct answer:** The smell of frankincense is carried by a small group of monoterpenes and oxygenated compounds whose balance differs by species, and the heavy diterpenes and acids that get the most research attention contribute little to the scent.

| Compound | Aroma impression | Where it matters |
|---|---|---|
| α-Pinene | Pine needle, fresh resin | Nearly all *Boswellia* species |
| Limonene | Citrus peel | The bright opening in *B. sacra* and *B. carterii* |
| α-Thujene | Green, camphoraceous, slightly medicinal | Strong in *B. frereana* and *B. serrata* |
| Myrcene | Soft, fruity, resinous | Rounds the citrus edge |
| p-Cymene | Terpenic, faintly solvent-like | Contributes to the "dry" quality of older resin |
| Octyl acetate | Waxy, citrus-floral | Characteristic of several African species |
| Incensole and incensole acetate | Faint balsamic; not a leading odorant | Diterpene fraction; studied for other reasons |
| Boswellic acids | No meaningful odour | Resin fraction; not volatile |

The table says something a smell description cannot. "Citrus" here is an equilibrium, not an ingredient: limonene alone suggests orange rind, and the recognisable frankincense opening appears only once α-pinene and octyl acetate join it. It also separates the molecules that pharmacology discusses from the ones a nose actually meets at the burner.

This is also where species substitution becomes detectable by nose rather than by certificate. *B. frereana* carries more α-thujene and less of the octyl esters, so it reads greener and pepperier; *B. serrata* reads drier and more balsamic; *B. papyrifera* reads softer and sweeter. None of those differences is a defect. They are the reason a blend built on one species does not survive a silent switch to another.

## Matching the grade to the job

**Direct answer:** The right frankincense depends on what you are doing with it, and the most expensive grade is often the wrong purchase.

| Use | Buy | Ignore | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scenting a room daily | Mid-grade tears of a named species | Premium appearance grades | A heater does not care how the tear looks |
| Ceremonial or ritual use | Larger tears, charcoal setup | Show-grade pricing | Smoke and occasion dominate the experience |
| Perfumery | Distilled oil or absolute from a named species | Tear grading | The product is a fraction, not the lump |
| Incense manufacturing | Sifted powder at a specified mesh | Tear size and colour | Milling erases both within seconds |
| Study and comparison | Small lots across several species | Bulk pricing | The point is difference, not volume |
| Gifting | Whole, clean, pale tears | Powder grades | Appearance is part of the gift |

The logic behind the table is simple: grade signals are about handling and appearance, and they matter only to the extent that handling and appearance are part of what you are buying. For a manufacturer, a specification that fixes species, mesh, moisture and aroma is more useful than a photograph of large tears. For a household burner, a clean mid-grade tear from a known species will outperform an expensive show grade that has sat in a warm shop window for two years.

## Putting resin into a burnable format

**Direct answer:** Resin is thermoplastic and sticky, so a frankincense-heavy stick needs more binder, a coarser grind and slower drying than an all-wood stick, and a formula that ignores this will tunnel or go out.

Particle size starts the trouble. Resin grains soften ahead of the coal front, and a softened grain seals the air channels that keep a stick alive; the burn then wanders, tunnels down one flank, or dies where the load is heaviest. Humidity compounds it, because the gum fraction takes up water and turns the surface tacky.

| Format | Typical resin role | What to control | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose tears | The whole material | Tear size and heat source | Scorching on a hot disc |
| Cone | Resin as a major component with wood flour and binder | Grind consistency, moisture, pressing pressure | Cracking, uneven burn, crumbling |
| Stick | Resin as an accent, wood and binder as structure | Binder ratio, resin mesh, drying schedule | Tunnelling, extinction, soft stick |
| Powder blend | Resin powder for scent, wood powder for body | Particle size match, humidity | Segregation, clumping |

The practical rules that follow are unglamorous. Grind resin coarser than you would grind a wood, because fine resin powder melts into a paste. Increase binder when resin load rises, because the resin contributes no structural fibre. Dry slowly, because a fast dry case-hardens the surface and traps moisture inside, which later cracks the stick. And test the batch by burning it, not by smelling the paste: a wet paste smells stronger than the finished stick because the volatiles are still concentrated in the water.

For a manufacturer, the consistency question is bigger than any single formula. Frankincense from a different species, a different grade or a different storage history will change the burn. The way to keep a product stable is to specify species, grade, mesh and moisture, and to keep a retained sample of each incoming lot for comparison — the discipline described in the [factory vetting guide](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/) and the [spec sheet and COA](/blog/incense-spec-sheet-coa/) notes.

## Sourcing, sustainability and the tap

**Direct answer:** Nearly all commercial resin is cut from trees growing where they always grew — dry, rocky hillsides with no irrigation and little management — so the real question is how often a tree is wounded and whether young trees survive to replace it.

Tapping is a wounding practice. The harvester cuts shallow strips of bark, the tree responds by exuding resin, and the cut is reopened or extended over a season. A tree can survive this for years, but repeated tapping without rest reduces vigour and yield, and research on *Boswellia* populations in the Horn of Africa has reported poor seedling recruitment and ageing stands in some areas. Land conversion and grazing add pressure that has nothing to do with resin collection.

Follow the chain: repeated tapping without rest → smaller exudate and weaker trees → fewer viable seeds and seedlings → an ageing population → declining yield per hectare → pressure to tap the remaining trees harder. That loop is the sustainability problem in one line, and it explains why sourcing questions about rest cycles matter more than questions about total tonnage.

Ask a supplier four things and listen to the shape of the answer. Which species, and where was it cut? Wild stands or planted? When was the harvest, and how long was the tree left alone afterwards? Can the lot be traced to a collection area? Complete answers are rare, but partial ones still separate a trader who knows the ground from one who knows only the export port.

Check the listing per species and per market. *Boswellia* is not covered uniformly by international trade rules, and the position shifts between assessments, so the CITES checklist is the only sensible place to confirm the current entry. What does not shift is the pressure on the stands: a long and romantic trade history has never been a conservation measure, and no label yet certifies a rest cycle.

## Storage and decline

**Direct answer:** Frankincense loses its top notes before it loses its resin, so storage is about protecting the volatile fraction from heat, light and air.

| Factor | Effect | Practical response |
|---|---|---|
| Heat | Accelerates volatile loss and oxidation | Store away from radiators, kitchens and sun |
| Light | Degrades the aromatic profile over time | Opaque or dark glass, closed cupboard |
| Air | Oxidation and gradual loss of the top | Seal the container; avoid repeated opening |
| Humidity | Gum fraction takes up water; surface becomes tacky | Keep dry, with a desiccant if the climate demands it |
| Freezing | Condensation on thawing damages the surface | Avoid unless the material is sealed and acclimatised |

The decline is gradual and easy to miss because the resin still works. A tired frankincense will still melt and still smell resinous; what it loses is the bright citrus-pine opening that makes the material distinctive. The heart and base survive longer, so the material becomes sweeter and flatter rather than dead. That is why old stock can still be sold as frankincense without anyone lying — it is frankincense, just not the frankincense the buyer imagines.

Follow the decline: warmth and air → the light terpenes leave and oxidise → the opening flattens → the lump still melts, now sweet and dull → the material is older in effect while looking untouched. Storage is a quality decision, and the [storage and longevity guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) sets out the general rules that resin punishes hardest.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The risks are smoke and particulate exposure, hot surfaces and charcoal, and the temptation to treat a traditional medicine as a treatment — none of them is addressed by choosing a "natural" product.

Any burning aromatic produces smoke and fine particles. Burn in a well-ventilated room, keep the burner on a heatproof surface away from curtains and children, and do not inhale concentrated smoke directly. Charcoal discs produce carbon monoxide as well as particulate matter, so they need more ventilation than an electric heater, and they should never be used in a sealed room. People with asthma or other respiratory sensitivity should be cautious with any smoke, and pets should be able to leave the room.

Essential oil is a different exposure route with different rules. Do not swallow frankincense oil, do not apply it undiluted to skin, and treat oxidised oils as more likely to irritate rather than less. None of this page is medical advice, and nothing here supports using frankincense — burned, applied or ingested — to treat a condition. The site's [safety guide](/safety/) and the [incense smoke and air quality evidence](/blog/incense-smoke-air-quality-evidence/) note cover the general case.

## What this page does not claim

**Direct answer:** This page does not claim that frankincense treats disease, that "natural" means safe, that a grade name proves origin, or that burning resin delivers boswellic acids.

Four boundaries are worth stating, because each is crossed routinely in writing about this resin. Traditional use in Chinese materia medica is a record of practice, and laboratory interest in boswellic acids is a research programme; neither is a clinical finding, and none of this is advice to use the resin for a health condition. "Natural" describes origin, not risk: resin smoke is smoke, and charcoal adds carbon monoxide to the plume. Grade names describe appearance and trade convention, not laboratory results, so "hojari" is a claim about style and origin that only a known supplier — or a paid analysis — can support. And the compounds that interest pharmacologists are not the ones that rise in the smoke, so burning is a way to smell a resin, not a way to take a medicine.

## Chinese terms used in this entry

**Direct answer:** The Chinese vocabulary around 乳香 comes from two different systems — the incense trade and the medical materia medica — and the same characters can carry a technical meaning that English paraphrases lose.

| Term | Pinyin | Literal sense | How it is used here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 乳香 | rǔxiāng | milk fragrance | The material itself; named for the pale exudate |
| 没药 | mòyào | — (a transliteration) | Myrrh; the habitual partner of 乳香 |
| 香药 | xiāngyào | aromatic medicine | Imported aromatics handled as both scent and drug |
| 香方 | xiāngfāng | incense formula | A written recipe for a blended incense |
| 香乘 | xiāngchéng | incense record | The Ming compendium of incense materials and recipes |
| 本草綱目 | běncǎo gāngmù | outline of materia medica | The pharmacopoeia that records 乳香 among imports |
| 活血 | huóxuè | invigorate the blood | Traditional therapeutic language, not a clinical claim |
| 止痛 | zhǐtòng | stop pain | Traditional therapeutic language, not a clinical claim |
| 君臣佐使 | jūn chén zuǒ shǐ | ruler, minister, assistant, envoy | The four-role blending frame borrowed from medicine |
| 沉香 | chénxiāng | sinking fragrance | Agarwood, the canonical base wood |
| 檀香 | tánxiāng | sandalwood | The creamy aromatic wood often paired with resins |
| 熏香 | xūnxiāng | fumigating fragrance | The act of scenting a space with heat or smoke |

The distinction that matters most is between 香药 and 香方. The first names a category of goods — aromatics that arrived through trade and were handled by physicians as well as perfumers. The second names a practice — the written formula, with proportions and a sequence of operations. A material can be a 香药 without appearing in any 香方, and that is precisely where 乳香 sits in some periods: present in the pharmacopoeia, unevenly present in the recipe books.

Reading the characters also clears up a translation problem. English "incense" covers both the material and the act, while Chinese separates 香 (the aromatic substance) from 熏香 (the fumigation) and 香方 (the formula). When a Chinese source says a material "enters the incense", the sentence is usually about a formula, not about a category of goods.

The same character heads a family of compound words that name formats and equipment, and a reader moving between Chinese sources and English trade vocabulary needs them:

| Term | Pinyin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 线香 | xiànxiāng | Extruded incense stick |
| 盘香 | pánxiāng | Coil, formed as a spiral |
| 塔香 | tǎxiāng | Cone, shaped like a small pagoda |
| 香篆 | xiāngzhuàn | Stamped seal of powder, burned as a trail |
| 香粉 | xiāngfěn | Milled incense powder |
| 香丸 | xiāngwán | Rolled pill or ball of blended aromatics |
| 香材 | xiāngcái | Raw aromatic material before blending |
| 香炉 | xiānglú | Censer or burner |
| 香灰 | xiānghuī | Ash bed that insulates the heat |
| 香炭 | xiāngtàn | Incense charcoal |
| 香席 | xiāngxí | A formal gathering arranged around incense |

Two of these are worth separating from the English habit of translation. 香篆 is not a stick and not a cone: it is loose powder pressed into a continuous trail, and its appeal is the slow, linear burn and the pattern itself. 香席 is not a product but an occasion — a seated gathering with a sequence, a host and a judged aroma, closer to a tea ceremony than to lighting a stick in a room. Neither has a clean one-word English equivalent, which is why the Chinese terms are more useful than a forced translation.

## Summary

Frankincense is a dried exudate, not a timber, and everything practical follows from that: it melts before it burns, it can be distilled into a top-note oil, and it needs a binder when it goes into a stick. Five species carry the trade — *B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, *B. frereana*, *B. papyrifera*, *B. serrata* — and the differences between them show up in the first thirty seconds of heating, which is why a species name on the label is worth more than a colour photograph. In Chinese sources 乳香 arrives as an import, is discussed beside 没药, and is described in the vocabulary of 活血 and 止痛; that vocabulary is a historical record, not a clinical finding. The boswellic acids that attract laboratory attention are heavy, non-volatile molecules that remain in the residue rather than rising in the plume.

- **Species first, grade second.** A beautiful tear of the wrong species is still the wrong material for the blend.
- **Temperature is the whole game.** Below pyrolysis the resin reads citrus-bright; above it, the smoke reads tarry and the top note is gone.
- **Buy the form you will use.** Tears for heating, powder for manufacturing, oil for perfumery — the same resin, three different economics.
- **Keep the claims small.** Tradition, trade convention and laboratory interest are three different kinds of statement, and none of them is a medical claim.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between frankincense and myrrh?** Two genera, one family, opposite temperaments. 乳香 is the bright one — lemon peel, pine, a resin that melts into clarity — while 没药 is the dark one, bitter and medicinal, with a weight that sits low in a blend. Chinese physicians named them as a pair and Mediterranean worshippers burned them as a pair, which says they were used together, not that they were alike.

**Is frankincense a wood?** No — and the point is practical, not pedantic. What you buy is a dried exudate from the bark, made of resin, gum and a little volatile oil. Timber has fibres and burns as a solid; this material has none, and it liquefies on a warm surface long before it could char.

**How should frankincense be burned?** With as little flame as possible. A heater held somewhere between 150 and 220 °C volatilises the aromatic fraction without cracking it, so the lemon-and-pine opening survives; a charcoal disc runs several hundred degrees hotter at the contact point and gives the smoky, tarry reading that many people associate with churches. Both are legitimate, but they are not the same fragrance. Ventilate either way, and never leave a disc unattended.

**Is pale frankincense always better?** No. Pale, translucent tears are the classic high grade in the Omani trade, and they are genuinely pleasant to handle, but colour describes sorting and appearance rather than chemistry. A pale lot that has been stored badly can smell flatter than a darker lot from a fresh harvest. For grinding, appearance matters even less, because the material is milled before use.

**Do boswellic acids survive burning?** They stay behind. The acids are bulky pentacyclic triterpenoids, and bulk is the enemy of volatility: the molecules that leave a warm lump are the small terpenes, while the acid fraction remains in the residue. What the plume carries is a mixture of monoterpenes, pyrolysis fragments and fine particles, none of which is a boswellic acid. The smoke is therefore not a dose of the thing the research papers study, and it is still smoke.

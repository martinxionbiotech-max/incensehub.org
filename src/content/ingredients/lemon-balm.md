> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm (香蜂草, xiāngfēngcǎo) is *Melissa officinalis*, a hardy Lamiaceae perennial whose native range runs from southern Europe through the Mediterranean. Bruised leaves give off a soft, green lemon scent resting on citral and citronellal. The material belongs to modern herbalism and perfumery; it has no place in the classical Chinese incense repertoire.

## What the plant is

> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm is a soft-stemmed perennial herb grown in gardens and herb plots for its leaf. It dies back in winter and returns from the root, and it spreads readily enough to be treated as a vigorous ground cover in mild climates.

The plant grows in loose clumps of square stems, a shape it shares with mint, basil and the rest of the Lamiaceae. Leaves sit in opposite pairs, are ovate with scalloped edges, and carry the scent in tiny glands on the underside. A light rub is enough to rupture them; the smell arrives before the leaf is even picked.

Flowering runs through the warm half of the year. The small flowers are creamy white rather than the mauve of most mints, and they are unusually attractive to bees — a trait that gave the plant both its Latin and its Chinese names. Gardeners often leave a patch to flower for exactly that reason.

Two habits matter for anyone handling the material. The first is that the scent collapses quickly after picking: the leaf is thin, the glands are superficial, and the volatile fraction leaves with the moisture. The second is that the plant is a vigorous self-sower, so a "wild" patch may be garden escape rather than native stand. Neither point is a defect, but both shape how the herb is harvested and traded.

## The botanical record

> **Direct answer:** Flora of China records *Melissa officinalis* under the name 香蜂花 as a cultivated plant, native to southwestern Asia and Europe, with erect branched stems, ovate leaves and a creamy-white corolla.

The morphological description is precise enough to be useful for identification. Stems are erect, pyramidally branched and pubescent. Leaves measure 1–5 (–6.5) × 0.8–4 (–5) cm, ovate, with a serrate margin. The corolla is creamy white and 1.2–1.3 cm long, and flowering falls between June and August.

Flora of China also records the plant's uses in terms that are worth quoting because they summarise the material's whole commercial life: "used for flavoring salads, soups, and liqueurs; the oil is employed in perfumery; commonly known as balm tea." That single line covers the leaf as food, the oil as fragrance, and the infusion as a domestic drink.

The record treats the plant as cultivated in China rather than indigenous, which is the botanical half of the provenance question. A herb can be grown in a country for a century and still not belong to that country's classical materia medica. Cultivation is not canonisation.

The full treatment sits at [Melissa officinalis in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200019809), and the taxonomic record is at [GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/5341501).

## Why the Chinese name is about bees

> **Direct answer:** 香蜂草 breaks down as 香 "fragrant", 蜂 "bee" and 草 "herb", and the Flora of China variant 香蜂花 swaps "herb" for "flower". Both names describe a plant that bees work heavily, and neither carries any classical aromatic pedigree.

The naming logic is unusually transparent. 香 marks the scent, 蜂 marks the visitors, and the final character says whether the namer was thinking of the whole herb or the bloom. The Latin genus does the same job in Greek: *Melissa* is the word for honey bee, chosen because the flowers are a reliable nectar source.

That leaves a useful distinction for readers. A name that describes a plant's ecology is not a name that describes its place in a pharmacological or liturgical tradition. 香蜂草 says "fragrant bee plant"; it does not say "ingredient of the incense classics". The two kinds of name look similar on a label and mean very different things.

There is also a small translation trap. English "balm" is an old word for a balsam or healing ointment, and it has dragged a therapeutic implication into the plant's common name for centuries. "Lemon balm" does not mean the herb is a balm in any pharmacological sense; it means the herb was once grouped with soothing garden plants. The label carries history, not evidence.

## A lemon that is not a citrus

> **Direct answer:** The scent is lemony, but the plant is a mint. Lemon balm sits in Lamiaceae, not Rutaceae, and its lemon note is a chemical coincidence rather than a family relationship.

Smell the crushed leaf with that in mind and the differences become obvious. There is no rind, no zest, no sharp acid edge. What arrives is a soft lemon wrapped in green: the smell of lemonade made with too little fruit and a lot of garden. Under it sits a herbaceous, slightly hay-like body that no citrus has.

The softness is structural. Citrus peel oil is dominated by limonene, a hydrocarbon that reads bright and solvent-sharp. Lemon balm oil has little of that; its lemon comes from oxygenated aldehydes, which sit lower on the brightness scale and feel rounder. That is why the herb can be described as "lemony" and still be useless as a lemon substitute in a formula.

This is also why the material divides readers. People who expect citrus find it muddy; people who expect a green herb find it charming. The plant is neither, and the disappointment usually comes from the expectation rather than the oil.

## The chemistry of a borrowed note

> **Direct answer:** Citral and citronellal supply the lemon character, with citral itself being two related aldehydes, geranial and neral. Minor sesquiterpenes such as caryophyllene give the oil its green-herbal weight.

Citral is not a single molecule. It is the name for a pair of geometric isomers: geranial, the sharper and more lemon-like of the two, and neral, which is softer and slightly sweeter. Their ratio shapes the top of the oil, and it shifts with harvest timing, drying and storage.

Citronellal adds a rounded, slightly rosy-lemony note and is the reason lemon balm can smell closer to citronella than to lemon. In a good oil the two sit together with the green fraction, and the result is a scent that reads "leaf" before it reads "fruit".

The green weight comes from sesquiterpenes. Caryophyllene and related compounds are heavier, slower to evaporate, and they supply the herbal base that keeps the lemon from flying away. This is the structural difference from a citrus oil: grapefruit or lemon is a top note with a thin body, while lemon balm is a top note with a herbal floor under it.

The practical consequence is tenacity. A lemon balm note lasts longer on a strip than a lemon note at similar strength, and it lasts longer again when the material is a dried leaf rather than an oil. The lemon reads softer and the green reads stronger as time passes.

## Five plants, one adjective

> **Direct answer:** "Lemon-scented" describes a chemistry, not a family. Lemon balm, lemon, lemongrass, citronella and mint reach similar registers from three different plant families, and buyers who order by adjective rather than species get whatever the seller had.

| Material | Family | Lemon driver | Character | Structural role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lemon balm (香蜂草) | Lamiaceae | citral, citronellal | soft, green, herbal | top note with a herbal floor |
| Lemon (柠檬) | Rutaceae | limonene, citral | sharp, zesty, acidic | bright top, thin body |
| Lemongrass | Poaceae | citral | coarse, grassy, intense | strong top, cheap volume |
| Citronella | Poaceae | citronellal, citronellol | rosy-lemony, soapy | top to heart, repellent association |
| Mint (薄荷) | Lamiaceae | menthol | cool, sharp, camphoraceous | cooling top, dominates blends |

Read the table down the "lemon driver" column and the pattern is clear. Three unrelated families converged on citral and citronellal, most likely because the biosynthetic routes for these terpenoids are widespread in plants and the compounds are strongly detectable to animals. Evolution repeated the trick; the marketplace then blurred the results.

The mint row is the useful control. Mint shares the family with lemon balm but not the chemistry, and it smells nothing like lemon. Family predicts botany; chemistry predicts scent. A buyer who confuses the two will expect lemon balm to behave like mint in a blend and be surprised when it disappears instead of dominating.

For sourcing, the table is a warning about adjectives. A drum labelled "lemon herb oil" could be any of the first four rows. The species name, or a composition report, is the only thing that settles it. ## Why the leaf smells stronger when bruised

> **Direct answer:** The aroma lives in surface glands, and it is released by damage. Crushing the leaf breaks the gland walls, the oil meets air, and the light aldehydes reach the nose within seconds.

The sequence is easy to follow. A leaf is bent → the cuticle and the gland walls rupture → the oil spreads across the leaf surface → the most volatile aldehydes evaporate first → the nose gets a concentrated lemon burst → within a minute the burst fades to a green, tea-like smell as the light fraction leaves and the heavier sesquiterpenes remain.

That is why a lemon balm leaf smells strongest in the hand and weakest in the bag. It also explains a common disappointment with dried herb: the water that carried the aroma has gone, the gland structure has collapsed, and much of the citral has either evaporated or oxidized. What is left is pleasant but muted, closer to hay than to lemon.

Heat accelerates the same process. A leaf dropped into hot water releases its volatiles into the steam, which is why a covered cup of lemon balm tea smells far stronger than an open one. The aroma has not been created by the water; it has been moved from leaf to air, and it keeps moving until the cup is cold.

## Growing and harvesting: why two leaf samples differ

> **Direct answer:** The leaf's chemistry follows the plant's stage of growth. Leaf cut before flowering is the most aromatic, leaf cut after flowering is more abundant and more herbaceous, and the gap between the two explains most of the variation between samples.

Lemon balm is an easy plant to grow and a fussy one to grow well. It tolerates partial shade, prefers moist but drained soil, and dies back to the root in cold winters before returning in spring. In mild climates it self-sows freely and can move around a garden without help, which is charming in a herb bed and troublesome in a border.

The harvest decision is the one that affects the material. A plant allowed to flower puts its energy into stem and bloom, and the leaf becomes coarser and more hay-like. Cutting the stems back before flowering forces a fresh flush of soft leaf and keeps the clump compact. Growers who want aroma cut early and often; growers who want biomass wait.

Post-harvest handling matters as much as the cutting. Leaf piled warm in a bag heats up, and heat drives off the very volatiles the harvest was for. The practical rule is to move the leaf into shade or into the drier within the hour, spread thin rather than heaped, and keep it out of direct sun.

Propagation is by seed, division or cuttings. Division every few years renews a clump that has gone woody in the centre, and it is the cheapest way to keep a garden supply consistent. None of this is exotic horticulture, but it explains why a garden leaf and a commercial drum can differ so much: the same species, handled on different clocks.

## Sachets, linen and the pre-industrial leaf

> **Direct answer:** Dried lemon balm was a domestic aromatic before it was a fragrance material: bundled into sachets, laid among linen, and hung in rooms. The mechanism is passive evaporation, and it is the one use where the leaf outperforms the oil.

A sachet works by slow release. The dried leaf sits in a cloth bag → air moves through the weave → the light aldehydes evaporate a little at a time → the enclosed space takes on a soft lemon-hay smell → the effect fades over weeks as the volatile fraction is spent. No heat and no combustion are involved, which is why the note stays green rather than scorched.

The oil is poor at this job. A drop on cloth is far too concentrated at first and then gone, and the concentrated phase can stain or leave an oily patch. The leaf is dilute by nature, and dilution is the point.

European household practice treated the herb as part of the linen cupboard and the sickroom, and the association with clean cloth is why the plant's name carries a domestic rather than a perfumery tone. That history is worth keeping separate from any health claim: a sachet scents a drawer, and nothing more is established here.

In practice the technique has a modern use for incense makers. A small bag of dried lemon balm in a box of finished sticks adds a green note to the cold throw without adding oil to the paste, and it does not affect the burn. It is a finishing touch rather than a formulation, and it is easy to overdo.

## From leaf to oil

> **Direct answer:** The leaf is harvested, dried or distilled fresh, and the oil is recovered by steam or water distillation. Low oil yield and a delicate top make the material expensive, and the price is the root of most quality problems in the trade.

Harvest timing is the first decision. Leaf cut before flowering carries the highest citral content; leaf cut after flowering is more abundant and more herbaceous. Which is "right" depends on the product: a tea wants leaf, a fragrance wants the highest-yield aromatic window, and a nursery wants neither.

Drying is the second. Slow drying at ambient temperature preserves colour and much of the top; fast drying with heat saves time and costs aroma. The visual signal is the leaf colour — a good dried lemon balm is muted green, not brown, and it should still smell of lemon when a handful is crushed.

Distillation is the third. Steam carries the volatiles out of the leaf, the condensate separates, and the oil is drawn off. The oil is pale yellow to greenish, and its smell is much more concentrated than the leaf's, with the citral sharpened and the green fraction pushed back. Fresh leaf and dried leaf give different oils, which is one reason two samples with the same species name can smell unlike each other.

An absolute or concrete can be produced with solvent extraction, and it carries more of the heavy, waxy leaf character. For incense and perfumery the distilled oil is the common form; the absolute is a specialty.

## The adulteration problem

> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm oil is expensive because the plant yields little of it, and cheap citral-rich oils smell close enough to invite substitution. The result is a market where the label and the contents diverge more often than in most botanical materials.

Follow the economics and the fraud becomes predictable. A low-yielding herb → a high price per kilo → a buyer looking for a cheaper source → a supplier with lemongrass or citronella oil that shares the main aldehydes → a blend that passes a casual nose and fails a composition test. The chain needs no villain, only a price gap and an untested purchase.

Lemongrass is the usual extender because its citral content is high and its cost is low. Citronella brings citronellal and a soapy edge. Lemon oil brings limonene, which lemon balm lacks, and a trained nose can pick that up as an unnatural brightness at the top.

The defenses are unglamorous. A composition report from a laboratory is the only reliable check on identity, and it should show the expected citral ratio along with the minor components that a blend would not bother to reproduce. A price that sits far below the market band for genuine oil is information in itself. And a supplier who can name the origin, the harvest and the distillation run is more likely to be selling a single material than a compound.

There is a boundary to state. Adulteration is not the same as reconstitution, and a deliberately composed "lemon balm type" fragrance is a legitimate product when it is sold as one. The problem is the label that says *Melissa officinalis* on a drum that does not contain it.

## Reading a melissa label

> **Direct answer:** Most lemon balm products on the market fall into five categories, and the label usually names the category only indirectly. Knowing the five makes the price list legible.

| Label | What it should mean | How it reads | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Melissa officinalis* oil | distilled from the named species | soft lemon, green, tea-like base | composition report; citral ratio |
| Melissa absolute | solvent-extracted leaf | deeper, waxy, hay-like | solvent residue declaration |
| "Lemon balm type" | composed accord | even, thin, no green base | sold as a type, not a species |
| Dried leaf | leaf for tea or sachet | muted lemon, hay | colour, stem content, mustiness |
| Fresh leaf | culinary herb | bright, green, fleeting | cold chain; use within days |

The first two are single-material products with different extraction histories. The third is a fragrance construct and should be labelled as such; when it is, it is honest and useful. The last two are leaf products whose quality is judged by appearance and smell rather than by a certificate.

A practical test for the oil: place a drop on a strip and smell at ten minutes and at two hours. A genuine oil keeps a green, tea-like base after the lemon lifts; a lemongrass-extended oil goes flat and grassy, and a citronella-extended oil leaves a soapy trace. The test is not proof, but it is cheap, and it catches the crude substitutions.

## Dried leaf and oil are different materials

> **Direct answer:** The leaf and the oil share a name and a species but not a use. Leaf is a culinary and tea material with a soft, fugitive scent; oil is a concentrated fragrance stock with a sharp top and a herbal floor.

| | Dried leaf | Essential oil |
|---|---|---|
| Concentration | low; grams of leaf per cup | high; kilograms of leaf per gram |
| Character | hay, muted lemon, green | sharp citral, green, tea-like base |
| Stability | months in a sealed jar, fading | years if cold and full, oxidizing slowly |
| Main use | tea, cooking, sachets | perfumery, home fragrance, incense |
| Failure mode | mustiness, dust, colour loss | oxidation, adulteration, evaporation |

The concentration gap is enormous, and it explains a frequent error. A formulator who substitutes dried leaf for oil at the same weight will get a faint, hay-like result and conclude the material is weak. It is not weak; it is dilute, and the two forms cannot be swapped by weight.

The reverse error is more dangerous. Treating the oil as if it were the leaf leads people to use it at culinary concentrations, which is both wasteful and a handling risk. The oil is a concentrate; the leaf is a food.

For incense, the distinction decides the technique. Leaf can be included in a loose blend as a minor aromatic, where it contributes a soft hay-lemon note and burns poorly. Oil is added to a paste or a dip, where it contributes a top note and evaporates early.

## How it behaves in a blend

> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm is a soft top note that behaves more like a green herb than like a citrus. It lifts the opening, softens sharp edges and then steps back, which makes it a good bridge and a poor lead.

The pairing logic follows from the chemistry. With mint it moderates the menthol, which otherwise flattens everything around it. With bergamot it reinforces the citrus register while adding a green, tea-like softness that bergamot alone lacks. With basil it builds an aromatic-herbal accord that reads culinary rather than perfumed. With chamomile or lavender it sits in the same soft-green family and disappears politely.

Dosage is where the material earns its reputation for being difficult. The oil is not strong in the way that mint or clove is strong; it is quiet, and a formulator's instinct is to add more. Past a certain point the citral turns soapy and the green base turns hay-like, and the blend smells like a herb pillow rather than a perfume.

The judgment boundary is worth stating. Lemon balm does not anchor a blend, does not fix a top note, and does not survive a heavy base without support. It is a modifier, and the practical significance is that it should be dosed as one — a few percent in a formula, not a pillar.

## How it behaves in incense

> **Direct answer:** Dried lemon balm burns poorly on its own and is used as a minor aromatic in loose blends. The oil works as a surface top note, and in both forms the lemon impression is strongest before and just after ignition.

The combustion chain explains the limits. Leaf is mostly cellulose and water → the cellulose burns hot and fast → the smoke carries scorched-plant notes → the citral, which is volatile, leaves ahead of the flame → the room smells of burnt hay with a ghost of lemon. Nothing in that sequence is a fault of the herb; it is what a low-resin leaf does when it meets fire.

The oil behaves the way citrus oils do on a stick. The ember advances → heat preheats the paste ahead of the burn line → the aldehydes flash off before combustion → the lemon arrives early and briefly → the base materials carry the rest of the burn. A lemon balm stick is therefore a cold-throw experience as much as a burning one.

Three practical responses follow. Keep the leaf fraction small in a loose blend, where it contributes texture and a hay note rather than the lead. Use the oil at a low percentage on a base that can carry it, since it cannot carry itself. And judge the result cold as well as lit, because a good part of the experience happens in the box.

## Storage and the slow souring of citral

> **Direct answer:** Citral oxidizes, and the change reads as stale, cardboard-like and eventually sharp in an unpleasant way. Cold, dark, full containers slow it; nothing stops it.

The chemistry is a familiar one for aldehydes. Oxygen attacks the citral → it forms oxidation products with a heavier, duller smell → the bright lemon top is replaced by a waxy, stale note → the green base survives longer and starts to dominate. A two-year-old oil can be perfectly genuine and still smell tired.

Dried leaf fails on a different clock. Moisture is the enemy: leaf that picks up damp develops mustiness, and mustiness is not a scent that can be blended away. A sealed jar in a dark cupboard is adequate; a paper bag on a sunny shelf is not.

There is a handling point that is easy to miss. Every time a bottle is opened, fresh air enters and the headspace exchanges. A large drum opened weekly for small draws will oxidize faster than the same volume split into small, full bottles. Decanting is not fussiness; it is the cheapest quality control available.

The boundary: oxidation is a quality issue, not a safety verdict, and a stale oil is not necessarily hazardous. It is simply no longer the material the formula was written for, and in a top-note-driven composition that difference is audible.

## Modern use, without the health claims

> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm is used as a tea herb, a culinary herb, a home-fragrance note and a perfumery accent. Its documented role is aromatic and culinary; no therapeutic claim is made here.

The tea tradition is the oldest continuous use. Fresh or dried leaf is infused, often with other garden herbs, and the drink is mild, green and lemony. The culinary uses recorded by Flora of China — salads, soups, liqueurs — reflect a European kitchen habit rather than a Chinese one.

Home fragrance and perfumery use the oil for the soft green-lemon effect described above. It appears in candles, diffusers and colognes, usually as a modifier rather than a headline. Its quietness makes it a natural supporting note in "green" and "herbal" accords.

The European herbal tradition supplies the historical colour. Lemon balm was a monastery-garden plant, and it appears in the Carmelite water of seventeenth-century France, a distilled spirit aromatised with lemon peel, nutmeg, coriander and balm. That is a documented use in a European aromatic preparation, and it says nothing about Chinese incense.

Aromatherapy is where the language needs care. The oil is used in diffuser blends and carrier-oil dilutions, and there is a large popular literature around it. This page does not repeat efficacy claims, because an ingredient reference cannot responsibly assert specific outcomes. What can be said is narrower: the oil has a defined composition, a defined sensory character and a defined set of handling risks.

## What history does and does not record

> **Direct answer:** The botanical identity, the European herbal tradition and the oil chemistry are documented. A classical Chinese incense role is not, and no medical effect is established here.

The documented layers are clear. *Melissa officinalis* is a Lamiaceae perennial native to southern Europe, the Mediterranean and southwestern Asia. Flora of China records it as cultivated in China, with a full morphological description and a note on its uses as food, fragrance and tea. The oil chemistry is characterised, with citral and citronellal as the principal lemon drivers.

The undocumented layer is the one marketing tends to invent. There is no classical Chinese 香材 entry for 香蜂草, no recipe in the Ming compendia, and no traditional Chinese incense role to describe. The plant's Chinese name is descriptive and modern, and its cultivation in China is a horticultural fact rather than a canonical one.

The evidence layer is narrower still. There is no clinical finding that this page can responsibly present as a health benefit, and none is claimed. Traditional belief about lemon balm as a calming herb exists in European folk practice; it is a belief with a long history, not a demonstrated effect, and it is labelled as such.

For a reader, the practical significance is that the herb does not need a forged pedigree. Its real history — a Mediterranean garden plant, a tea, a distilled water, a modern fragrance note — is more interesting than an invented classical one, and it is the history a buyer can actually verify.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** Incense smoke is a respiratory irritant regardless of the herb, and lemon balm oil is a concentrate that can sensitise skin. Ventilate, dilute, and keep both leaf and oil away from children and pets.

Combustion comes first. Burning any plant material produces fine particulate matter and combustion gases, and a small closed room concentrates them. Burn in a ventilated space, keep the stick clear of curtains and shelves, and do not read a pleasant smell as evidence of clean air.

The oil carries a second set of rules. Citral is one of the fragrance allergens that cosmetic labelling regimes require to be declared, which is a regulatory recognition that it can sensitise skin in some people. Oxidised citrus aldehydes are more likely to provoke a reaction than fresh ones, so an old bottle is both a sensory and a dermatological liability. Dilute for any skin contact, keep it out of eyes, and do not ingest it.

Pets and children deserve their own line. Cats are sensitive to many essential-oil constituents, and a diffuser running for hours in a closed room is a different exposure from a stick burned briefly with a window open. The safe default is ventilation, short sessions, and no direct exposure.

One scoping note. The herb is a culinary one, and its leaf has a long food history; that does not extend to the oil, which is a concentrate with a different risk profile. Nothing on this page is medical advice. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general framework.

## An editorial view: "lemon" is a molecule, not a species

> **Direct answer:** The most common mistake about lemon balm is treating the word "lemon" as a botanical claim. It is a description of a smell, and the smell is produced by chemistry that several unrelated plants happen to share.

Three judgments follow, and they are editorial rather than documentary.

The first is that aromatic convergence is normal, not a curiosity. Plants in different families arrive at the same volatile compounds because those compounds do useful things — deterring herbivores, attracting pollinators, signalling damage. The result is a vocabulary of smells that cuts across taxonomy, and a marketplace that names materials by that vocabulary rather than by their family. "Lemon" is one such word, and treating it as a species marker is the root of a great deal of confusion.

The second is that a quiet material is not a weak one. Lemon balm's oil is subtle, and subtlety reads as low quality to a buyer who is used to measuring strength. In practice the opposite is often true: a material that can be smelled at low concentration is doing more work per gram than a loud one, and its failure mode is disappearing rather than overpowering.

The third is that provenance claims deserve the same scrutiny as composition claims. A seller who invents a classical Chinese lineage for a Mediterranean herb is telling you how they treat evidence, and that habit rarely stops at the marketing copy. The reverse is also true: a supplier who says plainly "this is a European herb, here is the country of origin, here is the distillation date" is giving you something you can check.

What follows for the encyclopedia is a rule I would apply to every quiet botanical: name the species, name the chemistry, name the limit, and resist the urge to inflate the history. A reader who wants a classical Chinese citrus note has citron, finger citron and pomelo peel. A reader who wants a soft green lemon has this herb, and it is enough.

## Summary

Lemon balm (香蜂草) is *Melissa officinalis*, a Lamiaceae perennial from southern Europe and the Mediterranean, recorded by Flora of China as cultivated in China under the name 香蜂花. Its leaf carries a soft, green lemon scent built on citral and citronellal, a chemistry it shares with lemongrass and citronella rather than with citrus. The oil is low-yielding, expensive and frequently extended, so composition reports matter more than labels. No classical Chinese incense role exists, and none is claimed. In practice the material is a quiet top note with a herbal floor: best as a modifier, weakest as a lead, and most itself when the leaf is fresh.

## FAQ

**What is lemon balm (香蜂草)?** A herbaceous perennial in the mint family, *Melissa officinalis*, whose native range covers southern Europe and the Mediterranean; it is grown widely in gardens and picked for tea. Crushed leaves smell softly of lemon, and the distilled oil is a fragrance material.

**Did lemon balm ever belong to the Chinese incense tradition?** No. It is a European herb, recorded in China as a cultivated introduction, and it has no entry in the classical 香材 literature. The Chinese name describes a bee-friendly aromatic plant; it is not a term inherited from the incense canon.

**Why does lemon balm smell of lemon if it is not a citrus?** Because scent is chemistry, not taxonomy. The herb produces citral and citronellal, the same aldehydes that give lemongrass and lemon peel their lemon character. Three different plant families arrived at the same compounds, so the smell converges while the plants do not.

**Is lemon balm the same as lemongrass?** No. They share a lemon note but not a family: lemon balm is a Lamiaceae herb with a soft, green, tea-like character, while lemongrass is a Poaceae grass with a coarser, stronger, more uniform citral profile. Lemongrass is also much cheaper, which is why it turns up in oils sold as melissa.

**Can I burn dried lemon balm as incense?** You can, but it burns poorly. The dried leaf is mostly cellulose with little resin, so it scorches and produces a hay-like smoke with only a trace of lemon. The oil is the practical fragrance form, and even then it works as a top note rather than a base.

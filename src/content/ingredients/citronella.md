**Technical answer:** 香茅 (xiāngmáo) is the fragrant leaf of *Cymbopogon citratus*, the grass English speakers call lemongrass — a tropical Poaceae species grown for cooking, for scent, and for the oil pressed out of its leaves. The English word *citronella*, though, belongs properly to two close relatives, *Cymbopogon nardus* and *C. winterianus*, which supply most of the oil sold as citronella. The two are different plants with different chemistry: lemongrass is citral-dominant and sharply lemony, while the citronella grasses are richer in citronellal and geraniol and smell rounder, greener, and faintly rosy. This page covers the 香茅 material used in incense and keeps that distinction explicit, because most of the confusion in this category starts with the label.

## Two grasses, one English word

> **Direct answer:** 香茅 is lemongrass, *Cymbopogon citratus*. Citronella is *Cymbopogon nardus* or *C. winterianus*. Both are *Cymbopogon* grasses with a citrus smell, and they are not the same species, the same oil, or the same product.

The naming problem is not a small technicality; it changes what a buyer is holding. Lemongrass is a culinary herb, sold in bunches at a vegetable stall and dried for tea. Citronella is an industrial aromatic crop, grown to be distilled, and its oil is the active ingredient in a large share of the world's mosquito-repellent candles, coils, and sprays. A formulator who orders "citronella" expecting the sharp lemony lift of 香茅 may receive an oil that is greener, sweeter, and rosier, with a fraction of the citral.

| Species | Chinese name | Usual English | Oil character | Typical product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *C. citratus* | 香茅, 柠檬香茅 | Lemongrass (West Indian) | Citral-dominant; sharp, clean lemon | Fresh herb, dried leaf, lemongrass oil |
| *C. flexuosus* | 芸香草 (regional) | East Indian lemongrass | Citral-dominant; similar, often coarser | Lemongrass oil |
| *C. nardus* | 亚香茅 | Citronella grass (Sri Lanka) | Citronellal and geraniol; greener, rosier | Citronella oil |
| *C. winterianus* | 爪哇香茅 | Java citronella | Citronellal-rich; the main oil crop | Citronella oil |
| *C. martini* | 玫瑰草 | Palmarosa | Geraniol-dominant; rose-like | Palmarosa oil |

Read the table left to right and the trade logic becomes clear. The two citral grasses are interchangeable in a kitchen and close in a perfume. The two citronellal grasses are the raw material for repellent products. Palmarosa sits off to one side, sharing the genus and almost none of the lemon character.

This page keeps the 香茅 focus, because that is the material that reaches incense makers as leaf and powder. Where the word citronella appears below, it refers to the oil grasses, and the distinction is stated.

## What the plant is

> **Direct answer:** *Cymbopogon citratus* is a clumping, shortly rhizomatous perennial grass. It forms dense tufts of culms, which Flora of China records as reaching up to 2 m, with glaucous leaf blades in the range of 30–90 cm. The leaf is the crop; the root, the stem, and the flower are not.

The habit explains the harvest. Lemongrass grows as a tight clump rather than a spreading lawn, so a grower can cut whole tillers close to the ground and the clump regrows from the base. The leaves are long, narrow, and stiff enough to stand upright, with a pale, slightly blue-green surface — the glaucous bloom that Flora of China notes. The aromatic oil sits in the leaf tissue, not in a resin duct or a heartwood, and it is present from the base of the blade to the tip.

That has three consequences for anyone buying the material:

- **The leaf is perishable in a way wood is not.** Cut leaf loses moisture and volatiles quickly, and the loss is not reversible.
- **The whole leaf counts.** Stems and sheaths carry far less oil than the blade. A lot with a high proportion of stem is not fraudulent, but it is diluted, and the dilution is invisible in a photograph.
- **Particle size is a decision, not a detail.** Grinding ruptures cells and releases oil onto the surface, where air can reach it. Fine powder smells wonderful in the bag and loses its top note sooner than a coarse cut.

Botanically, the plant is a grass of the Old World tropics. It is known mostly from cultivation, and its exact wild origin is uncertain — a detail worth flagging because suppliers often state a country of origin with more confidence than the botany supports. "Grown in Vietnam" is a supply-chain fact; "native to Vietnam" is a claim that needs a source.

## Three molecules that explain the category

> **Direct answer:** Citral gives the sharp lemon note of lemongrass. Citronellal gives the softer, greener citrus of citronella oil. Geraniol gives the rosy, waxy sweetness that both contain in different proportions. Learn these three names and most label arguments resolve themselves.

Aroma chemistry is usually presented as a list of unfamiliar compounds. For this category, three are enough.

**Citral** is not one molecule but a pair of isomers, geranial and neral, which occur together and read as lemon. It is the dominant volatile in lemongrass leaf and in lemongrass oil, and it is why the fresh herb smells the way it does. Citral is also reactive: the aldehyde group that makes it smell bright is the same group that lets it oxidise, which is why old lemongrass powder smells of hay rather than of lemon.

**Citronellal** is the signature of the citronella grasses. It reads as citrus with the sharpness rounded off — greener, slightly soapy, less obviously edible. A citronella oil with a high citronellal figure smells like a candle rather than a curry, which is exactly the impression the repellent trade wants.

**Geraniol** is a rose-scented alcohol. It appears in lemongrass at low levels and in citronella and palmarosa at much higher ones. It is the reason citronella oil has a floral shadow behind the citrus, and the reason a cheap blend can be pushed toward "citronella" by adding geraniol-rich material.

| Molecule | Sensory reading | Dominant in | Behaviour on storage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citral (geranial + neral) | Sharp, fresh lemon | Lemongrass leaf and oil | Oxidises to hay-like notes; heat and light accelerate it |
| Citronellal | Rounder green citrus | Citronella oil | More stable than citral, still volatile |
| Geraniol | Rosy, waxy, floral | Citronella, palmarosa | Relatively persistent; can dominate a stale blend |
| Limonene | Thin citrus peel | Many citrus and some grasses | Evaporates early; minor here |

**Mechanism:** citral exposed to air and light → the aldehyde oxidises → the bright lemon note flattens into a hay or linseed smell → the blend loses its opening and reads as dusty → storage, not dosage, becomes the limiting factor. This is the single most useful thing to know about lemongrass as an incense material: its best quality is also its least durable one.

## Why the leaf, not the wood

> **Direct answer:** Lemongrass has no heartwood, no resin, and no root aromatic worth using. The oil lives in the leaf blade, which means the material behaves like a herb in a formula, not like a wood or a resin.

Incense makers are trained by the wood and resin materials. Agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, and myrrh are dense, stable, and slow-releasing; they anchor a blend and tolerate long storage. A leaf is the opposite on every axis. It is light, it is bulky, it burns fast, it releases its volatiles early, and it degrades in months rather than years.

That difference has a mechanical consequence in production. A stick or cone is a composite: aromatic powder, a binder, water, and often a combustible base. Wood powders are dense and pack into a firm green stick. Leaf powders are light and springy, absorb water differently, and can make a soft, crumbly extrudate if the binder is not adjusted. The formulator's response is usually to treat lemongrass as an accent — a small share of the dry mass, chosen for the opening — rather than as a structural ingredient.

**Mechanism:** light leaf powder → low bulk density and high surface area → more binder and water needed to form a workable stick → a denser, slower-drying core → uneven drying and a higher risk of cracking → the batch needs a longer, gentler dry. None of that is a defect in the leaf; it is a difference in how the material must be handled. The practical [making sequence](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) changes at the drying stage, not at the blending stage.

## From field to powder

> **Direct answer:** The leaf is cut, wilted or dried, stripped, ground, and sieved. Each step trades moisture for fragility and volatiles for convenience, and the order matters: the gentler the dry and the cooler the grind, the more of the lemon note survives.

A working sequence for leaf material:

1. **Cut.** Whole tillers are taken close to the base, usually in the morning when the leaf is turgid.
2. **Wilt.** The cut grass loses water in a thin layer, out of direct sun. Wilting reduces bulk and makes stripping easier.
3. **Strip.** Leaf blades are separated from the tougher sheaths and stems. This is the step that sets the stem ratio in the finished lot.
4. **Dry.** Warm air, shade, or a low-temperature dryer. Fast high heat drives off the very volatiles the crop is grown for.
5. **Grind.** Milling generates heat. A hammer mill run hard will warm the powder and lose top notes; a cooler, slower grind preserves more.
6. **Sieve and store.** Particle size is set here, and so is the surface area that will be exposed to air for the rest of the material's life.

**Mechanism:** fresh leaf → mostly water → drying shrinks the tissue and concentrates the oil → but the same heat that drives off water also drives off citral → the dry matter rises while the aroma per gram falls → the target is a compromise between stability and scent, not maximum dryness. That is why a bone-dry, pale, dusty powder can be less aromatic than a slightly greener, coarser cut.

For a buyer, the process leaves fingerprints. A very uniform, very fine, very pale powder has usually been milled hard and dried hot. A coarser cut with visible green fragments has usually been treated more gently, and it will hold its opening longer in storage, though it will pack differently in a stick.

## What a lot of dried leaf tells you

> **Direct answer:** Judge colour, leaf-to-stem ratio, dust, aroma, and moisture, in that order of ease. None of these is a certified grade; together they predict how the material will behave in a blend better than any single number.

There is no official grading ladder for 香茅 leaf. The following scheme is a working receiving standard — an editorial tool, not an industry specification.

| Check | Good sign | Warning sign | What it predicts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | Dull green to olive, uneven | Pale straw or grey-brown | Over-dried or old; top notes gone |
| Leaf-to-stem ratio | Mostly blade, few sheaths | Stems and coarse sheaths dominate | Diluted oil; weaker opening |
| Dust fraction | Some fines, not a cloud | Heavy dust in the bag | Hard milling; fast volatile loss |
| Aroma | Sharp lemon when rubbed | Hay, dust, or nothing | Oxidation already underway |
| Moisture | Dry to the touch, cool | Clammy, clumping | Mould risk; poor storage |
| Foreign matter | None visible | Sand, twine, other leaf | Screening failure |

The aroma test is the one that matters most, and it needs a physical action: take a pinch, rub it between the palms, wait a few seconds, then smell. Rubbing ruptures cells and releases oil that the intact leaf was holding. A lot that smells of nothing when rubbed will smell of nothing in a stick.

Here is a judgment boundary. A stem-heavy lot is not automatically a cheat. Stems are part of the plant, and in some markets the whole chopped grass is what is sold. The problem arises when the price is set against a leaf-rich standard and the specification does not mention the ratio. In practice, ask for the ratio in writing; a supplier who cannot state it is not measuring it.

## How the scent behaves in a blend

> **Direct answer:** Lemongrass is a top note. It lifts the opening of a blend and then leaves, and no amount of extra dosage will turn it into a base. The formulator's job is to decide how much of the opening to give it, and what carries the scent after it has gone.

Perfumery's top-heart-base language is a rough model, and it is useful here because it predicts behaviour rather than describing poetry. A top note is made of small, volatile molecules that reach the nose quickly and dissipate quickly. Citral and citronellal are both in that class. A base note is made of heavier, slower molecules that persist. Lemongrass contains almost nothing in that class.

The consequence is a curve. Smell a fresh lemongrass stick and the first minute is vivid; the next ten are pleasant; after that the stick is mostly the binder and whatever else is in the formula. This is not a failure of quality. It is the physics of the material, and the reason a well-built citrus blend always pairs the top note with something that outlasts it.

| Partner | What it does for lemongrass | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood | Creamy, slow base that holds the citrus aloft | Can round off the sharpness too far |
| Cedar | Dry, structural base; keeps the blend from feeling sweet | A resinous cedar can fight the green note |
| Patchouli | Earthy anchor with a citrus-friendly top | Heavy doses swamp the leaf |
| Vetiver | Smoky-root base; extends the dry-down | Reads as "outdoor" rather than fresh |
| Frankincense | Resinous lift that bridges citrus and incense | Can dominate at low lemongrass doses |
| Mint or eucalyptus | Reinforces the fresh opening | Two top notes compete and both vanish faster |

The pairing logic is not a recipe. It is a way of asking what the blend is for. A summer room scent wants the citrus forward and a light base; a repellent-style coil wants the green note carried by smoke; a meditation blend probably wants lemongrass as a minor accent, because a sharp lemon top fights quiet resins.

## When a blend fails, read the symptom

> **Direct answer:** Most lemongrass failures announce themselves in one of five ways: a stick that smells strong cold and weak burning, a powder that smells of hay, a batch that cracks, a smoke that bites, or a scent that vanishes in the bag. Each points to a different cause, and none of them is fixed by adding more leaf.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| Strong raw, faint when burned | Volatiles driven off at the tip before they reach the nose; very fine powder | Coarser grind, lower aromatic share, cooler burn |
| Hay or linseed note | Citral oxidation in storage | Fresher lot, sealed dark pack, smaller orders |
| Sticks crack while drying | Leaf-heavy mix holding water unevenly | More binder, slower dry, less water in the batch |
| Smoke is harsh and acrid | Excess leaf powder, poor combustion, damp core | Reduce leaf share, check moisture, improve airflow |
| Scent gone within weeks | Permeable packaging, warm shelf | Barrier film, cool storage, shorter cycle |
| Batch-to-batch drift | Species change or stem ratio change | Written species and leaf-to-stem specification |

The first row is the one that surprises people. A stick can smell wonderful in the hand and deliver almost nothing when lit, because the heat front drives the most volatile fraction off ahead of the smoulder and much of it never reaches the smoker's nose. Adding more lemongrass makes the raw stick louder and the burned stick harsher without fixing the delivery. The answer is usually structural: a coarser particle, a slower burn, and a base that holds the opening in place long enough to be noticed.

The second row is a storage problem wearing a quality disguise. A powder that has turned hay-like was probably fine when it was milled. Blending it with fresh material does not restore the opening; it produces a louder hay note with a lemon shadow on top.

**Mechanism:** fine powder → fast volatilisation at the hot zone → most of the top note leaves before the smoke reaches the room → the smoker perceives binder and char → the formula is judged weak and dosed higher → the stick becomes harsher without becoming more aromatic. Dosage is the wrong lever when the problem is release.

## The repellent question

> **Direct answer:** Citronella oil has a real place in mosquito-repellent products, and a burning incense stick is not one of those products. Smoke may reduce landings nearby, but a smouldering stick does not deliver the controlled, tested dose of a topical repellent, and no incense maker should imply that it does.

This is where the category most often goes wrong, so it is worth being blunt. The repellent reputation belongs to the oil, and the oil is usually from *C. nardus* or *C. winterianus* — not from the culinary lemongrass on this page. Formulated topical products based on these oils have been studied, with results that vary by concentration, carrier, and test method; the effect is generally shorter-lived than synthetic repellents. That is a statement about formulated products applied to skin, not about a stick burning in a room.

A stick adds combustion. What reaches the room is a mixture of the aromatic volatiles that survive the burn, smoke, and fine particulate matter. Air movement carries it away; an open window removes it; a closed room accumulates smoke that is a respiratory irritant in its own right. The honest framing is that a citronella-style coil or stick is an outdoor ambiance product with a repellent tradition, not a personal protection device.

**Mechanism:** smouldering tip → pyrolysis of the leaf and binder → a fraction of the volatiles survives into the smoke → the plume disperses with air movement → concentration at any distance falls rapidly → protection, if any, is local and transient. That chain is why the product is used on a patio and not relied on in a bedroom.

For an incense page, the useful takeaway is this: sell the scent and the tradition, and do not borrow the efficacy language of a repellent. Buyers who expect a stick to do the work of a spray will be disappointed, and the disappointment is deserved.

## Storage and shelf life

> **Direct answer:** Lemongrass leaf and powder are degraded by light, heat, oxygen, and moisture, in roughly that order of speed. Sealed, dark, cool storage buys months; an open bag on a sunny shelf loses the opening in weeks.

The chemistry is the same oxidation chain described earlier, running in slow motion. Citral meets oxygen; the aldehyde turns into acids and other products that read as hay, linseed, or stale tea. Light accelerates the reaction, and heat accelerates it further. Moisture adds a second problem: mould, which can establish itself in a bag that feels dry at the surface.

Practical storage rules:

- **Keep it whole as long as possible.** Whole dried leaf has less exposed surface than powder. Grind what you need, when you need it.
- **Squeeze the air out.** A sealed bag with minimal headspace is better than a large container half full of air.
- **Keep it dark.** An opaque container or a closed cupboard, not a windowsill jar.
- **Keep it cool.** A stable cool store, not a warm production floor. Freezing is not a fix; condensation on thawing is worse than the slow loss.
- **Smell before you use.** A batch that has gone hay-like will not recover, and blending it with fresh material only dilutes both.

The package is part of the storage system, and it is where small buyers lose the most. A paper sack breathes; it lets moisture out and oxygen in, which is a reasonable trade for whole leaf sold within weeks and a poor one for powder held for months. A metallised barrier film or a sealed pail with a gasket slows the exchange in both directions, which is what a milled aromatic needs. Re-sealable pails are only an improvement if they are actually re-sealed; a lid left ajar on a production floor undoes the whole design.

**Mechanism:** milled powder → large surface area → oxygen and light reach the oil film on every particle → citral oxidises → the top note flattens → the powder is still "natural" and still green, and it no longer smells like lemongrass. Visual inspection cannot catch this; the nose can.

## In the kitchen and in the medicine cabinet

> **Direct answer:** Lemongrass is a defining culinary herb in Southeast Asia and a traditional aromatic in southern Chinese household use. Its medicinal reputation is traditional belief, not established clinical fact, and this page makes no health claims.

The culinary identity is the strongest and the least controversial. 香茅 appears in Thai tom yum and larb, in Vietnamese grilled dishes, and in southern Chinese cooking, where the whole stalk is bruised and simmered rather than eaten. The stalk is tough and fibrous; the flavour comes from the oil in the leaf sheaths, which is why recipes crush or bruise the base and then remove it before serving.

The domestic uses are equally practical. A bundle of leaf in a pot of hot water scents a room. Dried leaf goes into tea. The same lemony volatiles that make the herb pleasant in food are what make it useful around a house, and the leap from "smells clean" to "keeps insects away" is a short one for a household tradition to make.

The materia-medica layer needs a clear label. Chinese and Southeast Asian traditions record 香茅 among aromatic plants used for digestive and cooling purposes. Those are traditional uses recorded in traditional language. They are not clinical findings, and nothing on this page should be read as a treatment claim. The distinction matters because the repellent and medicinal reputations are the two places where this material is most often oversold.

## Adulteration and substitution

> **Direct answer:** Lemongrass is cheap enough that outright adulteration is uncommon; the real risks are species substitution and quiet dilution. The label is where the problem lives, not the chemistry.

Three substitutions recur:

1. **Citronella oil sold as lemongrass oil.** Both are *Cymbopogon* oils with a citrus smell. Citronella is greener and rosier, and it is not a culinary material.
2. **Lemongrass oil extended with citral.** Adding synthetic or isolated citral raises the "lemon" reading and lowers the cost. The result smells sharp but thin, without the grassy body.
3. **Leaf powder cut with stem, spent leaf, or other green matter.** The colour stays plausible; the aroma drops.

| Claim on the label | What it may actually be | How to tell |
|---|---|---|
| "Lemongrass oil, *Cymbopogon citratus*" | The citral grass | Sharp, clean lemon; species stated |
| "Citronella oil" | *C. nardus* or *C. winterianus* | Greener, rosier, less edible |
| "Lemongrass-citronella blend" | A mixture, often unspecified | Scent sits between the two; ask for the ratio |
| "Natural lemongrass fragrance" | Anything, including isolates | No species, no origin, no chemistry |
| "Pure 香茅 powder" | Leaf, possibly stem-heavy | Rub test; ask for leaf-to-stem ratio |

The general screening logic is set out in the [cross-reference on substitutions](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/), and it applies here without modification: name the species, name the part, name the form, and test the aroma physically.

## Buying checklist

> **Direct answer:** Confirm the species, the plant part, the form, and the aroma, in that order. A seller who can answer all four is a different kind of supplier from one who can only quote a price.

For an incense maker or a sourcing buyer:

- **Species.** Ask for *Cymbopogon citratus* in writing. If the answer is "citronella", ask which species, and expect *C. nardus* or *C. winterianus*.
- **Part.** Leaf, not whole chopped grass with a high stem fraction. Ask for the leaf-to-stem ratio.
- **Form.** Fresh, dried whole leaf, powder, or oil. Each has a different shelf life and a different behaviour in a blend.
- **Powder specification.** Particle size and moisture, stated against your process rather than against a generic ideal.
- **Aroma.** A rubbed sample, not a scent strip waved at a distance.
- **Consistency.** Ask how the supplier holds a specification across lots. A single good sample is a sample; a specification is a system.

One editorial caution about the word "pure". In aromatic materials, "pure" usually means "not blended with other named materials", not "tested" and not "traceable". It is a useful word and a weak guarantee. The [wholesale guide](/blog/wholesale-guide/) sets out the wider framework for vetting a supplier, and the [powder and resin guide](/blog/incense-powder-resin/) explains how a leaf powder differs from a resin in handling.

## What the oil specification does and does not tell you

> **Direct answer:** An oil specification states species, main volatile percentages, and a few physical constants. It describes composition, not quality, and it cannot tell you whether the still ran on fresh leaf or on leaf that had already given up its best material.

A typical document for a lemongrass or citronella oil will list the botanical name, the country of origin, the percentages of the principal volatiles, and physical values such as specific gravity, refractive index, and optical rotation. Each field has a purpose, and none of them is a grade.

- **Species and origin** are the identity fields. They matter more than everything else on the page, because they decide which category of material you have.
- **Citral, citronellal, geraniol** are the composition fields. A lemongrass oil should be citral-forward; a citronella oil should show citronellal and geraniol. A number outside the usual band is a question, not necessarily a fault.
- **Physical constants** are the consistency fields. They are cheap to measure and hard to fake across a whole batch, which makes them useful for catching a substitution between one oil and another.
- **What is missing** is usually the interesting part: leaf age, distillation date, storage history, and whether the oil was washed or fractionated.

**Mechanism:** leaf already stripped of some volatiles → lower oil yield per charge → the distiller runs longer or hotter to recover volume → heavier, less volatile material comes over with the oil → the colour darkens and the back note thickens → the headline citral figure can still look acceptable. The specification has recorded a composition while the sensory quality has quietly fallen. This is why a numbers-only purchasing habit fails on aromatic oils.

A second boundary is worth stating. A high citral figure does not prove a natural oil. Isolated citral is commercially available and can lift the number in a blend without adding the grassy body that the leaf contributes. The nose catches the difference before the certificate does: an extended oil smells sharp and thin, with the lemon note sitting on top of nothing.

The practical use of a specification, then, is to confirm identity and consistency, not to rank quality. Keep a retained sample from each lot, smell it against the previous one, and treat the document as the frame around the sensory judgment rather than a replacement for it.

## Growing it, and why supply is seasonal

> **Direct answer:** Lemongrass is a frost-tender tropical clump grass, propagated by division and cut several times a year where heat and water allow. Its supply is limited less by land than by drying capacity, which is why leaf quality moves with the weather rather than with the calendar alone.

The plant wants heat, sun, and steady moisture. It will grow from seed, and commercial fields are more often established from divisions because a clump copies true and establishes faster. A field is cut when the tillers are tall enough to give a useful leaf yield, then cut again as the clump regrows — several passes in a good season, fewer where the dry season bites.

That rhythm explains a pattern buyers often misread as supplier inconsistency. Harvest arrives in flushes. Wet-season growth is fast and the leaf is tall, but drying is the bottleneck: fresh leaf is mostly water, and every tonne of dry product needs a large volume of wet material moved through a dryer in a short window. When the flush outruns the dryer, the material is either dried faster and hotter than ideal or held wet for longer than ideal. Either choice costs aroma.

**Mechanism:** heavy harvest flush → dryer capacity exceeded → higher drying temperature or longer holding time → citral driven off or oxidised before the leaf is stable → pale, flat powder enters the warehouse → the buyer blames the supplier for a seasonal effect. The fix at the buying end is to sample across seasons and to ask when a lot was dried, not only where it was grown.

There is no vintage culture in this category, and that is appropriate. Lemongrass is not improved by age, and a two-year-old lot is not a curiosity; it is a degraded material. Origin still matters, because soil, rainfall, and the local cut-and-dry routine shape the leaf, but origin is a starting point for sampling rather than a guarantee of character.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The leaf is a food herb and low-risk in normal culinary use. The concentrated oil is a different material: it is a recognised skin sensitiser, it should not be taken internally, and burning any incense adds smoke and particulate matter to a room.

- **Skin.** Citral and citronellal are fragrance allergens in some regulatory lists. Undiluted oil on skin can sensitise; keep it off the face and off broken skin.
- **Ingestion.** Essential oil is not a food ingredient in drop form, whatever the culinary use of the leaf. Keep bottles out of the kitchen drawer and away from children.
- **Pets.** Cats and birds are sensitive to concentrated essential oils. A diffuser in a closed room with a cat is a poor idea.
- **Combustion.** Smoke and fine particles are the main exposure route for incense. Ventilate, do not lean into the plume, and keep the burner away from anything that can catch. Our [safety guide](/safety/) has the general rules.

Nothing here is a diagnosis, a prescription, or a substitute for one; treat it as background reading for people who work with aromatic materials.

## Summary

香茅 is lemongrass, *Cymbopogon citratus*: a clumping tropical grass whose leaf carries a citral-rich oil, sharp and clean and unmistakably lemon. The English word citronella belongs to two other grasses, *C. nardus* and *C. winterianus*, which supply the citronellal-rich oil of the repellent trade — a different plant, a different chemistry, and a different product. In incense, lemongrass is a top note: vivid at the opening, gone quickly, useful as an accent and useless as an anchor. Buy it by species and by nose, store it whole and dark, and treat the repellent reputation as a tradition attached to the oil rather than a promise attached to the smoke.

## FAQ

**Is citronella the same as lemongrass?** No. Lemongrass is *Cymbopogon citratus* (香茅), the culinary grass. Citronella is *C. nardus* or *C. winterianus*, grown for an oil rich in citronellal and geraniol. They are related grasses in the same genus, and they are different species with different chemistry and different uses.

**What does 香茅 smell like?** Sharp, clean lemon with a green, grassy body and a faint sweetness. The sharpness comes from citral; the greenness is the leaf itself. Compared with lemon peel it is less acidic and more herbal, and compared with citronella oil it is brighter and less rosy.

**Does burning lemongrass repel mosquitoes?** A smouldering stick produces smoke and some volatiles, and it has a folk reputation for keeping insects away. It is not a tested repellent delivery system, and the studied products are topical formulations of citronella oil. Use it as an outdoor ambiance product, not as protection.

**Why did my lemongrass powder lose its smell?** Almost certainly oxidation. Citral reacts with oxygen and is accelerated by light and heat, turning the bright lemon note into a hay-like smell. Powder loses it faster than whole leaf because every particle exposes oil to air. Store sealed, dark, and cool, and grind close to use.

**Can I use lemongrass powder in a stick?** Yes, as an accent. Leaf powder is light and bulky, so it needs more binder and water than a wood powder and dries less evenly. Keep the share modest, adjust the binder, and dry gently to avoid cracking.

## Evidence & Sources

### Where the botany comes from
Flora of China supplies the description used above: a tufted grass with culms to about 2 m, blue-green blades between 30 and 90 cm, placed in Poaceae. GBIF supplies the taxonomic record and the accepted name. Both are reference databases rather than field trials, so they settle identity and nothing else. Start with the [Flora of China treatment](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200025088); the [GBIF species record](https://www.gbif.org/species/2705275) is the second entry behind the botanical section.

### Where the cultural claims come from
Culinary use across southern China and Southeast Asia is documented practice, not a laboratory finding. The materia-medica layer is recorded in traditional language and is labelled as traditional belief wherever it appears. No clinical trial is cited because none is being relied on.

### Where the naming argument comes from
The separation of *C. citratus* and *C. flexuosus* from *C. nardus* and *C. winterianus* is a matter of established taxonomy, reinforced by the different volatile profiles of the oils. Palmarosa (*C. martini*) is included only to show how far the genus ranges in character. The practical test is the species name on the document.

### Where the safety notes come from
Citral and citronellal appear on fragrance-allergen lists in several jurisdictions. Essential-oil exposure around cats and birds is a recognised household caution. Smoke and particulate matter are combustion products, independent of which aromatic is burned.

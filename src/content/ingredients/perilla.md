Perilla is the rare incense material that most readers have already eaten. It arrives at the table as a wrap for grilled meat, a pickle, a garnish, or a tea, and it arrives at the blending bench as a dried leaf with a green, sweet, anise-like smell. The same plant does both jobs, which is the first fact worth holding on to: this is a food crop that happens to be aromatic enough for fragrance, not a rare forest material with a mystique attached.

That origin explains almost everything about how perilla behaves in incense. It is abundant, cheap, quick to grow, and best when fresh. It has no long aging curve, no resin content, and no scarcity story. What it has is a bright, fleeting top note — and a set of naming traps that cause more buying mistakes than any question of quality.

## Key facts

> **Direct answer:** 紫苏 (zǐsū) is the leaf-and-flower-top material harvested from *Perilla frutescens*, a Lamiaceae annual domesticated across East Asia. Its scent reads green and minty with a sweet anise core; on the bench it belongs in the opening, never in the base.

The shortness of that table is not a gap in the research. Perilla really is a simple material commercially: one species, two leaf colours, a handful of physical forms, and no grade ladder. The complexity sits in naming and in freshness, and both are covered below.

## What perilla actually is

> **Direct answer:** A soft-stemmed plant that completes its life in one season. Nothing woody develops, no exudate is tapped, and the entire aromatic yield is held in foliage and flowering tips.

*Perilla frutescens* is described in Flora of China across a range rather than as a fixed type: stems that can reach two metres, hairy or almost smooth, green or purple; leaves roughly egg-shaped to circular with saw-toothed margins; flowers from late summer into autumn. Gardeners select for broad, tender blades, while wild populations keep smaller ones. The dried herb in trade is the leafy top of those cultivated selections. See [*Perilla frutescens* in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200019964) and [on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/5341394).

The annual habit explains the material's limits. A tree can accumulate aromatic compounds over decades; a herb rebuilds its tissue from scratch each season. The whole value of a perilla crop is captured in one harvest and can be lost in one careless drying.

| Trait | What it means commercially |
|---|---|
| Annual, no woody tissue | Nothing to age; freshness carries the value |
| Soft leaf with high moisture | Drying determines whether aroma survives |
| Wide colour range | Purple and green are forms, not quality tiers |
| Short season | Supply is seasonal and perishable |

That is why perilla has no vintage story and no "aged" category. The closest thing to aging is deterioration, and the trade knows it.

## The name problem: 紫苏, 白苏, shiso, kkaennip

> **Direct answer:** Two Chinese labels — 紫苏 for pigment-rich leaves, 白苏 for green ones — describe a single species. Japanese shiso, Korean kkaennip and deulkkae, and the antique English "beefsteak plant" are aliases rather than separate botanicals.

Chinese gives the two colour forms separate labels, which is why they read like separate commodities in a price list. They are not. 紫苏 (zǐsū) is the purple-leaf expression, the one with the longer culinary and aromatic reputation; 白苏 (báisū) is the green-leaf expression. A seller can move between them without any change of species, and often does.

The label count is the real hazard. The same herb appears under at least nine names in regional trade, and only some of them distinguish leaf from seed:

| Term | Where it is used | What it actually tells you |
|---|---|---|
| 紫苏 (zǐsū) | Chinese | Purple-leaf perilla; the culinary and aromatic default |
| 白苏 (báisū) | Chinese | Green-leaf perilla, same species |
| 苏叶 (sūyè) | Chinese trade | A leaf form, not a variety |
| 苏子 (sūzǐ) | Chinese trade | The seed, which is a different product |
| Shiso (紫苏) | Japanese | The species; aka-shiso purple, ao-shiso green |
| Ōba (大葉) | Japanese trade | Large-leaf green shiso grown as a herb |
| Kkaennip (깻잎) | Korean | The leaf, eaten as a wrap |
| Deulkkae (들깨) | Korean | The seed crop, pressed for culinary oil |
| Beefsteak plant | Older English | An alias, not a separate plant |

A purchase order that says only "perilla" is therefore underspecified. Three questions close most of the gap: which colour form, which part, and whether the seed stream is involved. The last one matters commercially, because seed is a food-oil business with its own buyers and its own handling.

The neighbouring herbs cause a second layer of confusion. True mint (薄荷, *Mentha* species) brings a cooling, menthol sharpness that perilla does not have. Agastache (藿香, *Agastache rugosa*) is another Lamiaceae herb with a herbaceous, slightly camphoraceous profile and an entry of its own. Perilla sits between them: sweeter than mint, softer than agastache, with an anise note neither of them shares.

## How the plant grows

> **Direct answer:** Warmth, moisture, and open ground are enough. The plant races from seed to flower in a single warm season, and its August–November bloom marks the point where leaf quality starts to slide.

Perilla is not a fussy crop. It germinates in warmth, grows quickly, and bolts to flower as the days shorten. The flowering tops are part of the harvested herb, which is why the August–November window matters: a crop cut before flowering has a leafier, greener profile, while one cut with flowers carries a slightly different, more herbal top.

The leaf size range in the botanical description is wide because the species includes both small-leaved wild types and large-leaved cultivated forms. Gardeners know the large-leaf versions as a vegetable; the same selection pressure for big, tender leaves is what makes the dried herb look generous in a sample bag.

Two colour forms are the visible variation. The purple pigment is concentrated in the upper leaf surface and shows through in dried material as a dark, brownish-purple tone; the green form dries to a dull olive. Neither colour predicts aroma quality. A fresh, well-dried purple leaf and a fresh, well-dried green leaf are both good; a faded example of either is not.

## Where it comes from

> **Direct answer:** Its wild range runs from the Himalayan foothills through China and onward to Japan, Korea, and mainland Southeast Asia. The plant colonises broken ground and garden edges, a habit that made it a weed, a crop, and a kitchen staple in turn.

Flora of China documents it from the southern provinces through the centre and north of the country, and westward into Sichuan, Yunnan, and Xizang, as well as in Bhutan, Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Japan, Korea, Laos, and Vietnam. The habitat note — waste areas and gardens — says as much as the list: this is a plant that follows people.

That wide, weedy distribution has a commercial meaning. There is no single famous growing district to pay a premium for, and no scarcity story to tell. Perilla is grown, cut, and sold as an ordinary crop, and the trade is organized around freshness and volume rather than origin and mystique.

For a buyer, the useful questions are therefore unglamorous. When was it cut? How was it dried? How long has it been in the bag? A clean answer to those three is worth more than a place name on the label.

## The scent: green, sweet, anise

> **Direct answer:** Crushed foliage gives the first impression, green with a mint edge. A sweet, faintly liquorice warmth follows, then fades fast; the dry-down is brief and grassy rather than resinous.

Smell a fresh leaf and the first thing you notice is greenness — the smell of a garden after watering, with a faint mint sharpness at the edge. Rub it and the second layer appears: a sweet, anise-like warmth that is closer to basil or liquorice than to mint. That second layer is the reason the herb is used in fragrance at all.

The sequence is short. On paper, the green opening is gone within minutes and the anise follows soon after. What remains is a dry, faintly herbal trace that most noses stop noticing. A blend that depends on perilla for its identity will therefore feel front-loaded; a blend that uses it as an opening gesture will feel bright and deliberate.

| Phase | Impression | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| First | Green leaf, soft mint | The part that survives a burn |
| Second | Sweet anise, basil warmth | What separates it from mint |
| Last | Dry herb, fading | Why it needs a base beneath it |

Two smell tests sort most samples. A leaf that gives only hay and mint has lost its anise, usually through age or poor drying. A leaf that smells sharply of menthol is probably mint, or a blend, rather than perilla.

## Perillaldehyde, and what drying does to it

> **Direct answer:** Perillaldehyde carries most of the sweet-anise impression. It is small, volatile, and easily driven off, so the drying regime decides whether the bag keeps that note or settles into hay.

Perillaldehyde is the compound most often named in descriptions of perilla's aroma, and it explains the sweet-liquorice impression. It is also a small, volatile molecule, which is why the scent lives at the surface of the leaf and leaves it easily.

A drying chain explains most of the quality variation in the trade:

fresh leaf with high moisture → slow or crowded drying → leaf temperature rises and air cannot move → volatiles evaporate before the tissue is stable → the dried herb smells of hay → the anise note is gone before the leaf reaches the blender.

The reverse chain is what a good producer aims for:

quick, shaded, ventilated drying → water leaves fast enough to stop spoilage → tissue stabilises before the aromatic fraction escapes → the dried leaf keeps a green, anise-sweet top → the material behaves as a top note should.

Heat is the ambiguous variable. Some warmth speeds drying and reduces mold risk; too much heat drives off exactly the compounds the buyer is paying for. The practical compromise is shade, airflow, and thin layers rather than a hot box. That is a process judgment, not a laboratory result, and it is one of the places where a supplier's handling shows up in the sample.

## Why a culinary herb appears in incense

> **Direct answer:** This is a food crop that crossed into fragrance on the strength of its smell, not a temple resin with a ritual pedigree. Its job is to freshen an opening, and it does that without mint's menthol bite.

Most incense materials have a religious or trade history that runs back centuries: resins traded along routes, woods reserved for temples, aromatics used in medicine. Perilla's history is domestic. It was a food plant, a pickle, a tea, a wrap, and a household aromatic long before anyone put it in a stick.

That background shapes its role. A material with a strong cultural and ritual pedigree tends to be used at the centre of a formula; a kitchen herb is used at the edge, as a brightener. Perilla is almost always an accent. It can be the green note in a blend that would otherwise be all wood and resin, and it can soften a formula that has become too dry or too sweet.

There is also a practical reason for restraint. Leafy material burns fast and leaves little behind. A formula that leans on perilla for its character will smell wonderful for the first minute and thin for the rest of the burn. The fix is not more perilla; it is a base that can carry the opening.

## Blending around a short top note

> **Direct answer:** Give it the first thirty seconds and nothing more. A middle-weight partner underneath turns its brevity from a defect into a deliberate bright entrance.

The traditional 君臣佐使 (jun-chen-zuo-shi) frame — chief, minister, assistant, guide — is useful here because it forces a decision about role. Perilla is an assistant or a guide: it lifts the opening and points the blend in a green direction. It is a poor chief, because it cannot sustain the composition, and a poor minister, because it has no weight to support anything.

Two skeleton approaches work.

The first is a green-over-wood structure: a dry woody base, a resin or balsam for sweetness, and perilla as the opening accent. The wood carries the burn; the resin rounds the transition; perilla gives the first minute a fresh, anise-green lift. This is the most reliable use of the material.

The second is a green-over-citrus structure: citrus peel for brightness, perilla for the herbal middle, and a light resin to stop the whole thing from evaporating. The risk here is that citrus and perilla compete for the same opening, producing a bright but muddy top. The fix is to let one lead and use the other as a supporting note.

Pairings that tend to work: cedar, sandalwood, benzoin, green tea, bergamot, and a restrained mint. Pairings that tend to fail: agastache and perilla together, because the two herbs occupy the same aromatic space and blur into a generic "green" note; and heavy floral absolutes, which can drown a top note that lasts seconds.

Dose is a matter of proportion, not a fixed number. Start with the smallest amount that registers on the blotter, then build the base until the opening has something to fall into. If the blend needs more perilla to be noticeable, the base is probably too heavy, not the top too weak.

## A top note with a short arc

> **Direct answer:** Whether rolled, pressed, or scattered, the leaf gives its scent early and then steps aside. Format alters the tempo; it cannot extend the note's lifespan.

A stick heats a narrow band of material ahead of the ember. The light fraction of perilla vaporises first, so the green-anise opening dominates the first third of the burn. A cone runs hotter and faster; the opening is louder and shorter. Loose powder burns cooler and gives a cleaner, quieter version of the same arc.

The chain is simple:

leafy aromatic heated → light fraction vaporises → green top fills the room → ember moves on → little heavy material remains → the note collapses into the base.

That collapse is not a defect. It is what a top note does. The mistake is expecting perilla to behave like a heart or a base note, then adding more and more to compensate for a fade that is inherent to the material.

| Format | How perilla reads | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Stick | Clear green opening, quick fade | Best showcase for the anise note |
| Cone | Louder, smokier, shorter | The leaf can scorch if the cone runs hot |
| Loose powder | Quieter, cleaner, very short | Good for testing the leaf's freshness |
| Indirect heat | Gentle and brief | Closest to the dry-leaf smell |

A maker who wants perilla to last longer has two honest options: pair it with a heavier material that holds the base, or accept the short arc and design the blend around a bright opening. Adding more leaf is the third option, and it usually produces a harsh opening followed by the same fade.

## The Japanese and Korean kitchen

> **Direct answer:** Japanese kitchens distinguish ao-shiso, aka-shiso, and ōba by colour and leaf size; Korean ones separate kkaennip, the wrap leaf, from deulkkae, the seed crop. Both naming systems describe one species.

Japanese cooking uses the leaf as a visual and aromatic garnish. Ao-shiso, the green form, appears beside sashimi and sushi, in tempura, and in salads. Aka-shiso, the purple form, colours and flavours pickled plums and shows up in preserves. Ōba is the large, tender green leaf sold as a herb in its own right. The same plant also gives shiso juice, a sweet-sour drink that relies on the leaf's colour and aroma.

Korean cooking treats the leaf as a vegetable rather than a garnish. Kkaennip wraps grilled meat, appears in kimchi, and is eaten fresh with rice. The seed has its own career: deulkkae is toasted and ground into a nutty powder used to thicken soups, and the seed is pressed for deulgireum, a culinary oil with a strong toasted aroma.

The Chinese kitchen uses 紫苏 in a different register again — with fish, in stir-fries, in pickles, and as a dried leaf for tea. The common thread is that every tradition treats perilla as food first. The incense use is a later, smaller application grafted onto a plant that was already domesticated for the table.

That history has a practical consequence for a buyer. Culinary supply chains are built around freshness, cold storage, and rapid turnover. A herb that reaches a kitchen in good condition has been handled well; a herb that reaches a fragrance blender through a food broker may have been sitting in a warehouse far longer than the label suggests. Ask how the material was stored, not only where it was grown.

## Processing routes

> **Direct answer:** Harvested herb passes through drying, sorting, and milling, in that order, and each step can undo the one before. Steam distillation branches off as an independent product stream.

Drying comes first and matters most. Spread thin in shade with air moving through it, the herb loses water fast enough to stabilise without cooking off the aromatic fraction. Piled thick, it heats in the middle; the centre of the pile smells of hay before the outside is dry.

Cleaning follows. Stems, soil, and foreign leaf are removed by hand or by screen, and the thoroughness of that step is visible in the bag. A stemmy sample is a sample that was rushed.

Size reduction is the third operation, and the one most likely to undo good drying. A mill generates heat, and heat drives off the compounds the grower worked to preserve. Small batches, cool equipment, and immediate packing protect the powder; a large batch left in an open drum does not.

Distillation runs on a separate line. Steam passed through the herb yields an essential oil with a concentrated green-anise profile, and the spent herb is a by-product rather than a second product.

- Whole leaf keeps longest and suits sachets and loose blends.
- Broken leaf gives up its aroma faster and blends more easily.
- Powder is for sticks and cones, where the leaf must pass a screen and bind with the paste.

The mechanical side of that powder — mesh size, water, binder, drying of the formed stick — sits inside the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process. The numbers change with every recipe, so a trial batch is worth more than a specification sheet.

## Seed, oil, and the other perilla

> **Direct answer:** "Perilla oil" covers at least three unrelated goods — herb distillate, pressed seed fat, and leaf macerated in a carrier. Only the first belongs on an incense bench; the others follow food and skincare logic.

The naming overlap is a genuine trap. A supplier listing "perilla oil" may mean any of the three, and the buyer's intended use decides which one is correct.

| Product | Made from | Character | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaf essential oil | Steam-distilled herb | Sharp green-anise, concentrated | Fragrance, incense blending |
| Seed oil | Pressed seed | Nutty, mild, food-like | Cooking, dressing |
| Infused oil | Leaf steeped in a carrier | Soft, herbal, variable | Salves, culinary finishing |

Leaf essential oil is the only one that belongs on an incense bench. It is steam-distilled from the aerial herb, and its aroma is a concentrated version of the dried leaf: green, sweet, anise-heavy, with a sharp edge that needs dilution. Seed oil is a food fat. It smells of toasted nuts, not of anise, and it turns rancid in a way that is obvious to anyone who has left a bottle too long. Infused oil sits in between: a carrier oil that has taken on some of the leaf's aroma, usually weakly and inconsistently.

A buyer can tell them apart without a laboratory. Seed oil is pale, mild, and edible; leaf oil is mobile, sharply aromatic, and not something to taste. Infused oil smells of both the carrier and the herb, and its strength depends on how much leaf went in.

The seed side also has its own supply chain and its own seasons. It is a food product with food expectations, and it should be kept away from the incense workspace for the same reason flour is kept away from a perfume bench: it belongs to a different craft.

## Freshness is the only grade

> **Direct answer:** No trade ladder, no scoring sheet, no estate hierarchy. What separates a good sack from a poor one is how recently it was dried, how clean it is, and how much of the leaf survived intact.

A buyer from the tea or coffee world expects a scoring system. Perilla does not have one. What it has is a short shelf life for its aroma and a set of visible signals that correlate with how it was handled.

| Signal | Poor | Good | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smell | Hay, dust, faint mint | Green, sweet, clear anise | The aromatic fraction is the product |
| Colour | Brown, faded, patchy | Even olive or dark purple, depending on form | Fading tracks oxidation and age |
| Leaf integrity | Shattered, stem-heavy, dusty | Whole leaves, few stems | Surface area controls how fast scent is lost |
| Moisture | Soft, clumping, cool | Brittle, rustles, snaps | Damp leaf invites mold |
| Cleanliness | Soil, foreign leaf, visible mold | Clean, uniform, no off-note | Contamination cannot be blended away |

Two failures deserve names. The first is "flat" leaf: material that still looks acceptable but smells of hay. It was probably dried too slowly or stored too long, and no blending trick brings the anise back. The second is damp leaf: it clumps in the bag and smells of cellar. That is a mold risk, and the lot should be rejected rather than dried out and used.

Colour is the most over-read signal. Purple leaf is not "better" than green, and green is not "purer" than purple. They are two forms of one species, and the label often does not say which one is in the bag. A buyer who cares about consistency should ask, not assume.

## Telling perilla from its neighbours

> **Direct answer:** Against mint it is softer and sweeter; against agastache it is greener and less medicinal. The plants are easy to separate in the hand — it is the translated labels that cause trouble.

The confusion is mostly linguistic. 紫苏, 薄荷, and 藿香 are three different herbs that all pass through the same "mint family" description in English. A buyer reading a translated label can easily merge them.

| Feature | Perilla | Mint | Agastache |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaf shape | Broad, ovate to round, serrated | Narrower, often textured | Ovate, serrated, softer |
| Leaf colour | Green, purple, or purple-black | Usually green | Usually green |
| Scent | Sweet anise, soft mint | Sharp menthol | Herbal, minty, medicinal |
| Flower | Small, in spikes, late summer | Whorls, summer | Dense spikes, summer |
| Chinese name | 紫苏 / 白苏 | 薄荷 | 藿香 |

A simple bench test separates them. Crush a leaf and smell: perilla gives anise behind the green, mint gives cooling sharpness, and agastache gives a herbaceous, slightly camphoraceous note. The difference is obvious in the hand and almost invisible in a photograph.

There is one more naming layer for English readers. "Beefsteak plant" is an old common name for perilla, and it appears in older garden writing. It is the same species, not a different herb. A supplier list that mixes "beefsteak plant," "shiso," and "perilla" may be naming one material three ways.

## In the kitchen and in the materia medica

> **Direct answer:** East Asian cooks treat leaf and seed as groceries, not remedies: wrappers, pickles, teas, cooking oil, soup thickener. The 本草綱目 entry is a historical text, and this page does not promote it to clinical proof.

Start with the food, because that is where the plant's reputation was built. In Korea, kkaennip wraps grilled pork and rice, and the seed is toasted, ground, and stirred into soup. In Japan, ao-shiso accompanies sashimi, aka-shiso colours pickled plums, and ōba is a herb in its own right. In China, 紫苏 goes into fish dishes and stir-fries, and 苏叶 is dried for tea. None of these uses depends on the plant being rare; all of them depend on it being fresh.

The dried leaf carries that culinary expectation into the incense workshop. A cook rejects tired leaf; so should a blender. The difference is that a cook can taste the dish and adjust, while a blender often discovers the problem only when the stick is burning.

The materia medica record is separate. 紫苏 appears in the 本草綱目 (Běncǎo Gāngmù), in the herbal section, described in the language of its period. That entry is a historical document and a cultural marker. It is not a clinical trial, and this page does not treat it as one.

- **Food:** fresh leaf, pickled leaf, dried leaf for tea, seed powder, seed oil.
- **Fragrance:** dried leaf as a green top note; essential oil as a concentrate.
- **Medicine:** traditional literature only; no clinical claim is made here.

The three registers share a plant but not an evidence standard. Keeping them apart is the difference between a useful reference and a sales page.

## The evidence boundary

> **Direct answer:** Identity and range are settled science; food and fragrance use are long documented; therapeutic statements remain inherited tradition. Nothing here diagnoses, treats, or promises a health outcome.

Three layers are worth separating. The botanical layer — identity, distribution, morphology — is settled and citable. The cultural layer — culinary use, incense use, the 本草綱目 record — is historically documented. The therapeutic layer is traditional language.

The chemistry sits between the second and third layers. Perillaldehyde and the oil's other constituents are characterized in the literature, and that characterization explains the smell. It does not establish that burning the herb or applying the oil treats anything. A compound being identifiable is not the same as a clinical effect.

That boundary is not a legal formality. Incense is a combustion product, and the smoke is a separate consideration from the herb. The honest description is: a well-documented culinary and aromatic herb, with a traditional medicinal literature and no established clinical role in incense form.

## Buying and keeping

> **Direct answer:** Judge the sack on aroma, breakage, and moisture, not on a growing region. Pin down the colour form and the plant part before price, then keep the goods sealed against air, light, and heat.

A buying checklist for an incense maker:

- **Form.** Whole leaf, crushed leaf, powder, or oil. Each behaves differently.
- **Colour form.** 紫苏 or 白苏; ask, because the label may not say.
- **Smell.** Green and anise-sweet, not hay or dust.
- **Texture.** Brittle and dry, not soft or clumping.
- **Cleanliness.** No soil, no foreign leaf, no visible mold.
- **Powder spec.** Particle size and moisture agreed against the recipe, not against a generic data sheet.
- **Sample first.** A small trial tells you more than a certificate.

Storage follows from the chemistry. The aromatic fraction is volatile, so the enemies are air, light, heat, and time. Whole leaf in a sealed glass jar in a dark cupboard will hold its character for a normal production cycle; powder will not, because the surface area is larger. Buy powder in quantities that match use, and keep it away from strong-smelling materials, which it will absorb.

A note on the freezer: it slows loss but does not stop it, and every time the container is opened, condensation forms on the cold leaf. For a material sold on freshness, a dry cupboard beats a frost-covered bag.

## A bench test for leaf

> **Direct answer:** Weigh a constant dose, burn it the way production will, and write down what you smell at fixed moments against a known reference. The result describes behaviour; it cannot certify pesticide status or provenance.

A bench test is only useful if it is repeatable. Weigh the same amount each time, use the same burner or stick format, and keep the room similar. A sample tested in a cold workshop and a sample tested in a warm office are not comparable, and the difference will be blamed on the leaf.

- **Smell the dry leaf first.** Crush a pinch in the palm. Green and anise-sweet is the target; hay or dust is a fail.
- **Burn a fixed dose.** A small cone or a short stick, not a handful of powder.
- **Record at set points.** The first minute, the middle, and the end of the burn. Write what you smell, not whether you like it.
- **Compare blind.** Two coded samples, same dose, one reference. The label should not be visible.
- **Note the room.** Humidity and airflow change the opening more than they change the base.

What the test cannot tell you: whether the leaf was grown without prohibited pesticides, whether it was stored near mold, or whether the supplier's paperwork is accurate. Those are supply-chain questions, answered by documents and audits rather than by nose.

A leaf that performs well in the test but smells flat in the bag is a storage problem. A leaf that smells good in the bag but collapses in the burn is usually too thin, too dry, or simply old. The two failures look similar on a blotter and different in a burn, which is why the test uses fire rather than paper.

## Writing a purchase spec a supplier can meet

> **Direct answer:** Fix the form, the colour form, the part, the cleanliness standard, the packing, and the retention sample. Put aroma in the spec as a described reference, not as an adjective.

Most buying problems begin before the order, in a document that says "dried perilla leaf, good quality." That sentence means nothing to a producer and everything to a dispute. A workable spec fits on a page and leaves little room for interpretation.

- **Species and form.** *Perilla frutescens*, whole leaf, broken leaf, or powder. Name the form, not just the herb.
- **Colour form.** 紫苏 or 白苏, or "either, declared per lot" if the buyer has no preference. Silence here guarantees surprise.
- **Part.** Leaf and flowering top, or leaf only, with a stated stem tolerance. Stem content is easy to measure and hard to argue about later.
- **Cleanliness.** No mold, soil, foreign leaf, or insect damage; state whether a screen or hand sort is required.
- **Moisture.** A limit agreed with the supplier and tested on receipt. The number matters less than applying it the same way each time.
- **Aroma.** A retained reference sample, described in words: green, anise-sweet, no hay, no cellar. The sample is the standard; the words explain it.
- **Packing.** Food-grade liner, sealed, labelled with lot code, drying date, and net mass.
- **Retention.** A sealed sample from each lot, held by both parties for the agreed period.

The retention sample is the most useful clause in the document. When a delivery smells unlike the approved sample, the argument stops being about adjectives and becomes a comparison of two bags. Buyers who skip this step negotiate from memory, which is the weakest position in a sensory trade.

Two more clauses are worth writing before the first shipment: what counts as a failed lot, and who pays for rejection or rework. Sampling belongs there too — agree whether the sample comes from the bag's surface or from a probe, because the surface is always the best-smelling part.

## Storage, humidity, and the shelf

> **Direct answer:** Dried perilla fails through moisture, heat, light, and odour transfer, in that order. Sealed, dry, dark, and cool covers most of it; the rest is rotation.

The leaf is hygroscopic. It pulls water from humid air through paper or an imperfect seal, and as moisture rises, two problems follow: mold risk and a flatter smell. The mold shows up eventually; the flatness shows up earlier, when a crushed leaf fails to give the expected anise.

The chain is worth stating plainly:

warm, humid air → moisture enters the leaf → microbial and enzymatic activity resumes → colour darkens and aroma fades → the lot is rejected at the bench, long after the cause.

The countermeasures are unglamorous. Use a gasketed container rather than a folded bag, keep it out of sunlight, and add a food-safe desiccant where humidity is high, replacing it on a schedule. Do not store leaf beside spices, soap, or resin; it absorbs its neighbours within weeks.

Temperature matters less than cycling. A cupboard that swings from cold nights to hot afternoons pulls moisture in and out repeatedly, which is worse than a steady cool room. Insulate the container before worrying about the building.

Rotation is the last control. Label each container with the date it was opened, use the oldest first, and buy powder to match a production cycle rather than a price break. A large bag is not a bargain if half of it is flat before it reaches a stick.

## Seasonality and the annual reset

> **Direct answer:** Perilla is a seasonal crop with a short shelf life, so buying is a planning problem rather than a pricing problem. The work is matching a year's production to a harvest window and a realistic refresh cycle.

A leaf crop does not arrive evenly across the year. It is cut in a window, dried in batches, and then sits in storage until someone buys it. That shape creates two risks for a buyer: running out before the next harvest, and holding stock long enough that the last bags are flat.

The first risk is a buffer question. A workshop that uses leaf steadily needs enough stock to bridge the gap between harvests, plus a margin for a bad season. The buffer does not have to be large, but it has to be deliberate. Buyers who order hand-to-mouth end up taking whatever is available in the lean months, which is exactly when quality is hardest to police.

The second risk is a rotation question. Every bag has a clock on it, and the clock runs faster once the seal is broken. A simple rule works: open one container at a time, finish it before opening the next, and write the opening date on the lid. That single habit prevents the common failure of five half-empty jars, all of them fading.

Seasonality also shapes price. Leaf is cheapest when the new crop lands and dearest when the old crop is thin. A buyer who understands the calendar can plan purchases around it, but only if the storage is good enough to carry the material forward. Cheap leaf stored badly is not a saving; it is a delayed loss.

For planning purposes, treat whole leaf and powder as different clocks. Whole leaf holds its character long enough to bridge a season in good storage. Powder does not, and buying powder on a seasonal price break usually means throwing away the advantage in the last month. Match the purchase to the form, not to the discount.

## Four questions for a supplier audit

> **Direct answer:** Ask where and when the leaf was dried, how it is stored, how lots are coded, and what happens when a delivery fails. The answers say more than a certificate.

An audit does not need a checklist of a hundred items. For a material this simple, four questions get to the parts that matter.

**Where and when was it dried?** A supplier who can name the drying site and the week is tracking their own material. One who says "from our partner" is not.

**How is it stored?** Sealed, dark, cool, and rotated is the right answer. "In the warehouse" is not an answer; it is a location.

**How are lots coded?** A code should resolve to a harvest, a drying batch, and a packing date. If it resolves only to a year, the supplier cannot compare one delivery with another, and neither can you.

**What happens when a lot fails?** Rejection, rework, or credit should be agreed in advance. A supplier who has never thought about it will improvise when it happens, and improvisation favours the seller.

Ask these before price. A supplier who answers them well is likely to be a better partner than one who answers only the price question, because the answers show a system rather than a sales script.

The same four questions apply to a new crop year as to a new supplier. Materials change; systems usually do not. That is why a buyer who has audited once can re-audit quickly, and why a buyer who has never asked is starting from zero every season.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** Combustion generates smoke and fine particles regardless of how benign the herb is. Work with airflow, keep the oil off skin and away from children, and treat the distillate as the industrial concentrate it is.

Three practical points. Smoke is the main exposure: any burning herb releases fine particles, and a closed room concentrates them. Ventilation is the control. The oil is potent and should not be applied neat to skin or left within reach of children. Storage should follow the supplier's safety data sheet, including disposal.

Perilla seed oil is a food product and belongs in the kitchen, not the incense bench. Keep the two apart, especially if the same workspace handles both.

This page makes no medical claims for perilla, its oil, or its smoke. Readers looking for health information should consult qualified professionals rather than an incense reference. The general handling guidance for burning materials sits in the [safety guide](/safety/).

## FAQ

**Is perilla the same as shiso?** Yes. Shiso is the Japanese name for *Perilla frutescens*. Aka-shiso is the purple form, ao-shiso the green, and ōba the large-leaf culinary selection. The plant does not change with the label.

**Are 紫苏 and 白苏 different plants?** No. They are the purple and green leaf forms of one species. A bag marked only "perilla" may hold either, and the colour says nothing about freshness.

**What does perilla smell like when burned?** Green and leafy at first, then sweet and anise-like, then gone. It is gentler than mint and warmer than a plain herb, and its arc is measured in minutes rather than hours.

**Can I use perilla seed oil in a blend?** No. Seed oil is a culinary fat pressed from the seed; the aromatic oil is distilled from leaf and flowering top. They share a plant and nothing else that matters on an incense bench.

**Why does dried perilla go flat?** The aromatic fraction is volatile, and it leaves the leaf during slow drying, warm storage, or repeated opening of the bag. Once the anise is gone, no blending restores it.

## Summary

Perilla is a food plant that learned to be a top note. Its leaf carries a green, sweet, anise-like scent that opens a blend and then disappears, which makes it useful as an accent and useless as a foundation. The commercial picture is unusually simple — one species, two leaf colours, four physical forms — while the naming is unusually messy: 紫苏, 白苏, shiso, kkaennip, and beefsteak plant all describe the same herb. Quality is freshness rather than grade, and the tests that matter are smell, texture, and a small burn trial. Buy it for the opening, store it away from air and light, and do not ask it to do a base note's job.

**Direct answer:** 甘松 (gānsōng) is a cured piece of underground stem and root that smells of wet earth, old wood, and a sweetness which sits at the back of the throat. It comes from *Nardostachys jatamansi*, a small plant of the high Himalaya. Incense makers rarely burn it for a solo performance. They use it to give a formula a floor.

## Key facts

**Direct answer:** A high-altitude rootstock with a musky-sweet smell, a documented place in Chinese blending, and a supply chain tight enough that the paperwork matters as much as the aroma.

## The smell, without the romance

**Direct answer:** The first impression is soil and old timber. The sweetness comes later, and it is the sweetness of a dried root rather than a flower. The musk arrives last and stays longest.

A fresh piece does not smell like a perfume counter. It smells like the floor of a forest after rain has stopped and the leaves have begun to rot — a damp, brown, slightly bitter smell with a woody spine. Put a sliver on a warm surface and the picture changes. The wetness burns off, the wood softens, and a rounder sweetness comes forward. It is not sugar and it is not fruit. The closest everyday comparison is the smell of a dried root chewed slowly at the end of a walk: starchy, faintly sweet, with a bitter edge that keeps it from cloying.

The last phase is the musk. It is warm, close to the skin, and slightly animal. Some noses read it as comfort; others read it as a smell that has been left out too long. Both readings are honest, and both depend on dose. At a small fraction of a formula the musk reads as depth. At ten times that amount it reads as staleness, and the whole blend feels tired.

That sequence is not a poetic invention. It follows the material's chemistry. The rootstock carries a heavy aromatic load, dominated by sesquiterpenes rather than the light monoterpenes that give citrus and fresh florals their lift. Heavy molecules evaporate slowly. So the bright, earthy top of the root arrives first, the sweet middle unfolds as the material warms, and the musky base lingers after the flame has gone.

**Chain:** root warmed → lightest aromatic fraction leaves → earthy-woody opening → heavier fraction follows → sweet-musky body → residue holds the base.

The practical lesson is to smell the material twice: once cold, once warm. The cold jar tells you what the top note is. Gentle warmth tells you what the material will do in a blend.

## A root that behaves like a floor

**Direct answer:** Spikenard does not add a separate smell on top of a formula. It changes the architecture underneath: it lowers the centre, slows the fade, and gives lighter materials something to stand on.

Think of a blend as a building rather than a list. Woods and resins supply the walls; florals and citrus supply the windows; a root like 甘松 supplies the foundation. Remove the foundation and the structure may still stand, but it will feel thin, and the upper floors will rattle. Add too much and the building sinks.

This is why the material belongs to *huxiang* (合香), the Chinese blended-incense tradition, rather than to single-material burning. Burned alone, it gives you the root and nothing else: a dense, earthy-sweet smell with no contrast, no lift, and no story. Blended, its weight becomes useful. It holds a sandalwood base down, softens the dry edge of a resin, and keeps a light floral from evaporating into nothing.

The Chinese framework of 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) is a useful way to talk about that role, though the assignment is editorial rather than a rule from a manual. A chief note (君) states the idea of the composition. A minister (臣) supports it. An assistant (佐) corrects or rounds it. A guide (使) harmonizes the parts. 甘松 usually reads as an assistant or a guide: it is not the idea, but it is the reason the idea holds together. See the [hexiang blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) for the broader logic.

There is a limit to the metaphor. A floor is passive; spikenard is not. Its sweetness actively softens bitter edges, and its musk actively extends the dry-down. The material is doing chemistry, not just occupying space. That is why a small amount can change a blend out of proportion to its weight.

## What the name does not tell you

**Direct answer:** "Spikenard" can mean a dried root, a distilled oil, or a historical perfume ingredient called nard. The Chinese name is narrower, but it too travels in more than one form.

The Chinese name is the more precise entry point. 甘松 names the dried rootstock. 甘松香 adds the generic suffix 香, "aromatic," the way an older materia medica might label a material by its category. The first character, 甘, means sweet, and it describes a taste rather than a smell. That is an old naming habit: a plant was often named for what happened in the mouth, not in the nose.

The English word is looser. "Spikenard" descends from nard, a luxury aromatic traded across the old Mediterranean and Indian perfume worlds. It has been attached to this genus for centuries, and in modern commerce it usually means an oil from the still, sold to aromatherapy. The genus is the same. The product is not.

| What the label says | What it usually is | What you are holding | The mistake it invites |
|---|---|---|---|
| 甘松 / 甘松香 | Dried rootstock of *N. jatamansi* | Whole, sliced, or ground root | Assuming it is a wood or resin |
| Spikenard root | Dried rootstock, usually the same species | Root pieces or powder | Expecting the smell of the oil |
| Spikenard oil | Steam-distilled essential oil | Liquid | Swapping it by weight for powder |
| Nard | A historical perfume name, not a specification | Whatever the seller decides | Reading romance as botany |
| 甘松, not 松 | The character 松 appears, but this is not a pine | Root | Expecting a pine-wood note |

A root lot and an oil lot differ in strength, storage, dosing, and price. A formulator who treats them as interchangeable will not get the same blend, even when both labels carry the same species name. The first question to ask is not "is this genuine spikenard?" but "which spikenard product is this?"

## The plant and the cliff

**Direct answer:** The plant is a low herb of high, open ground, neither tree nor vine. What the trade wants is underground; the flowers are a botanical signature, not the crop.

Flora of China describes flowering stems between 5 and 50 cm, a rosette of basal leaves, and blooms in magenta, pink, or purple-red. The wide height range is typical of alpine herbs, which stretch or stay compact depending on exposure, soil, and season. What matters commercially is below the surface: a thickened rootstock that stores the aromatic material the plant is gathered for.

The family has moved. Older references place the genus in Valerianaceae, the valerian family. Modern systems place it in Caprifoliaceae, a larger family that absorbed several former valerianaceous genera. Both statements describe one species. A supplier who writes "Valerianaceae" is using an older classification, not making a false claim, and a buyer who rejects a lot on that basis is rejecting a bookkeeping convention rather than a plant.

The range runs from western Sichuan and Tibet through Nepal and Bhutan into northern India. That is high, broken country, and it explains much of the supply story. The plants are not grown on a plantation grid. They are gathered from open alpine ground, often at a distance from roads, and the material moves through several hands before it reaches a drying floor.

## Curing, and the mistakes that follow

**Direct answer:** The rootstock is lifted, cleaned, and dried. Curing is the step that decides whether the lot keeps its sweetness or picks up a musty, cardboard edge.

The sequence is short: dig, shake off the soil, wash, trim the leaves and stems, dry, sort, pack. The difficult part is the drying. A fresh rootstock is wet, and its interior is denser than its skin. Water has to travel from the core to the surface and then into the air. If the surface dries too fast, it can set into a hard layer before the core has given up its moisture. The outside looks finished; the inside is still damp.

That is how a lot passes a visual check and develops mould in the bag weeks later. The safe path is slow and even:

Even drying → water moves from core to surface → the piece shrinks without stress → tissue stays sound → aroma stabilizes.

The risky path is the mirror image:

Hot, fast drying → surface seals → core remains wet → uneven shrinkage → cracks or hidden damp pockets → mould and off-notes.

This is a general principle of drying roots, not a claim about one producer. It explains why "dry" is not one condition. A piece can feel dry and still be wet at the centre, which is why breaking one open belongs in any inspection.

Heat also changes the smell. The aromatic fraction is volatile, and aggressive drying drives off some of what the buyer is paying for. A gently cured root tends to keep a fuller sweet-musky profile; a root dried hard and fast can smell thinner and more woody. The difference is not always visible, which is another argument for the warm-smell test.

## Three blend scenarios

**Direct answer:** Spikenard earns its place in three recurring situations: a wood-heavy blend that needs a floor, a creamy blend that needs depth without more cream, and a resin-heavy blend that needs its sharp edges softened. In each case the dose is small and the effect is structural.

The first is an agarwood-led composition. Agarwood supplies the idea and most of the movement from opening to heart, but a blend built only on agarwood can read top-heavy: all the interest in the first third, then a thin tail. A little 甘松 gives the base somewhere to land. The root's weight stretches the dry-down, and its sweetness keeps the wood from turning bitter at the end. The failure mode is overdose; too much and the agarwood's own character disappears under a musky blanket.

The second is a sandalwood-led composition. Sandalwood is creamy, soft, and already a base note, so adding another creamy material does little. Spikenard is useful here because it adds depth in a different texture: earthy rather than creamy, musky rather than sweet-woody. The blend gains a floor without gaining more of the same softness. The boundary is that the two together can read dense, and a lighter top note may be needed to keep the composition from settling.

The third is a resin-heavy composition. Frankincense, myrrh, and similar resins bring lift, brightness, and a balsamic film, but some carry a dry, slightly bitter edge. Spikenard's sweet-musky middle softens that edge and gives the resin something to sit on. The risk is that resin and root together can produce a heavy, smoky base, especially at high heat. The fix is usually temperature control and restraint, not more fragrance.

None of these scenarios needs a number. The useful question is not "how much spikenard?" but "what is missing from the base?" If the answer is weight, a root can supply it. If the answer is brightness, a root is the wrong tool.

## Heat, and the shape of the release

**Direct answer:** Gentle warmth opens the root slowly; direct flame scorches it. The material rewards a low, steady heat and punishes impatience.

As the rootstock warms, aromatic compounds migrate toward the surface and evaporate. The rate depends on temperature and on the size of the pieces. A whole or sliced root exposes little surface, so the release is gradual. A powder exposes far more, so the first minutes are stronger and the reservoir empties sooner.

The failure mode is predictable:

Too much heat → the plant matrix chars → pyrolysis compounds form → acrid, ashy notes rise → the earthy-sweet body disappears behind smoke.

That is why indirect heat suits spikenard: a heated plate, a bed of ash, a coal with a barrier between it and the material. The aim is to warm the root, not to set it on fire. The same principle runs through the wider [incense-making process](/blog/how-incense-is-made/): the heat source and the forming method have to match the material's volatility.

Grinding adds a second trade-off. A fine powder disperses evenly and burns consistently in a stick or cone, but it loses aroma faster in storage and can cake if the moisture is wrong. A coarse powder releases more slowly and may leave a gritty residue. The right particle size is a formula decision, not a universal number. It depends on the binder, the forming method, and the geometry of the burn. The general behaviour of powders is covered in [powder and resin behaviour](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## Formats: stick, cone, loose, and heated plate

**Direct answer:** The same root reads differently in each format because density, airflow, and temperature change the release. A stick slows and smooths it, a cone concentrates it, and a heated plate gives the clearest look at the material itself.

A stick is a composite. The root is mixed with a binder, water, and often other aromatic materials, then formed and dried. Density and binder control how much air reaches the burning zone, which in turn controls temperature. A dense stick burns slowly and evenly, but it can mute a heavy root like spikenard; the sweet-musky body arrives late and may never fully open. A coarser grind and a lighter hand at the press often help.

A cone is denser still, and its shape concentrates heat in the core. The result is more smoke and a faster release. Spikenard in a cone can read stronger and heavier than the same dose in a stick, and the musky base can tip toward acrid if the cone burns hot. The practical adjustment is to reduce the dose or to pair the root with a lighter wood that keeps the core from dominating.

Loose powder on a heated plate is the most direct format. There is no binder to dilute the material and no flame to add combustion notes. The root warms gradually, and the scent unfolds in the order of its volatiles: earthy first, sweet-musky later. The weakness is control. A plate with hot spots can scorch one part of the pile while another part is still cool. Spreading the powder thin and keeping the temperature low gives the cleanest result.

| Format | Heat profile | Effect on 甘松 | Best use | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stick | Slow, steady, mediated by binder | Smooth but sometimes muted | Everyday blending and consistency | Binder masks the base; stick smells flat |
| Cone | Hot core, more airflow | Stronger, heavier, faster | Short, intense sessions | Acrid base from overheating |
| Loose powder on a plate | Low and gradual | Fullest, clearest progression | Evaluating a lot; small rooms | Hot spots scorch the powder |
| Charcoal | High and uneven | Fast, smoky, easy to scorch | Traditional setups with a barrier | Direct contact burns the root |

The format is not a neutral container. It is part of the recipe, and a root that performs well in one format can disappoint in another. A mono-stick test and a plate test answer different questions: the stick shows how the material behaves in a finished product, while the plate shows what the material itself contributes.

## A quality screen you can actually run

**Direct answer:** There is no official grade for 甘松. A buyer judges aroma when warmed, soundness, cleanliness, species, and moisture — in that order — and treats any grade name as sales language.

Start with the nose. Warm a small piece gently and smell it. A sound root gives a clear earthy-sweet-musky impression. A stale root smells flat, dusty, or like old cardboard. A root stored badly may smell of mould before it smells of anything else.

Then break a piece open. The core should be dry and firm. A dry shell around a damp centre is a storage problem waiting to become a mould problem. Look for insect damage, grit, and foreign plant matter while the piece is open.

Then ask what the species is and where it came from. Because wild material is limited and monitored, the name on the sack is not proof of the plant in the sack. A credible lot comes with a species name, a region, and a statement about whether the material was wild-collected or cultivated.

For powder, add a moisture check. Squeeze a handful: it should feel dry and move freely. A powder that clumps in the bag will clump in the hopper, and a damp powder can mould before it reaches a burner.

| What you check | What good looks like | What it may signal | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smell when warmed | Clear earthy-sweet-musky note | Sound, aromatic root | Species identity by itself |
| Broken core | Dry and firm | Adequate drying and storage | That no earlier damage occurred |
| Debris | None visible | Basic cleaning | That the lot is not stretched |
| Powder flow | Free-moving, no clumps | Suitable for blending | Exact water content |
| Paperwork | Species, region, harvest status | A traceable chain | That every claim is true |

No formal grade scale exists. "AAA," "premium," and "wild top grade" are commercial words, not technical categories. The useful question is not "what grade is this?" but "what is it, where did it come from, and will the next lot match?"

## The oil is not the root

**Direct answer:** Western aromatherapy "spikenard" is usually an essential oil; Chinese 甘松 is a dried rootstock. They share a genus and a smell family, not a dosage.

The confusion is structural rather than dishonest. Aromatherapy suppliers sell oils. Incense and herbal suppliers sell dried plant material. Both can use the word "spikenard" because both trace back to the same genus. A customer who orders one and receives the other has bought a real product, just not the one they meant.

The two behave differently. Oil is concentrated and disperses quickly; a drop goes a long way, and the aroma is dominated by the lighter part of the distillate. Root is dilute by comparison and releases slowly, especially under gentle heat. Replacing oil with root, or root with oil, changes strength, timing, and character at once.

There is a historical layer too. Nard was a luxury of the ancient perfume trade, and the word carries associations that no modern product fully owns. That history is worth knowing, but it is not a specification. A label that leans on the romance of nard without naming the species and the form has not told you what you are buying.

## What can stand in for it

**Direct answer:** Substitutes fall into three groups: materials with a similar job, materials from the same family, and materials that share a name or a traditional role. Only the first group is a real blending substitute, and even then the match is partial.

| Material | Relation to 甘松 | Aroma overlap | What it can do | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Cyperus* (香附子) | Unrelated sedge; a traditional *huxiang* root | Some earthy weight, different profile | Add rooty body in a non-critical blend | Reproduce the sweet-musky character |
| Valerian root | Same broad family | Earthy, musky, heavy | Add weight and a musky base | Match the sweetness or the classical record |
| Vetiver root | Different family, also a root | Dry, earthy, smoky | Ground a blend with a cleaner edge | Supply the sweet animalic middle |
| Sandalwood | A wood, not a root | Creamy, soft, warm | Provide a warm base | Act as a root note |
| Spikenard oil | Same genus, distilled | Concentrated version of the root | Add the same family of scent in oil work | Be swapped by weight for powder |

The point of the table is that function and identity are separate axes. *Cyperus* can add weight; it is not spikenard. Valerian can add musk; it is not spikenard. Vetiver can ground a blend; it grounds it in a drier direction. A substitution is a decision about which axis matters for the formula in front of you.

Scarcity creates an incentive to stretch a lot with a cheaper root, and a mixture can still smell "rooty" enough to pass a casual check. The defence is a chain rather than a single test: paperwork, a reference sample, a warm-smell check, and a mono-stick. The [cross-material comparison](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) is a starting point for that kind of check.

## The documentary trail

**Direct answer:** 甘松香 appears in the Song manual *Xiangpu* (香譜) as a familiar incense material. The same tradition records garment-scenting and bathing; the *Bencao Shiyi* (本草拾遗) notes the bathing use with the phrase 浴人身令香.

The documentary trail is modest and clear. *Xiangpu*, the Song manual attributed to 洪芻, lists 甘松香 among the materials an incense maker would know. That listing places the root inside the blended-incense tradition rather than at its edges. It was not a rare curiosity; it was part of the working palette.

The *Bencao Shiyi* reference belongs to materia medica rather than to an incense manual. It describes a bathing use, and the phrase 浴人身令香 says that a bath leaves the body fragrant. That is a record of a practice, not a clinical finding. It should be read as traditional belief, not as evidence of a health effect.

Garment-scenting, 裛衣 (yìyī), is another recorded use. The technique belonged to a world in which scent travelled through cloth, sachets, and stored garments as much as through smoke. A root with a heavy, sweet-musky smell suits that application because it clings and lasts. The same property that makes it a good base note makes it a good material for perfuming textiles.

One caution about reading old sources: a manual's list proves that a material was known and used. It does not prove that every workshop used it the same way. Formulas varied by region, budget, and fashion. The honest claim is that 甘松 has a documented place in the tradition, not that there was one canonical recipe for it.

## Nard before 甘松: the word's long travel

**Direct answer:** The English word arrives from a perfume trade older than the Chinese name. Classical Mediterranean writers list nard among the costly imports of the east, and Indian aromatic practice knows the plant as jatamansi. 甘松 is the Chinese chapter of a much longer story.

The word "nard" has the sound of a luxury about it, and that is not an accident. In the classical Mediterranean world it belonged to the small group of aromatics that were priced by the ounce and kept in sealed jars: frankincense, myrrh, cinnamon, cassia, and nard. These were not everyday smells. They were temple materials, funeral materials, and the kind of gift that signalled wealth. A perfume house that could obtain real nard had something to advertise.

The supply came from far east of the Mediterranean. The exact routes shifted with politics and weather, but the shape was constant: a light, high-value material moved through many hands, and each hand added a markup and a story. By the time a jar reached Rome or Alexandria, the material had acquired a reputation that had little to do with the plant's appearance and everything to do with the distance it had travelled.

India had its own relationship with the plant. Jatamansi is the name that follows the rootstock through Indian aromatic and medicinal practice, and it belongs to a different vocabulary from the Chinese 甘松. The same physical material could be a ritual perfume in one market, a medicinal root in another, and a blending ingredient in a third. Those roles did not always agree about what mattered: scent, potency, appearance, or provenance.

This history explains a modern confusion. A word that once meant a precious imported aromatic now appears on labels for a dried root, a distilled oil, and sometimes a synthetic reconstruction. The old prestige does not tell you which of those you are holding. Only the species and the form do.

## What the mountain makes difficult

**Direct answer:** High, broken ground imposes three pressures on the trade: distance, drying, and traceability. Each one shapes the material before it ever reaches a blender.

Distance is the first. A rootstock growing on open alpine ground may be hours of walking from a road, and the people who gather it are often working a landscape rather than a field. Material accumulates in small bundles, and small bundles accumulate at an aggregator. By the time a sack reaches a drying floor, it may contain the output of many collectors working different slopes.

Drying is the second. A fresh rootstock is heavy with water, and water is expensive to carry. The practical response is to dry as close to the collection point as possible, which means drying conditions vary from place to place. Sun, shade, airflow, and the thickness of the pieces all matter. A lot dried on one slope may behave differently from a lot dried on another, even when both are the same species.

Traceability is the third. A long chain of small handoffs is difficult to document, and documentation is exactly what a monitored trade requires. The result is a familiar tension: the material may be genuine and the paperwork thin, or the paperwork may be complete and the material mixed. Neither condition can be inferred from the other.

These pressures do not make Himalayan material worse. They make it variable, and variability is the thing a buyer has to manage. A consistent lot is not a lucky find; it is the result of someone upstream deciding to sort, dry, and record the material carefully.

## A naming habit written on the tongue

**Direct answer:** 甘松 is named for a taste, not a smell. The same habit runs through Chinese materia medica: 甘草 is the sweet herb, 苦参 the bitter one, 辛夷 the pungent one. A name can record a sensory property without describing the plant.

甘 means sweet. 松 is a pine. Put together, the name says "sweet pine," which is doubly misleading: the plant is not a pine, and the sweetness is a taste rather than the musk that dominates the smell. The name survives because it was useful to the people who coined it. A root that tasted sweet was a root with a property worth remembering.

The habit is old and widespread. 甘草 (gāncǎo, licorice) is literally "sweet herb." 苦参 (kǔshēn) is "bitter ginseng," named for a taste that makes it unpleasant raw. 辛夷 (xīnyí) carries 辛, pungent. These names are not botanical descriptions; they are notes from the mouth, the hand, and the nose, compressed into two characters.

For a buyer, the lesson is that a Chinese name is not a specification. It may encode a taste, an appearance, a place, or a function. 甘松 tells you that someone once found the root sweet. It does not tell you the species, the form, or the grade. The binomial does that, and the binomial is a modern tool.

## Root, rhizome, and the trade's loose word

**Direct answer:** The traded part is a rootstock — a thickened underground stem with roots attached — but the trade calls it a root. The shorthand is harmless in conversation and unhelpful in identification.

Botanically, a root is an organ that absorbs water and nutrients; a rhizome is a horizontal stem that can produce roots and shoots. The piece sold as 甘松 is a mixture: a swollen underground stem, the roots that grow from it, and the remains of the stem base. "Rootstock" is the honest word for that mixture. "Root" is the trade's shorthand.

The distinction matters when a supplier describes material as "root only" or "rhizome only." Those descriptions imply a sorting step that may or may not have happened. They also affect behaviour: a piece with more stem tissue may dry differently, grind differently, and release aroma differently from a piece that is mostly fine root. The difference is not a grade, but it is a physical fact.

For identification, the rootstock is also the part that carries the most distinctive aromatic load. The fine roots smell, but the swollen stem is where the material accumulates. That is why a lot of thin roots can smell weaker than a lot of thick, sound rootstock, even when both come from the same species.

## Roots in the perfumer's palette

**Direct answer:** Spikenard is one of several roots and rhizomes that perfumers use for weight. Orris is powdery and violet-like, costus is warm and oily, vetiver is dry and smoky, valerian is heavy and musky. The differences are large enough that the materials are not interchangeable, even when they occupy the same broad base-note territory.

Orris comes from the rhizome of certain iris species, and its smell is unlike anything else in this group: cool, powdery, faintly violet, with a softness that reads as cosmetic rather than earthy. It is used at low doses as a fixative and a texture, not as a heavy floor. Put orris beside spikenard and the contrast is immediate: one is powder and pale light, the other is damp earth and musk.

Costus is a root from the Asteraceae, and its character is warmer and oilier than spikenard's, with an animalic quality that can read as scalp-like or old wood. It has a long history in perfumery and a complicated regulatory history in some markets. The sensory overlap with spikenard is real but partial: both are heavy roots, but costus is more oily and less sweet.

Vetiver is a grass root, and its earthiness is drier and smokier than spikenard's. It grounds a blend without supplying the sweet-musky middle that 甘松 contributes. A formulator who substitutes vetiver for spikenard will get weight, but the blend will lose its roundness and gain a dusty edge.

Valerian is the closest botanical relative in this group, and the closest in smell: earthy, musky, heavy, with a sweetness that appears at low dose. It is also more animalic and less sweet than spikenard, and its traditional role is European rather than Chinese. The two can sit in the same family and still be different tools.

| Material | Family | Part used | Smell | Role | Difference from 甘松 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spikenard | Caprifoliaceae | Rootstock | Earthy, sweet, musky | Grounding body | — |
| Orris | Iridaceae | Rhizome | Powdery, violet-like, cool | Fixative, texture | Paler, cooler, less earthy |
| Costus | Asteraceae | Root | Warm, oily, animalic | Heavy base | Oillier, less sweet |
| Vetiver | Poaceae | Root | Dry, earthy, smoky | Dry base | Drier, smokier, less musky |
| Valerian | Caprifoliaceae | Root | Earthy, musky, heavy | Heavy base | More animalic, less sweet |

The table is a map, not a licence. A blend that needs weight can use any of these; a blend that needs the particular sweet-musky register of 甘松 can use only a close relative, and even then the match is approximate. The mistake is to treat "root note" as a single job when the materials inside it do different work.

## Scenting cloth, and the bath

**Direct answer:** Classical texts describe 甘松 in garment-scenting and bathing as well as in incense. The techniques belong to a world where scent was carried by cloth and water, not only by smoke.

Garment-scenting, 裛衣 (yìyī), was a layered practice. Aromatic materials were enclosed with cloth in a chest, a box, or a sachet, and the textile absorbed the smell over time. The technique rewards materials that cling: roots, resins, and musks rather than fresh flowers. A root with a heavy, sweet-musky profile suits the job because it releases slowly and leaves a trace in the fibre.

The bath use recorded in the *Bencao Shiyi* is a different application of the same property. The phrase 浴人身令香 describes a bath that leaves the body fragrant. The mechanism is simple: warm water opens the material, aromatic compounds partition into the water and the steam, and some remain on the skin. The effect is modest and temporary, which is why the practice belonged to a culture of layered scent rather than to a single dramatic gesture.

Both uses are traditional practices, not clinical treatments. The records describe what people did and what they noticed. They do not establish a health effect, and this page makes no medical claim. What they do establish is that 甘松 was understood as a material with staying power — a root that could perfume a garment or a bath because it did not flash off in minutes.

That understanding is useful to a modern formulator. A material that clings to cloth is a material that will cling in a base. The old applications are evidence about physical behaviour, and physical behaviour is what a blend is built on.

## The jar and the caravan

**Direct answer:** Nard travelled as a luxury because it was light, potent, and hard to substitute. The economics of the journey shaped the material's reputation more than any botanical property.

A perfume material that loses half its value on the road is not worth carrying. Nard did not. A small quantity perfumed a large batch of oil or a chest of cloth, and the smell survived the journey in a sealed jar. That combination — high value per gram, stable in storage, easy to hide — made it a natural long-distance good.

The trade that carried it was layered. Growers and gatherers sold to local intermediaries; intermediaries sold to merchants who moved material between regions; merchants sold to perfumers and apothecaries at the far end. Each layer added cost and distance, and each layer had an incentive to stretch the material. Ancient writers on aromatics complain about adulteration, and the complaint is as old as the trade itself.

For a modern buyer, the lesson is not that ancient nard was fake. It is that a valuable aromatic under pressure attracts substitution, and the pressure has not gone away. The names change; the economics do not. A scarce root with a famous name is exactly the kind of material that gets stretched.

The caravan story also explains why "nard" became a word for luxury rather than a word for a plant. A material that arrives from far away, in a sealed jar, at great cost, acquires a meaning beyond its chemistry. That meaning is real in the market even when it is unreliable in the bag.

## Sachets, chests, and the slow release

**Direct answer:** Scenting cloth is a study in slow release. The fibre absorbs aromatic compounds, holds them, and gives them back over weeks. A root that clings is worth more for this use than a flower that shouts and vanishes.

The technique varied, but the principle did not. Aromatic material was enclosed with cloth in a chest, a box, or a padded sachet. Sometimes the material sat in a separate pouch; sometimes it was layered between sheets of paper or fabric. The aim was to keep the scent close without staining the textile, and to let time do the work.

Fibres are not inert. Wool, silk, and linen all take up volatile compounds to different degrees, and a fibre that holds scent well also holds it for a long time. That is why garment-scenting favoured roots, resins, and musks over fresh florals: the heavy materials stayed, while the light ones disappeared in a day.

The physical mechanism is the same one that operates in a blend. A material with a low evaporation rate lingers; a material with a high rate flashes off. The difference is that cloth gives the material a surface to cling to, which slows the loss further. A root that performs well in a sachet will usually perform well as a base note, because both applications reward the same property.

This is a useful bridge between the old craft and the modern formula. The sachet was a slow-release device. A base note is a slow-release device. The materials that worked in one tend to work in the other.

## A root among resins

**Direct answer:** Roots and resins both supply weight, but they supply it differently. A resin glues and sweetens; a root textures and dries. Putting them together can produce a strong base or a smoky mess, depending on the heat.

A resin such as frankincense or myrrh brings a balsamic film. It coats the blend, softens transitions, and leaves a sweetness that lingers. A root such as spikenard brings a granular earthiness and a musk that sits closer to the skin. The two are complementary because they are not the same kind of weight.

The combination has a failure mode. Resins can burn smoky, and roots can scorch. Put them together at high heat and the base turns acrid: the resin's smoke and the root's char reinforce each other. At low heat the same combination can be excellent, with the resin supplying lift and the root supplying depth.

That is why the heat source matters as much as the recipe. A blend that works on a heated plate may fail on a coal, not because the materials are wrong but because the temperature is wrong. The formulator's job is to match the material to the heat, not to expect the material to survive any heat.

The comparison also clarifies what spikenard is not. It is not a resin, and it cannot do a resin's job of gluing a blend together. It is not a wood, and it cannot supply a creamy body. It is a root, with a root's particular kind of weight, and its value depends on using it as one.

## Grade names, and what they are worth

**Direct answer:** "Wild," "premium," "AAA," and "hand-picked" are commercial words, not technical categories. They can describe a real difference or conceal the absence of one. The questions that matter are species, origin, harvest status, and behaviour in the nose.

A grade name is a shortcut, and shortcuts are useful when the thing they point to is standardized. In coffee, tea, and timber, some grade terms are anchored to measurable criteria. In the aromatic trade they are often not. A seller who writes "AAA spikenard" has told you that the seller thinks highly of the lot. That is not nothing — a reputation is a kind of evidence — but it is not a specification.

| Claim | What it could mean | What to ask | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wild | Collected from self-sown plants | Which region, and under what permit | That the harvest was sustainable |
| Cultivated | Grown from planted stock | Who grew it, and for how long | That the material is inferior |
| Premium | The seller's better lot | Better by what measure | That it suits your formula |
| AAA | An in-house sorting tier | What the tiers are based on | That the tier is recognized |
| Hand-picked | Sorted by hand | Sorted for what defect | That the lot is uniform |
| Himalayan | A broad region | A specific valley or district | That the species is correct |

The useful move is to treat a grade name as a question rather than an answer. "Premium" invites "premium compared with what?" "Wild" invites "which permit?" "Himalayan" invites "which district?" A seller with a real supply chain can answer. A seller with a story will change the subject.

This is not a demand for laboratory paperwork on every purchase. A small buyer may never see a permit. The point is to know which claims are doing work and which are decoration, so that the price reflects the material rather than the adjective.

## The word "wild", and the word "cultivated"

**Direct answer:** Wild means self-sown and unplanted; cultivated means deliberately grown. Neither word is a quality grade, and neither settles the question of sustainability or legality on its own.

Wild material is variable by definition. It comes from plants that were not selected, irrigated, or spaced by a grower, and it carries the range of that uncontrolled background: different ages, different sizes, different aromatic loads. That variability can be an advantage for a blender looking for character and a disadvantage for a manufacturer looking for consistency.

Cultivated material is more uniform, but uniformity is not the same as quality. A well-grown root can be excellent; a poorly grown one can be bland. Cultivation also changes the supply chain: a planted crop is easier to trace, easier to scale, and often easier to document. Those are practical advantages that have nothing to do with the romance of the wild.

The boundary worth holding is that "wild" does not mean "organic," "sustainable," or "legal," and "cultivated" does not mean "inferior." Each word answers one question — how the plant was sourced — and leaves the other questions open. A buyer who treats either word as a complete specification has stopped asking.

For a formula, the relevant test is behaviour: does the lot smell right, does it burn right, and will the next lot match? The harvest label is context for that test, not a substitute for it.

## Paperwork for a listed material

**Direct answer:** For a CITES-listed aromatic, the file travels with the goods: species, country of origin, harvest status, permit reference, and a chain from collector to exporter. The paper does not prove quality, but it makes someone accountable for the claim.

A CITES Appendix II export normally requires a permit. Behind the permit sit two judgments: a scientific finding that the export will not be detrimental to the species' survival, and a legal-acquisition finding that the specimens were obtained lawfully. Those judgments belong to the exporting country's authorities. The buyer's job is narrower: confirm that the permit matches the lot in front of them.

The match is where problems appear. A permit names a species, a country, a quantity, and a description. A sack on a loading dock names none of those unless someone writes them down. When the description says "dried plants" and the invoice says "spikenard root," the gap is not a technicality. It is the space in which a substitution can travel.

The chain has several handoffs:

Collector or harvest crew → local aggregator → drying and cleaning point → exporter → importer → warehouse.

Each arrow is a chance for material to be mixed, swapped, or relabelled. The permit covers the export, not the integrity of every earlier step. A lot file should therefore record who sold to whom, in what quantity, and under what description.

| Document | What it should show | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Export permit | Species, country, quantity, reference | That the bag matches the permit |
| Phytosanitary certificate | Plant-health compliance | Species identity or aroma quality |
| Invoice | Seller, buyer, quantity, description | That the description is accurate |
| Chain-of-custody note | Handoffs from collector to exporter | That every handoff was honest |
| Sample record | What was checked, when, by whom | That the whole lot matches |

Two boundaries matter. Import rules differ by country and change over time; this page describes the general shape of the system, not the law of any jurisdiction. And a permit does not make a harvest sustainable in a broader sense. It records that an authority made a finding at a point in time. Sustainability is a longer argument about rates, habitat, and alternatives.

For a buyer, the routine is unglamorous: keep the permit reference in the lot file, photograph the labels, and record the quantity received against the quantity declared. If a supplier cannot produce a permit reference for a listed material, that is not a paperwork inconvenience. It is a reason to stop and ask what is actually being sold.

## The words we use for smell

**Direct answer:** Smell language is useful when it points to a direction and a weight. It becomes noise when it borrows the authority of wine reviews or medical language without adding anything a second sample could confirm.

"Earthy" is a direction, not a smell. It can mean damp soil, dry bark, a cellar, or a root pulled from the ground, and two people can use the same word for different experiences. "Rooty" is narrower: it points at the particular sweetness-and-bitterness that underground plant parts tend to share. "Musky" describes a warm, skin-adjacent quality that sits close to the body rather than lifting away from it. "Sweet" needs a modifier to be useful — root sweet, floral sweet, and resin sweet are different destinations.

The wine-review habit of piling on adjectives feels precise and is often the opposite. A description that says "notes of forest floor, antique leather, and dried fig" may be evocative, but it does not tell a buyer whether the next lot will match. A description built on three axes does: direction (earthy, woody, resinous), weight (light, medium, heavy), and texture (dry, creamy, musky). Those three can be checked against a reference sample without a poet.

This is a boundary worth stating: a descriptor is not a grade. "Earthy" covers fresh roots and mouldy ones. "Musky" covers a clean animalic warmth and a stale, dusty base. The word only becomes useful when it is tied to a sample and a date. That is why a written note on the bag — three axes, plus a comparison to the reference — beats a paragraph of adjectives.

The practical consequence is modest but real. If two people on the same team use the same three-axis frame, a disagreement about a lot becomes a disagreement about a specific axis rather than about taste in general. That is easier to resolve, and easier to record for the next purchase.

## Room, nose, and the same stick twice

**Direct answer:** The room and the nose change what a stick seems to be. Volume, airflow, humidity, and fatigue can make one lot read heavy in a small room and thin in a large one.

A small room concentrates everything, including the smoke. A large room dilutes it. A room with strong airflow carries the scent away before it accumulates, while a still room lets it pool. None of these differences is a property of the root, but all of them shape the impression a person forms.

Nose fatigue is the other variable. After a few minutes in the same space, the nose stops registering what is constant and starts noticing change. A stick that smelled rich at first can seem to fade, not because the material stopped releasing aroma but because the observer adapted to it. Leaving the room for a few minutes and returning gives a truer reading than sniffing harder.

The practical rule is to judge a stick in the room where it will be used, at the distance where people will sit. A shop test in a large, ventilated space is a different experiment. That does not make the shop test wrong; it makes it a test of a different situation.

## Storage, and the slow loss

**Direct answer:** Whole root keeps longer than powder. Both need dry, dark, cool, airtight storage. Moisture is the fast enemy; aroma loss is the slow one.

Aromatic compounds are volatile. They leave the material over time, and they leave faster when the surface area is large and the temperature is high. A whole root has a small surface-to-mass ratio, so it holds its scent longer. A powder has a large one, so it releases more at first and fades sooner.

Moisture drives the other failure. Dried root is hygroscopic; it takes water from humid air. Once the water content rises, mould and mustiness follow, and the aroma is spoiled before any visible growth appears.

Damp storage → water uptake → microbial activity → musty off-notes → the sweet-musky character is masked → the lot is unusable for fine blending.

The routine is simple: sealed container, away from light and heat, checked periodically. Whole root can be broken down as needed. Powder should be bought in quantities that will be used within a reasonable period. If a powder has started to clump, smell it and inspect it rather than assuming it is fine. The [incense storage guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) covers the wider principles.

## Buying: the questions that matter

**Direct answer:** Ask for species, form, origin, wild-or-cultivated status, and a sample. Then smell the sample warm, break a piece open, and compare it with a reference lot.

A workable order:

1. **Species.** Check the species name. A label reading only "spikenard" has not answered the question.
2. **Form.** Confirm whether the offer is whole root, sliced root, powder, or oil. Price and use differ for each.
3. **Origin.** Ask for a region, not a continent.
4. **Harvest status.** Ask whether the material was wild-collected or cultivated. The answer affects paperwork, legality, and consistency.
5. **Sample.** Warm a piece and smell it. Break it open and check the core. Compare it with a lot you already trust.
6. **Repeatability.** Ask whether the next lot will match the sample. A single good lot is a sample; a repeatable lot is a supply.

Each question narrows the space of what could be in the bag. Species and form remove the largest confusions. Origin and harvest status address the supply-chain risks. The sample addresses the material itself. None of the questions is exotic; the problem is that buyers often stop after the first one.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Smoke is the part of incense that has nothing to do with scent. A smouldering stick gives off fine particles and combustion gases alongside the aroma, so the room needs air and the burner needs a stable place out of reach.

A root is plant material, and burning plant material always produces more than fragrance. The aromatic fraction is only one part of the plume; the rest is gas and particulate. Clear the smoke rather than sitting in it, and treat a sealed room as the wrong room for any incense.

Nothing here is medical advice, and no claim is made that burning 甘松 treats, cures, or prevents illness. General precautions for smoke, heat, and open flame are collected in the [safety guide](/safety/).

## Summary

甘松 is the cured rootstock of a small Himalayan herb, and it smells like the ground it came from: wet earth, old timber, a root sweetness, and a musk that stays close to the skin. In Chinese *huxiang* it is a floor rather than a headline, so it is blended instead of burned alone. The Song-era *Xiangpu* treats 甘松香 as part of the working palette, and materia-medica texts describe aromatic and bathing uses in traditional language. Wild collection and the CITES listing make provenance part of the product. The two lasting risks are mistaking distilled oil for root and accepting a stretched lot. A repeatable supply beats a romantic grade name.

## FAQ

**Can spikenard be burned on its own?** It can, but it is not at its best that way. A solo burn gives a dense earthy-sweet-musky smell with little contrast, and the heavy fraction can read as flat if the heat is too high. The material's strength is structural: it gives weight to woods and resins and softens their dry edges. That is why the classical use is blended.

**Is 甘松 a kind of pine?** No. The character 松 appears in the name, but the plant is a low herb in the Caprifoliaceae, not a pine tree. The name is a Chinese compound, not a botanical description. A buyer who expects a pine-wood note from the word alone will be surprised by the earthy, musky root.

**Is spikenard oil a substitute for the root?** Not by weight. Oil is concentrated, fast, and dominated by the lighter part of the distillate; root is dilute, slow, and carries the full aromatic profile. Oil can be used in oil-based work, but swapping it for powder in a stick changes the strength and the timing. The form is part of the specification.

**What does CITES Appendix II mean for a small buyer?** It means the trade is monitored, not banned. Export should travel with a permit and the associated findings, and a supplier ought to state the species, the region, and how the root was obtained. The practical takeaway is to keep the permit reference with the lot and to treat missing paperwork as a warning sign.

**Does spikenard equal valerian?** No. They are related in the modern family Caprifoliaceae, and both smell earthy and musky at the root, but they are different species with different chemistry and different traditional roles. Valerian is not a classical substitute for 甘松, and swapping one for the other changes the blend even when the family name matches.

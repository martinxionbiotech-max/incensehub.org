**Direct answer:** A root drug of the carrot family, 藁本 (gǎoběn) is the dried underground portion of *Ligusticum sinense*, a perennial of the Chinese mountains of the centre and west. English catalogues usually render the name as ligusticum. It is a low-intensity, herbaceous, faintly pungent root; its place in Chinese incense is supporting: a middle-register note that gives a classical blend body and continuity. The most cited appearance is a plum accord attributed to the Shouyang princess (寿阳公主梅花香), preserved in the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) with 藁本 at 半两 beside spikenard, angelica, and the plum-and-wood idea the accord is named for.

## The plant behind the name

> **Direct answer:** A carrot-family perennial of the Chinese mountains; its swollen rootstock, not its stems, is the drug.

It is a soft-stemmed perennial of the Apiaceae; fennel, cumin, angelica, and celery belong to the same family. Flora of China puts the plant at half a metre to a metre, with a rootstock the flora describes as swollen at the nodes; buyers want that swelling. The stems and leaves above ground are not the drug, and buyers should not be receiving them.

Elevation is the first clue to its habits: this is a mountain plant, recorded between roughly 500 and 2,700 m, in forest, on scrubby slopes, in grassy clearings, along streams, and on damp ground at the roadside. The flora's provincial list runs to nine: Gansu and Nei Mongol at the northern edge, Shaanxi and Henan through the centre, Sichuan, Yunnan and Guizhou in the south-west, with Hubei and Jiangxi on the eastern rim. Together they trace a belt through the centre and west of the country rather than a single production region. Flowering falls in July and August. Cultivation is recorded alongside wild collection, so the trade draws on both.

Both records are open to anyone: the [species treatment in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200015664) and the corresponding [GBIF entry](https://www.gbif.org/species/6026996). Those records establish what the plant is. They do not tell you what is in a sack, which is a separate question and the one this page spends most of its length on.

It helps to fix the plant by contrast with the materials that dominate incense. Agarwood and sandalwood are woods; frankincense and myrrh are resins; borneol is a crystalline isolate. 藁本 is none of those. It is a soft, starchy root that dries light and brittle, grinds easily, and burns to a modest herbal smoke. That ordinariness is why it is overlooked and why it is useful: it can be dosed generously by resin standards and does not fight for attention.

## One name, more than one plant

> **Direct answer:** 藁本 is a drug category covering two *Ligusticum* species, while the English "lovage" points at a different genus.

The first thing to settle with 藁本 is what the word refers to. It is used at two levels, and the levels do not coincide.

Botanically, the name points to *Ligusticum sinense* — a species with a described morphology, a mapped range, and a flowering window. At the level of the Chinese drug trade, 藁本 is a category, and the category is broader than the species: under the drug name 藁本 the pharmacopoeia admits two species — *L. sinense* and *L. jeholense*, the second often sold as 辽藁本. Two species, one drug name. That is not a scandal; it is how pharmacopoeial categories behave. It does mean that a purchase order reading only "藁本" has not specified a plant.

The second layer is linguistic. English catalogues sometimes gloss 藁本 as "lovage", though the garden lovage they mean belongs elsewhere in the family: *Levisticum officinale*, with its strong celery savour. Calling 藁本 "Chinese lovage" is a convenience, not a synonym. A supplier who translates the name that way may be describing the aroma family rather than the species, and the two can drift apart quickly in a bilingual catalogue.

| Label on the sack | What it may legitimately mean | What it never tells you |
|---|---|---|
| 藁本 | *L. sinense* or *L. jeholense* | Which of the two, or where it grew |
| *Ligusticum sinense* | The species | Whether the identification is correct |
| "Chinese lovage" | A common-name convention for 藁本 | Anything precise; lovage proper is another genus |
| "Ligusticum" | A genus of aromatic Apiaceae | Which species, and whether it is 藁本 at all |
| 川芎 | *L. chuanxiong*, a different drug | Why it is being offered under a nearby name |

The last row causes the most trouble, because 川芎 and 藁本 are close relatives whose histories overlap. The Chinese drug tradition gives them separate status, and the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) gives them neighbouring entries. Present-day treatments place both near *Conioselinum anthriscoides*, and the binomials therefore discriminate less sharply than they once did. The drug name is the steadier identifier here, and a buyer should insist on it.

There is a practical test that follows from all this. Ask for the drug name and the binomial together. A seller who can supply both is working from a specification. A seller who supplies only one is working from a habit, and habits vary by region.

## What the root smells like, and why it reads as medicine

> **Direct answer:** Herbal, faintly spicy, slightly camphoraceous — a middle-register aroma closer to pharmacy than to perfume.

Warm a slice of 藁本 and the first impression is herbal rather than sweet: dry root, a green-woody note, a prickle of spice, and something faintly camphoraceous at the edge. It is not a perfume material in the way a floral absolute is. It smells like the aromatic part of a pharmacy, which is precisely the register it contributes to a blend.

Volatile oil held in the root's tissues carries that character. Carrot-family roots store aromatic compounds in oil ducts distributed through the root body, and the profile of those compounds is what distinguishes one Apiaceae root from another. Drying concentrates them by removing water; grinding releases them by opening the tissue. A root that has been stored badly loses them, and the loss shows as a flat, dusty, hay-like smell with none of the prickle.

In a blend, 藁本 behaves as a middle note. The phrase is used loosely in perfumery, so it is worth being concrete: a middle note is a material whose volatiles leave the matrix after the lightest top notes have gone and before the heaviest base materials have settled in. It occupies the middle minutes of a burn. A blend without one can feel like two separate experiences — a bright opening and a heavy close with nothing joining them.

The mechanism chain deserves spelling out: the root's aroma compounds sit mid-range on the volatility scale → they leave the matrix after the lightest aromatics and before the resins → they occupy the middle of the burn → they fill the interval that would otherwise read as a gap → the blend feels continuous rather than segmented. This is a formulator's model rather than a measured profile; we have not run headspace analysis on burning 藁本, and the model should be tested on the bench rather than trusted as data.

Smelling the root and smelling its smoke are two different exercises, and the difference catches people out. The root, warmed, gives a green-woody herbal note with a prickle of spice. The smoke is drier and flatter, with much of that green edge burned away and a faint char in its place. Combustion destroys some of the compounds that made the root interesting and creates others that were never there. A formulator who chooses a lot on the beauty of its raw aroma may find the smoke disappointing, and a lot that smells ordinary in the jar can burn surprisingly well.

There is a second reason the material reads as "medicinal" to Western noses. The aromatic vocabulary of the carrot family overlaps heavily with the vocabulary of pharmacy and cooking — the same notes that say "herbal root" in a blend say "medicine" or "stock" in another context. That is a cultural association, not a chemical property. A Chinese formulator working in the classical tradition does not describe 藁本 as medicinal; they describe it as a middle-register aromatic with a specific place in an accord. The English word "medicinal" is a translation artifact, and it is worth flagging as such.

## The Shouyang princess formula, and what 半两 is telling you

> **Direct answer:** 半两 is a proportion, not a gram weight: enough to give the middle body, not enough to take over.

The best-documented use of the root is a plum-scented formula attributed to the Shouyang princess (寿阳公主梅花香), which the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) carries in 卷十八 with 藁本 at 半两. The accord sets plum over wood; its herbal middle is carried by 甘松 and 白芷. 藁本 joins that middle group.

Two features of the entry repay attention. The first is the quantity. 半两 is half a liang, a unit from the classical Chinese system, and in the Ming system a liang divides into ten qian. The absolute mass of a liang shifted across dynasties, so the figure is best read as a proportion inside the formula rather than as a gram weight to reproduce. Half a liang is a substantial supporting quantity — larger than the trace additions given to potent materials, smaller than the chief aromatics. It marks 藁本 as a real structural component of the accord, not a seasoning.

The second is the company it keeps. A plum incense needs three things: the plum note itself, a wood base, and a herbal middle that keeps the fruit from reading as confectionery. Spikenard, angelica, and 藁本 do that work together. Take the herbal group out and the plum turns sweet and thin; double it and the blend turns into a herbal preparation with plum in the background. The classical proportion is a balance point, and the recipe records it rather than argues for it.

For the full recipe context, the site's [plum incense walk-through](/blog/shouyang-princess-plum-recipe/) sets out the accord in order. What matters here is the lesson about how classical formulas encode judgment: the weight of a minor ingredient is a decision that was made once, tested, and written down. Reading it as a ratio recovers the decision.

## Processing: dry, roast, grind

> **Direct answer:** Drying fixes the material, roasting decides its character, and grinding decides how it behaves in a stick.

Digging, cleaning, and drying are the common opening of nearly every aromatic root's journey, and 藁本 follows it without ceremony. What distinguishes 藁本 in classical practice is the option to roast before grinding, and the choice changes the material in ways a formulator can hear.

Drying is the first change. A freshly dug root is wet and carries a raw, sharp aromatic edge; as the water goes, the tissue turns brittle and what remains is more concentrated. Dry it slowly in shade and more of the sharp top survives; dry it faster with gentle warmth and the profile rounds. Neither method is wrong, and the gap between them is a matter of degree.

Roasting is the second change, and it decides more. Dry-frying in a clean pan drives off residual moisture, opens the tissue for grinding, and volatilizes a portion of the lighter aromatic compounds. The result is a powder that smells rounder and softer than the raw root, with the prickle reduced. Push the heat too far and the sugars and starches scorch, which introduces an acrid, burnt note that no amount of blending will hide.

The chain runs like this: heat drives off moisture → the tissue becomes brittle → grinding yields a more uniform powder → the lightest volatiles are partly lost → the aromatic profile reads rounder → the powder sits more quietly in the middle register. A second chain describes the failure: excess heat → scorching of starches → acrid and smoky notes → the middle register turns harsh → the blend reads burnt even at low dose. The difference between the two chains is minutes and a pan temperature, which is why roasting is a skill rather than a step.

Grinding follows. Particle size affects how the powder packs, how it burns, and how quickly it loses aroma. A fine powder distributes evenly through a blend and releases quickly; a coarse one can leave visible specks and an uneven burn. Moisture at the moment of grinding matters as much as moisture at storage: a damp root gums the mill and produces a paste rather than a powder.

The wider sequence of turning plant material into a burnable stick is set out in [the stick-making sequence](/blog/how-incense-is-made/); 藁本 is one of the easier materials in that sequence, provided the roast is controlled.

## Grinding: heat, static, and the disappearing top note

> **Direct answer:** Grinding is where a good root most often becomes an ordinary powder. The mill generates heat, heat drives off the lightest volatiles, and static carries the finest fraction onto the equipment instead of into the jar. Both losses are invisible in the sieve record.

A blade mill run hard raises the temperature of its charge within seconds. For a root whose aromatic character lives in moderately volatile compounds, that warmth is a slow subtraction: the powder still smells of 藁本, but the prickle at the edge is gone and the profile reads flatter than the whole root did. The chain is short — fast grinding → heat in the milling chamber → light volatiles leave the powder → the middle register loses its top edge → the blended result reads dustier than the raw material promised.

The practical corrections are unglamorous. Grind in short bursts and let the mill cool between them. Sieve as you go, so the fine fraction leaves the chamber instead of being repeatedly heated. Keep the total milling time as short as the target particle size allows. None of this requires new equipment; it requires attention to a step that most workshops treat as automatic.

Triboelectric charging is the second loss. A dry, fine powder accumulates charge as it moves, clinging to the mill, the sieve, and the scoop. The material that sticks is usually the finest and most aromatic fraction, so the powder that reaches the jar is not the powder that left the root. Grounding the receiving container and letting the powder rest briefly before sealing reduce the loss. So does accepting a slightly coarser target: the finest fraction is the one that behaves worst in both static and storage.

Cross-contamination belongs in the same conversation. A mill that held a resin will leave aromatic residue in the next botanical lot, and a mill that held a pungent root will leave a trace that shows up in a delicate blend. Cleaning between lots is a quality step, not housekeeping.

## Forms, and what each one is for

> **Direct answer:** Whole root for identification, slices for inspection, raw powder for edge, roasted powder for roundness.

| Form | Physical state | Where it belongs | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole rootstock | Dried, unsliced | Identification, long storage, reference samples | Must be milled; interior hidden |
| Sliced root | Cross-sections | Incoming inspection, small batches | More surface, faster aroma loss |
| Raw powder | Ground, unroasted | Blends that want the sharper herbal edge | Can read harsh in the middle register |
| Roasted powder | Dry-fried, then ground | Classical-style formulas; softer middle | Roast must be controlled; easy to scorch |

The choice between raw and roasted powder is a formulation decision, not a quality ranking. A blend that already contains sharp materials may want the rounded version; a blend that needs the herbal note to cut through a heavy base may want the raw. The classical record treats roasting as an option, which suggests both were in use.

The behaviour of root powders next to resins is covered in [powder and resin in a stick](/blog/incense-powder-resin/). The general principle is that powders carry the structure and resins carry the weight, and a root like 藁本 belongs firmly on the structure side.

## A bench protocol for a new lot

> **Direct answer:** Six steps settle a 藁本 lot: read the specification, inspect the cut surface, smell cold then warmed, grind a sample, burn it beside a trusted lot in a formula you know, and write the result down. Each step can stop the lot before the next one wastes an afternoon.

Begin with the paper. The specification should carry the drug name (藁本), the binomial (*L. sinense* or *L. jeholense*), the origin province, the harvest year, and whether the root was raw-dried or roasted. Without both the drug name and the binomial, a lot cannot be approved as 藁本, whatever its appearance suggests.

Inspect before you smell. Break or cut a rootstock across its length and look at the interior: a sound root is pale, firm, and evenly coloured, with no dark streaks, soft patches, or insect channels. The exterior should be dry and free of soil in the crevices, which is where damp material hides.

Smell cold, then warm. Cold, a good lot is faint. Warm a slice in the hand or on a plate for a minute and the herbal, spicy note should lift cleanly. Mustiness, a sour edge, or a scorched smell are disqualifying, and none of them improve with heat.

Grind a small sample. The powder should run dry and free, without coating the fingers or gumming the sieve. Note the fractions that pass and the fractions that stay. A lot that grinds to a paste is a lot that was dried badly, whatever its aroma suggested.

Then burn it. Make two small sticks of a formula you already understand, one with the trusted lot and one with the candidate, and compare them side by side rather than from memory. Listen for where the middle register sits and whether the join between top and base has changed. A candidate that smells right in the jar but shifts the join is not interchangeable.

Record the outcome against the lot number, with the date and the initials of the person who ran the comparison. A protocol that is not written down is a preference, and preferences leave with the person who held them.

## Ash, grit, and what the residue says

> **Direct answer:** Root materials leave more mineral residue than resins or woods, and the amount and colour of that residue are a crude but useful screen for soil load and cleanliness. Ash is a screening signal, not a grade.

Burn a small sample of powder on a heat-safe surface and look at what remains. A clean, well-washed root leaves a pale, even ash in modest volume. A root that was poorly cleaned leaves more, and the extra is largely soil: sand, silt, and clay that were never washed out of the crevices. Visible grit in the ash is the same finding in a more obvious form.

Colour adds a second reading. Dark specks can be scorched tissue, soil, or fragments of stem that should have been picked out. A uniformly pale ash is the ordinary case for a sound root; a mottled ash invites a closer look at the raw material rather than a conclusion about the lot.

The chain from soil to smoke is worth spelling out: soil load → higher mineral residue → more fine particulate carried in the plume → a drier, harsher note in the middle register. That final link is inference rather than instrumentation, and it should be tested rather than assumed. What is not in doubt is the first step: dirty root makes dirty ash, and dirty ash is easy to see.

Two cautions keep the test honest. Ash volume varies with the root's mineral content, which varies with soil and season, so one sample proves little; compare lots from the same supplier over time. And ash says nothing about aroma: a lot can burn clean and smell stale, or leave heavy ash and smell excellent. The test belongs in a set, not at the top of it.

## Grading: what to check and what each check misses

> **Direct answer:** No formal grading scale exists for 藁本. A buyer judges a lot on warmed aroma, rootstock soundness, cleanliness, moisture, and powder behaviour — while remembering that each check is blind to something another one catches.

| Check | What it reveals | What it hides |
|---|---|---|
| Aroma when warmed | Stale, mouldy, or scorched root; overall strength | Whether the species is correct |
| Rootstock density | Thin, immature, or spent material | Internal rot that has not surfaced |
| Cut surface | Soil, insect damage, dark streaks | Uniformity across the sack |
| Moisture | Damp root, caking risk, mould risk | Whether drying was even |
| Powder behaviour | Particle spread, gumming, static | Aroma that was lost before grinding |
| Lot record | Traceability, lot-to-lot consistency | Whether the record is accurate |

The order matters less than the combination. Aroma alone will not tell you the species. Species identification alone will not tell you whether the root was dried properly. A clean cut surface says nothing about the other end of the sack. Buyers who rely on a single check tend to be surprised by the one they skipped, and a lot that has been re-bagged loses its paper trail even when the material is sound — on a small-volume material, a lost trail is a permanent discount.

One boundary is worth stating: strength is not the same as quality. A root can be intensely aromatic because it is fresh and well dried, or because it is a different species with a louder oil profile. For a supporting material, consistency of character matters more than peak intensity. A lot that smells exactly like the last good lot is worth more than a lot that smells stronger and different.

## Telling 藁本 from its neighbours

> **Direct answer:** The practical confusions are three: 藁本 against 川芎, 藁本 against garden lovage, and 藁本 against a generic "ligusticum" label. Each resolves with a different piece of information, and none resolves by smell alone.

| Offered as | Actual plant | Origin | What changes in a blend | How to resolve it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 藁本 | *L. sinense* or *L. jeholense* | China, montane | The intended middle-register herbal note | Accept as the drug category; ask which species |
| 川芎 | *L. chuanxiong* | Sichuan | Stronger, heavier, more penetrating; a different drug | Drug name on the label |
| "Lovage" | Garden lovage, *Levisticum officinale* | Europe, culinary herb | Celery-like, culinary, not the classical note | Binomial; "lovage" alone is not a specification |
| "Ligusticum" | Any *Ligusticum* species | Various | Unpredictable; could be a different aromatic root | Binomial plus origin |
| Roasted 藁本 | *L. sinense*, heat-treated | China | Softer, rounder, less prickle | Processing statement, not species |

The 川芎 confusion matters most, because the two are close relatives and both are legitimate. A blend built with 川芎 where 藁本 was intended will not be ruined, but it will be heavier and more insistent, and the middle register will dominate in a way the formula did not ask for. That is a change of drug, not of supplier.

The lovage confusion is a translation problem, worth correcting wherever it appears in an English-language catalogue. *Levisticum officinale* is a garden herb with a strong savoury flavour; it is not 藁本, and it does not carry the same classical role. A supplier who lists "lovage root" for a Chinese incense material may be translating loosely, or may be selling something else. The binomial settles it.

## When 藁本 is unavailable

> **Direct answer:** There is no true substitute for 藁本. The nearest materials each change the blend in a different direction, and the honest options are to accept a different middle register, rebalance around the gap, or wait for stock.

| Candidate | What it brings | What it alters | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 川芎 (chuanxiong) | A heavier, more penetrating herbal middle | Pushes the register downward and forward; can dominate | Closest in family, furthest in behaviour |
| Angelica (白芷) | A warmer, sweeter aromatic middle | Sweetens the join; shifts the blend toward the base | Useful if the gap is warm, not herbal |
| Spikenard (甘松) | A loud, dense, valerian-like middle | Takes over the blend at any normal dose | Not a substitute; a different subject |
| Asarum (细辛) | A sharp, pungent top-middle | Adds a hard edge rather than filling a gap | Wrong direction, and dosed far smaller |
| Nothing | A thinner middle | Exposes the join between top and base | Sometimes the cleanest answer |

The table is a set of consequences rather than recommendations. Each row answers the same question — what happens to the join if I use this instead — and the answers are not interchangeable.

Omission deserves more respect than it usually gets. A blend with a thin middle is not automatically a broken blend; some compositions are built as a bright top over a deep base and are meant to have air between them. If the formula's identity lives at the top and the base, removing the herbal group may clarify it. The mistake is to fill every gap simply because a gap exists.

If substitution is unavoidable, change one thing and reduce the dose. A material that is louder than 藁本 should enter at a fraction of the weight, and the blend should be judged after a full dry, not immediately after mixing. The middle register is the part of a blend most likely to change during drying, because the volatiles that carry it are the ones most exposed to the air.

## Where it sits in the formula

> **Direct answer:** 藁本 is an assistant, not a chief. It supplies the herbal middle that joins a bright top to a heavy base, and its correct dose is the one that makes the join inaudible.

Read through 君臣佐使 — chief, minister, assistant, guide — 藁本 belongs among the assistants. It does not carry the composition's identity, and it does not extend the chief's aroma. It fills a register. In the plum accord, the chief is the plum-over-wood idea; 藁本 joins the supporting group that gives it a body.

The dose question follows from the role. Too little and the middle register thins, so the blend reads as a bright top sitting on a heavy base with a gap between them. Too much and the herbal note becomes the subject, which is a particular risk with 藁本 because its aroma is more insistent than its modest reputation suggests. The classical 半两 is a useful anchor: substantial enough to be structural, small enough to stay subordinate.

Format shifts the register. A stick, open and drawing air along its length, releases the middle early and lets it fade before the base settles. A pressed cone, denser and slower, holds the middle longer and pushes it closer to the base, which can make a balanced stick feel top-heavy as a cone. The same formula, the same powder, two different temporal shapes. Tuning the middle register is therefore always tuning it for a format, not in the abstract — which is why a formula transferred from stick to cone usually needs the herbal group reduced rather than increased.

A practical rule for a modern bench is to tune the middle register last. Build the top and the base first, then add the herbal group in small increments until the gap closes. Stopping at the point where the join becomes inaudible is the whole skill; the moment you can name the herb in the smoke, you have gone past it.

## Tuning the middle register: three failure modes

> **Direct answer:** A middle note fails in three recognisable ways — a gap, a muddle, or a harsh edge — and each has a different cause. Adding more material fixes only the first. The other two get worse when you add.

The gap is the simplest. The top notes lift and fade, the base settles in, and between them there is a stretch of smoke with nothing to hold the nose. The cause is usually too little of the herbal group, or a herbal group that was ground too coarse to release in the middle window. The correction is a small increase, ground finer, added to the batch rather than to the finished stick.

The muddle is the opposite failure and the more common one in amateur work. The middle note is loud enough to be named in the smoke, and once a listener can name it, the blend has lost its hierarchy: the top and base have become background to the herb. The cause is over-dosing, and the correction is subtraction, not compensation. Adding a brighter top to cover a muddy middle produces a busy blend, not a balanced one.

The harsh edge is a processing failure more often than a dosing failure. Raw root, ground coarse and burned hot, can throw a prickle that reads as acrid rather than herbal. Roasted root taken too far does the same thing with a burnt note instead. The correction is at the pan, not the bench: lower heat, shorter time, and a sample tasted before the whole lot goes through.

Diagnosis is easier in a model blend than in a finished formula. Build three materials — one bright top, one middle, one base — and change one variable at a time. A gap closes when the middle rises; a muddle deepens; a harsh edge persists whatever the dose. That test costs an hour and saves a week of guessing inside a complex formula.

## Sourcing and sustainability

> **Direct answer:** 藁本 reaches the market from wild collection and from cultivation across the centre and west of China, and no CITES-style restriction applies to it — nothing like the controls that surround agarwood or certain orchids. For this material the sourcing work is identification and quality control, not conservation.

The flora describes the species as wild through its range and adds that it is cultivated too. That combination is typical of a mountain aromatic root with a modest market: wild collection supplies the trade where the plant is common, and cultivation fills in where demand or access makes collection unreliable. Neither source is inherently better. Wild material can be excellent if dried well; cultivated material can be more uniform if the grower manages harvest age and handling.

Small materials pass through more hands than large ones, and each hand is a place where a name can drift: collector or grower, then a regional buyer, then a wholesaler who may blend lots from several counties, then the workshop. By the time a sack reaches a bench, the province on the label may describe where the wholesaler bought rather than where the root grew. None of that is deception; it is how low-volume trade works. It does mean the buyer writes the specification and checks it on arrival, because the chain will not.

The conservation picture is calm. No CITES listing covers *Ligusticum sinense*, and no wild-harvest crisis is widely reported. That does not mean the chain is transparent, but a buyer need not treat every purchase as a conservation decision.

Verification is where the real work sits. A low-volume material carrying three plausible names is exactly the kind of product where an error can travel through a supply chain unnoticed for years. The [supplier-vetting framework](/blog/wholesale-guide/) sets out the general case; for 藁本, add the specific requirement that the drug name and the binomial appear together on the specification.

## Storage and handling

> **Direct answer:** Whole root keeps; powder fades. Seal it, darken it, cool it, and buy lots you will finish.

Whole rootstock stores well. Kept dry, dark, and cool, it holds its aroma for a long time, and its density buffers moisture exchange with the air. Sliced root is intermediate: still stable, but with more surface exposed to the room. Powder is the fragile form, and it is the one most workshops buy.

A powder that has lost its aroma smells dusty and flat, with none of the prickle that makes the root recognisable. A powder that has taken up moisture clumps, gums the sieve, and burns unevenly. Both failures happen quietly, which is why storage discipline matters more than storage equipment.

The rules are ordinary: sealed containers, out of light, away from warm equipment; lots sized to use within a season; labels carrying species, drug name, origin, processing (raw or roasted), and receipt date; no topping up of old stock with new. Whole and sliced root keep longer under the same conditions.

Roasted powder is more hygroscopic than raw in our experience, and loses its rounded aroma faster. Buy or roast it in smaller lots; treat the roast date as part of the lot identity.

Season matters more than most workshops admit. Powder that behaves in a dry winter can cake within weeks in a humid summer, and that is not the material's fault. If stock rotates slowly, buy less and more often rather than adding a desiccant and hoping. A desiccant manages the headspace of a sealed jar; it does not dry powder that went in damp.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The risks attached to 藁本 are the ordinary risks of burning aromatic plant material and of grinding it into dust. Smoke and suspended particulate call for ventilation; powder calls for extraction and a mask. Nothing in this entry supports a medical claim, and material bought for burning should not be treated as a food or a medicine.

Burning any aromatic produces smoke and airborne particulate, and those are the exposures that matter at the censer. Work in moving air, keep your face out of the plume, and store both raw material and finished product out of reach of children and household animals. The general case is set out in the site's [safety guide](/safety/).

Grinding adds a second exposure. Root dust stays suspended after the mill stops, and a small workshop without extraction will breathe it for hours. A fitted mask, local extraction, and a settling period before cleaning are ordinary practice for any botanical powder, not a special precaution for this one.

One boundary is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to cross. 藁本 is a materia medica item and an incense material; it is not sold to a food standard, and incense-grade lots are not screened for the contaminants that a food or pharmaceutical supply chain would screen for. Do not brew it, chew it, or treat a workshop purchase as a remedy. That is not a comment on the plant's traditional reputation; it is a comment on the difference between material grades.

## The record and the claim

> **Direct answer:** The incense use of 藁本 is documented in a named classical formula. The medicinal use is traditional. The taxonomy is unsettled. Keeping those three statements apart is the whole of the evidence question.

The documented fact is narrow and checkable: in 卷十八 of the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘), the 寿阳公主梅花香 formula gives 藁本 at 半两, with 甘松 and 白芷 beside it. From that record we can say the material was used in a specific accord, at a specific proportion, in a specific text. We cannot say it was used everywhere, or that the formula was typical.

The root's medicinal reputation belongs to the older materia-medica literature: old, recorded, and not clinical evidence in the modern sense. This page does not present it as treatment or prevention, and no claim of that kind appears anywhere in the entry.

Taxonomy is the third strand, and the one most likely to mislead a present-day reader. The genus has been reorganized, and current treatments associate both 藁本 and 川芎 with *Conioselinum anthriscoides*. A reader who looks up the current binomial may conclude that the two drugs are the same plant. The Chinese drug tradition does not; it keeps the two apart, as the *Bencao Gangmu* did long before modern taxonomy. When the botanical name and the drug name disagree, the drug name is the safer guide to what a formula intended.

Reading a classical formula responsibly means holding three things at once: the text, its date, and its purpose. The *Xiangcheng* is a Ming compilation, so a formula inside it may be older than the book, and the weights belong to the compiler's system rather than to the original author's. The purpose is practical — these are workshop instructions, not pharmacological arguments — which is why they give quantities and procedures rather than reasons. A reader who wants reasons has to infer them from the proportions, and that inference should be labelled as inference.

## FAQ

**What kind of material is 藁本?** A dried rootstock-and-root drug from a carrot-family perennial, *Ligusticum sinense*, gathered and farmed in the mountains of the centre and west. Inside a blend it fills a middle register: not a top note that opens the burn, and not a base that closes it.

**How do I tell 藁本 from 川芎 on paper?** By the drug name first and the binomial second. The 藁本 category covers *L. sinense* and *L. jeholense*; 川芎 is *L. chuanxiong*. Modern taxonomy has shuffled both, which is exactly why the drug name, not the binomial, is the steadier identifier on a purchase order.

**Does roasting change the material?** Yes, in a direction most formulators want. Dry-frying drives off residual moisture and part of the lightest volatile fraction, so the powder reads rounder and less prickly than raw root. Take the pan too far and the starches scorch, leaving an acrid note that no blend will hide.

**Why does the plum formula use half a liang?** Because the herbal group is structural in that accord rather than decorative. 半两 gives the middle register real body while staying beneath the plum and its woods. Treat it as a proportion from a classical text, not as a gram weight to copy.

**Is there a standard grade for 藁本?** No. No published scale covers incense-grade 藁本. A lot is judged on aroma, soundness, cleanliness, moisture, and lot-to-lot consistency, and a buyer who wants "a grade" is really asking for a specification — which the buyer writes, not the market.

**Does 藁本 work in a sachet?** Only weakly. A sachet delivers cold aroma, and this root's character is built for combustion: the cold material is faint, and the herbal register barely reaches the nose. Its place is in a burning blend.

## Summary

藁本 fills a narrow slot: a herbal middle layer, drawn from the rootstock of *Ligusticum sinense* (and of *L. jeholense* in trade), that ties a bright opening to a heavy close. Its documented home is a plum accord credited to the Shouyang princess, carried in 卷十八 of the Ming *Xiangcheng* at 半两. Commercially, its difficulties are naming difficulties — 川芎 on one shoulder, garden lovage on the other. Quality turns on scent, sound condition, and evenness; no formal grade exists. The safety notes are the ordinary ones for burned botanicals, plus the reminder that incense-grade stock is not food.

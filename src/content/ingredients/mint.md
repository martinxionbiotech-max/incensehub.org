**Direct answer:** 薄荷 (bòhé) names the leaf-and-top herb of a perennial mint that spreads by underground rhizome and grows across East Asia in damp ground. Botanical databases usually file the plant under *Mentha canadensis*, keeping *Mentha haplocalyx* as the older synonym. Its scent is menthol-cool and fresh, and in incense it behaves as a fleeting top note rather than a base. Its home is the Chinese kitchen and the old herbals, not the peppermint of European commerce.

## Key facts

- **Name:** 薄荷 (bòhé) — the everyday mint of Chinese cooking and old herbals.
- **Species:** *Mentha haplocalyx*, listed in global databases as *M. canadensis*.
- **Family:** Lamiaceae.
- **Habit:** a perennial that spreads by underground stem, with knee-high upright growth.
- **Season:** flowers in the second half of summer.
- **Ground:** damp places from lowland to upland, in every province.
- **Part used:** leaf and flowering top, fresh or cured.
- **Smell:** cold, green, lightly sweet.
- **Role:** a brief cooling accent in blends; a kitchen herb.
- **Claims:** none therapeutic.

## Three plants behind one English word

> **Direct answer:** Three commercial plants hide behind the English word, and Chinese gives each its own name. 薄荷 is the East Asian herb; 留兰香 is spearmint; 胡椒薄荷 is peppermint. Neither the names nor the smells can be swapped.

Settle the species first. Scent, price, behaviour in a mix and even the wording on the label all follow from which plant is in the sack, and none of them can be judged until that is known. Order "mint," receive spearmint, and the paperwork may be perfectly legal. The formulator has still been misled, because the invoice described a category while the sack contained a plant.

| Chinese | Species | Common name | Character | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 薄荷 (bòhé) | *M. haplocalyx*, the East Asian species | Chinese mint | Green-sweet and menthol-led | Cooking; a cooling accent in blends |
| 留兰香 (liúlánxiāng) | *Mentha spicata* | Spearmint | Sweet, round, carvone-driven | Gum, sweets, gentle cooking |
| 胡椒薄荷 (hújiāo bòhé) | *Mentha × piperita* | Peppermint | Hard and cold, with camphor | Oral care and forceful mint accords |

The chemistry behind the table is simple enough to remember. Carvone gives spearmint its round, confectionery sweetness. Menthol gives peppermint its hard, cold edge. Against peppermint, the Chinese plant feels gentler and less camphor-driven. It is a separate species rather than a variant, and its reputation at home is a kitchen one, not a pharmacy one.

Where a supplier uses only the English word, the burden falls on the buyer to ask for the binomial. That is not pedantry. The three plants behave differently in a blend, and a substitution made without telling anyone changes the finished product.

## Botany: rhizome, stem and range

> **Direct answer:** The species is a herbaceous perennial that survives on an underground stem and grows in wet places, standing roughly knee-high. Its natural range is huge — China, much of monsoon Asia, and North America — which is exactly why the East Asian name was absorbed into a wider species concept.

Start with the organ that explains everything else: the underground stem, or rhizome. It creeps through saturated soil, throws up shoots at intervals, and lets one plant colonise a whole ditch. Upright stems follow, standing about knee-high, with flowers from late summer into early autumn. Wet feet are no problem for it; coastal flats and upland valleys both suit it, and the altitude record runs to 3,500 m.

The geography is just as broad. Chinese material occurs in every province, and the same species turns up across mainland Southeast Asia, Korea, Japan and the Russian far east, with separate populations in North America. Botanists who worked province by province once saw several species in that spread. When specimens were lined up across the whole range, the distinctions dissolved into gradients, and the East Asian name was reduced to a synonym of the wider one.

Clonal growth has a commercial consequence. Because a patch often expands from one rhizome system, a field can be a single genotype, and successive harvests from it behave alike. Change the planting stock and the scent can move without any change in species, soil or method. That is why a buyer who values consistency should ask not only which species is grown but which stock, and why a new supplier's first lot deserves a burn test rather than a signature.

The route from ditch to sack is short:

- rhizome creeps → shoots crowd the patch → the canopy closes and weeds lose light → the crop is cut at flowering → air-drying shrinks the leaf and stem → the lot carries the signature of one field and one season.

This is the practical difference between an agricultural aromatic and a forest one. A resin accumulates over years and can be blended across harvests; a herb is a snapshot of a single growing season, and its quality is decided long before it reaches a blender.

## The cold signal is a nerve effect

> **Direct answer:** Menthol lowers no temperature. It triggers the nerve endings that report cold, so the brain registers a chill in tissue that has not changed. The effect is real, but it is a perception.

This distinction matters more than it first appears. Read "cooling" on a product page and you could be looking at any of three claims: a menthol effect on the nerves, a fresh smell that the culture reads as cool, or an adjective borrowed from the mint family for atmosphere. Only the first is a physiological effect, and only the first behaves predictably in a blend.

The mechanism is a chain of perception rather than thermodynamics:

- menthol contacts a cold-sensitive nerve ending → the channel opens as it would under actual cooling → a signal travels to the brain → the brain reports cold → the skin or mouth feels cool while the tissue temperature is unchanged.

Because the effect is neural, it fades with exposure. A strong menthol impression is followed by a period of reduced sensitivity — the same reason a strong mint sweet feels less intense by the end of the packet. In an incense context this matters for evaluation: the first minute of a burn is not a fair sample of the note's strength, because the nose adapts quickly.

The second meaning of "cooling" — the associative one — is where most fragrance writing lives. A blend can read cool because it contains green, watery or camphoraceous notes that the culture has learned to associate with freshness. No menthol is required. A formulator who wants the sensation needs the compound; a formulator who wants the impression has more options.

## A top note with a short lease

> **Direct answer:** Mint evaporates quickly, which means it arrives at the nose first and departs first. That makes it an excellent lifter and a poor anchor, and it sets a hard ceiling on the share of a blend it can usefully occupy.

Volatility is a physical fact, and it shapes everything the material does on heat. Weight decides timing. Light molecules lift off at modest heat, heavy resin molecules need much more, so a mint accent peaks in the first seconds and has vanished by the time the resins begin to speak.

The chain runs like this:

- a burning stick develops a heat front behind the ember → volatile compounds ahead of the front vaporise first → menthol and its relatives reach the nose in the opening seconds → the compounds that need more heat — resins, woods, roots — release later → the mint note has already peaked while the base is still warming.

Experienced blenders treat the dose as a ceiling, not a target. The right amount is the least that can be detected, not the most the recipe will bear. Push the dose and the composition collapses into toothpaste or room spray: the opening is all cold sweetness, the heavier materials never get a chance to establish themselves, and the finish feels hollow.

What counts as an overdose depends on the format. A cone burns hotter and faster than a stick, so the same percentage reads sharper. A loose powder burned on charcoal releases in a different curve again. The only reliable method is to test in the intended format, at the intended dose, with the intended base — not in a perfume blotter and not in a different binder.

There is also a perception effect that catches people out. Menthol's cold signal is strong enough to dominate a blend subjectively even when its measured share is small. Identical percentages do not guarantee identical results. A leaf-based version and an oil-based version of the same formula smell different, since leaf tissue gives up its scent slowly while oil gives it up at once.

## The cooling shelf: four materials compared

> **Direct answer:** Cold is not one note but a small family, and mint is its lightest member. Borneol and camphor are crystals with a denser, more medicinal chill; eucalyptus oil sits in between. Pick by the weight of the composition the chill has to survive.

A blender who wants "something cooling" is really choosing among four tools with different masses. Lumping them together produces the familiar failure in which a delicate floral turns into a liniment.

| Material | Usual form | What the cold feels like | How it behaves over heat | Chinese |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mint | Leaf or oil | Bright, green-sweet, sharp at the edges | Gone in the opening; little residue | 薄荷 |
| Borneol | Crystal | Sweet-medicinal, camphor-adjacent, calm | Steady sublimation; carries into the middle | 龙脑 / 冰片 |
| Camphor | Crystal | Blunt, penetrating, faintly industrial | Rushes out early, then sits heavy | 樟脑 |
| Eucalyptus | Oil | Thin, clean, slightly medicinal | Brief and shallow; lifts without warming | 尤加利 |

Treat the table as an editorial map for blending, not a pharmacopoeial classification. Its value is comparative: it shows why two materials that both "feel cold" can sit at opposite ends of a burn.

Borneol earns its place in the classical palette for a physical reason. It passes from solid to vapour without burning, so a stick can release it slowly as heat travels, keeping a cool thread alive well past the opening. Mint cannot do that; its compounds are already mobile at low temperature and leave almost at once. Substituting one for the other is not a matter of adjusting a number — it changes where in the burn the coolness lives.

Camphor is the blunt instrument. Its odour drags associations of mothballs and muscle rub, which suits some compositions and ruins others. Eucalyptus is the opposite extreme: it lifts and freshens without sweetness, useful when a formula already has sugar from flowers or fruit. Mint's own advantage is that it supplies cold and sweetness together, which is why it fits floral and culinary directions that would reject either crystal.

The rule that follows is simple. Match the cooling agent to the section of the burn that needs it: mint for the first breath, borneol for the middle, camphor only where its weight is wanted. A light composition can carry mint alone; a resin-heavy one needs a crystal to keep the chill from being swallowed.

## Where mint sits in a 君臣佐使 formula

> **Direct answer:** Classical 君臣佐使 (chief, minister, assistant, guide) thinking places mint in the assistant or guide seat, never the chief seat. It supports and directs the opening of a composition; it does not carry the composition's identity.

The 君臣佐使 framework is a way of talking about hierarchy in a formula. The 君 (chief) sets the character; the 臣 (minister) supports and extends it; the 佐 (assistant) modifies, cools or brightens; the 使 (guide) carries the mixture and shapes how it arrives. The terms come from classical pharmacy and were adapted to 合香 (blended incense) as a grammar of composition.

Read through that grammar, mint's role is clear. It is too volatile and too distinctive to be a chief in any mixture where it appears at a normal dose — a chief ingredient needs the weight and persistence to define the dry-down. It works as a 佐 when a formula needs cooling or lift against a heavy warm base, and as a 使 when it is used in a small amount to shape the first impression.

This is an editorial reading of the framework, not a classical prescription. The classical texts do not hand down a mint formula with mint labelled 佐, and anyone who claims otherwise is inventing authority. What the framework offers is a vocabulary for a decision a blender has to make anyway: is this ingredient defining the mixture, supporting it, or merely directing its opening?

A buyer can use the same grammar on a product description. "Mint-forward" is a claim that mint occupies the 君 seat, which is a legitimate but narrow style: the whole formula has to be built around the menthol rather than beside it. When mint sits far down an ingredient list, the more likely reading is an assistant role, which is the commoner and more flexible pattern.

## Four trade forms and their release curves

> **Direct answer:** Four versions of the plant circulate: fresh leaf, dried leaf with its flowering top, milled powder, and distilled oil. They differ in how they give up aroma under heat — a question of delivery, not of rank. Form belongs on the specification, next to species.

| Form | Release behaviour | Made by | Where it earns its place |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh leaf | Bright and watery, gone in minutes | Cutting and using quickly | Kitchen, garnish, fresh infusion |
| Dried leaf and top | Gradual, held in plant tissue | Air drying or gentle heat | Tea, decoction, milling for incense |
| Powder | Burst at first contact, then flat | Milling dried leaf to a mesh | Sticks, cones, loose blends |
| Distilled oil | Fast and concentrated | Steam distillation | Fragrance work needing precision |

Leaf and oil are not stronger and weaker versions of one another. In leaf, the aromatic compounds sit inside cells and heat must liberate them, which slows the release. In oil they are already free, so they leave the moment the temperature rises and can sink into binder during curing. Swap one for the other at the same nominal percentage and the burn changes shape.

Powder sits between the two, and it changes the physics of a stick as well as the smell. Grinding exposes fresh surface, so the first contact with heat gives a burst; the rest tails off. A fine, consistent mesh takes up binder evenly and dries without splitting, while a mixed mesh produces soft spots and cracks. The behaviour of herbal powders in a mix is treated in [powder and resin behaviour](/blog/incense-powder-resin/), and the forming stage belongs to the wider [making process](/blog/how-incense-is-made/).

## Reading a lot without a grade

> **Direct answer:** No official grade exists for 薄荷, so assessment falls on the buyer. The order of importance runs freshness, species, leaf-to-stem ratio, cleanliness, moisture, storage history.

A missing standard does not mean missing criteria; it means nobody has codified them. What follows is an editorial checklist, not an industry norm.

- **Smell out of the sack.** A fresh lot releases a sharp, cold cloud that fills the air for a moment. Hay and dust mean age, heat damage, or leaf that has already been distilled.
- **Colour of the leaf.** Grey-green to green and fairly even. Brown patches point to oxidation, sun damage or a long spell in storage.
- **How much stem.** Leaf and flowering top should dominate. A stemmy lot is filler with a smaller aromatic payload.
- **Foreign matter.** Soil, twine, other species: any of it means the screening step was skipped, and cross-contamination is possible.
- **Water content.** It should crumble, not clump. Damp stock invites mould and suggests the drying was rushed.
- **Mesh consistency, for powder.** A single grade burns evenly; a mixed one cakes and splits.
- **What the label claims.** A full binomial can be checked; the bare word "mint" cannot.

Freshness ranks first because it decays fastest. The cold note leaks out of the leaf from the day it is cut, and warmth, damp and moving air all accelerate the loss. Cool, dark storage buys a season; a bad warehouse costs the top note in weeks.

Species ranks second because no amount of looking settles it. As dried cut leaf, spearmint and 薄荷 can pass for each other. Only the nose separates them, and only for someone who knows both references. Without those references, ask for the binomial, and for a larger order ask for a sample drawn from the lot itself.

## Substitution: what changes when you swap mints

> **Direct answer:** Move from 薄荷 to spearmint and the mix loses its cold bite; move to peppermint and the top sharpens toward medicine. A swap reshapes the burn curve, and the note is only part of what changes.

Swaps happen: harvests differ, stock runs out, a cheaper stock becomes attractive. The useful question is not whether a swap is allowed but what it does to the composition, so the change can be corrected before the batch is signed off.

| If you replace | You gain | You give up | What to do about it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 薄荷 with 留兰香 spearmint | A softer, sweeter, more food-friendly top | The cold shock and the sharp edge | Nudge the dose up; add a green material for lift |
| 薄荷 with 胡椒薄荷 peppermint | More cold, more projection | The rounded sweet-green middle | Cut the dose; guard against a medicinal read |
| 薄荷 with 紫苏 perilla | A herbal-green colour, less chill | All of the menthol freshness | Rethink the formula; this is a different note |
| Leaf with oil | Convenience, strength, repeatability | The slow release that leaf tissue gives | Lower the dose and add a fixative |
| Oil with leaf | A gradual, textured release | Precision and concentration | Raise the dose and expect lot variation |

The perilla line is a warning, not a recommendation. 紫苏 (*Perilla frutescens*) is a Labiate with a green, mildly spicy smell, and that family connection is enough for some sellers to market it as a mint stand-in. It is not a stand-in in any sense a formulator would recognise: the two share a taxonomic rank, not a scent. Family membership is weak evidence of interchangeability, and treating it as strong evidence is a recurring sourcing mistake.

The general method for deciding whether two stocks can replace one another sits in the [substitution screening method](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/). Ask about function first, scent second, price last.

## Two failure modes in the mint trade

> **Direct answer:** Spiking dried mint with synthetic menthol is uncommon; mislabelling the species is not, and spent leaf from the still sometimes finds its way back into the dried trade. One set of questions — species, provenance — covers both.

The naming trap is structural. English collapses three plants into one word; Chinese keeps them apart. A translation layer between the two can lose the distinction entirely, and a product description written in English for an international marketplace has no obligation to name the species. The result is a market where "mint" is a category, not an identity.

The exhausted-herb problem is less discussed and more interesting. Steam distillation is a business that produces two outputs: oil, which is valuable, and spent biomass, which is a disposal cost. Dried spent mint still has colour, still has some scent, and can be sold into a lower-tier market as if it were primary leaf. The defence is to buy from a supplier who can describe the relationship between the leaf and the oil operation — or who does not run one.

Synthetic menthol is a different case. Adding it to dried leaf is possible but unusual, because the economics favour selling the menthol as oil. The more common risk is a blend: lower-grade leaf sprayed with a menthol solution to restore the opening note. That produces a lot that smells strong at first and fades abnormally fast, which is a pattern worth watching for in a sample evaluation.

In practice, a label that names the species, the harvest year and the form does the work a grading standard would otherwise do. Where those three are missing, the buyer is relying on the seller's reputation alone.

## A comparison protocol for two lots

> **Direct answer:** Compare two mint lots in the same format, at the same dose, on the same day, and score them on opening strength, mid-burn character and residue. A side-by-side test done this way takes an hour and settles questions that a bag-sniff cannot.

Most buying decisions come down to a choice between two samples, and most sample evaluations are done badly: one lot sniffed on Monday, the other on Friday, in different rooms, at different doses. The method below removes the obvious biases without pretending to be a laboratory assay.

1. **Prepare identical test units.** Same binder, same powder mesh, same stick weight. If the stock is loose powder, weigh equal charges rather than eyeballing a spoonful.
2. **Code the samples.** Ask someone else to label them A and B. Knowing which is which changes what you notice.
3. **Burn in the same room, one after the other, with a gap.** Ventilate between burns, and give the nose ten minutes to reset.
4. **Score three moments.** The first thirty seconds, the middle of the burn, and the last third. Mint's weakness usually shows at the end, where a fresh lot still carries a green trace and a stale one is flat.
5. **Record the residue.** Colour of the ash, any cracking of the stick, and whether the charge drew evenly.

The scoring sheet can be simple. What matters is that the same questions are asked of both lots.

| Criterion | What to record | Weight in the decision |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Intensity and clarity of the cold note | High for top-note use |
| Mid-burn | Whether the note holds or collapses | Medium |
| Finish | Green trace versus flat hay | High for freshness |
| Ash and draw | Even burn, no cracking | Medium |
| After-smell | Clean or scorched | Low but diagnostic |

A result that reverses the supplier's story is worth taking seriously. If lot B was sold as the fresher harvest but finishes flat while lot A holds a green trace, the harvest date on the invoice is not describing what is in the bag. That does not prove fraud; it may mean a storage problem between the field and the warehouse. It does mean the buyer should ask about storage before placing a larger order.

For a buyer, the protocol also protects against the opposite error — rejecting a good lot because the first sniff was sharp. Menthol hits hard in the first seconds, and a strong opening is not the same as a lasting one. Scoring the middle and the end is what separates a fresh lot from a loud one.

## Storage and the slow fade

> **Direct answer:** Dried mint declines because its aromatic compounds evaporate and oxidise. Cool, dark, dry and airtight storage slows the process; nothing stops it. Buy in quantities you will use within a season.

The decline is a chain rather than a single event:

- residual moisture and warmth mobilise volatile compounds → the compounds migrate to the leaf surface → air movement carries them away → oxygen reacts with what remains → the bright top note flattens into hay → the colour fades from green to brown.

Temperature is the biggest lever, because it drives the first step. A sealed jar in a cool cupboard outperforms a beautiful tin on a sunny shelf by a wide margin. Light matters next, then air. Moisture is the wild card: a lot that picks up humidity in storage can develop mould before it loses its scent, which is a safety issue rather than a quality one.

Freezing is sometimes recommended for long storage. It works for whole dried leaf if the leaf is sealed and allowed to come to room temperature before opening, so condensation does not form on the leaf. It works badly for powder, which clumps and can take up odours from the freezer. For a working stock, a cool dark cupboard and a small jar that gets refilled is more practical than a freezer full of half-used bags.

The storage question connects back to purchasing. A 25 kg sack is cheaper per kilo and a poor decision for a small workshop that will not use it within a year. The right unit is the amount that gets consumed while the stock is still good, which is a calculation about turnover rather than price.

## Kitchen use and the Bencao record

> **Direct answer:** In the kitchen 薄荷 goes into soups, teas and garnishes; in blended incense it supplies a cooling accent. The 本草綱目 (Bencao Gangmu) also lists it, in the 草部 (herb section) — an entry written in the traditional vocabulary of its period, not a modern clinical finding.

Cooking is the familiar use. Leaves go into soup or noodles at the end of the boil, where the leftover heat wilts them without driving off the scent. Dried leaf goes into teas and decoctions. The plant's role in the kitchen is closer to a finishing herb than to a base ingredient, which is consistent with its chemistry: the volatile note wants to be added late.

The incense role mirrors the kitchen one. 薄荷 turns up in blended formulas as a cooling accent rather than a structural ingredient, added in small proportion to a base of woods, resins or other leaves. Its work is to freshen the opening and cut the weight of warmer notes. The sources that mention it are consistent about the modesty of that role.

The 本草綱目 record is often quoted in product copy as though it settled the plant's effects. It did not. The text is a Renaissance-era compendium of materia medica, organised by category and informed by the humoral medicine of its time. Reading it as a clinical dossier is a category error. What can be said accurately is narrower: 薄荷 has a place in the old herbals, and a place in a book is a historical fact, not a trial result.

Nothing here endorses a therapeutic use, and no medical claim is made. What the page documents is a kitchen and fragrance record.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The hazards are smoke and concentrated menthol, not the leaf itself. Work in moving air, site the burner where children and animals cannot reach it, and do not read a strong chill as proof of a health effect.

Any burning incense releases smoke and fine particles, regardless of what it is made from. Ventilation, distance and duration are the variables that matter, and a pleasant scent is not an indication that the air is clean. The site's [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general precautions.

Menthol is intense on mucous membranes. A strong dose in a confined space can feel like it is burning even when nothing is burning, and the sensation can be unpleasant for people who are not expecting it. Small children and pets are more sensitive to both smoke and menthol, so keep burners out of reach and do not burn in a closed room where they sleep.

Do not ingest essential oil. Do not apply it undiluted to skin. Do not use mint preparations as a substitute for medical care. These are ordinary cautions, and they are worth stating because the culinary familiarity of the leaf encourages people to treat the oil as harmless.

## Summary

薄荷 runs by rhizome through wet ground from China into much of Asia, and databases file it under *M. canadensis*. The menthol load gives it a cold green freshness that speaks first and stops speaking first. Spearmint and peppermint are relatives, not synonyms, and one English word hides the split.

- **Identity:** one East Asian plant, two Latin labels, three plants in English use.
- **Sensation:** the chill is a nerve message from menthol, not a fall in temperature.
- **Function:** a top note, an assistant rather than a chief in 君臣佐使 terms.
- **Quality:** freshness ahead of species; no official grade exists.
- **Trade:** mislabelled species and spent herb are the hazards; provenance questions are the guard.

For a formulator, treat mint as a precision tool. A little alters the opening; too much alters the identity. Dose, format and base decide which of those happens — not the poetry on the label.

## FAQ

**Is 薄荷 the same as peppermint?** No. Peppermint is a hybrid, *Mentha × piperita*, with a hard camphor edge; the Chinese herb is *Mentha haplocalyx*, filed as *M. canadensis* in databases. Family and menthol are shared, which is exactly why the two get confused, but they are separate materials in a formula.

**Why does mint feel cold when it is not cold?** Menthol switches on the nerve endings that report cold. Those endings send the usual message to the brain, so the mouth or skin feels chilled while its actual temperature stays put. The impression weakens as the endings adapt, which is why the first minute of a burn is the strongest.

**How much mint should go into an incense blend?** No figure travels well between formats: a cone, a stick and a loose heap all release differently. Start at the least amount the nose can catch, then judge the finished burn rather than the raw powder. Menthol reads louder than its percentage, so a formula that looks modest on paper can still taste like toothpaste.

**Is mint incense safe for pets?** Smoke is the issue, not the leaf. Cats and dogs react more strongly than people to particles in the air and to concentrated menthol, so burn where they can walk away, and never in the room where they sleep. The site's [safety guide](/safety/) covers the basic precautions.

**Why do old texts call mint a "cooling herb"?** Old herbals grouped plants by the qualities a person could feel, and temperature was one of the categories — cool, warm or neutral. That was a convention of description rather than a measurement, so the phrase records an impression, not a clinical finding.

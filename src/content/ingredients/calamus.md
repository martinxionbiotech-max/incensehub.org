**Direct answer:** Calamus (菖蒲, chāngpú) is the dried rhizome of *Acorus calamus*, the wetland plant English speakers call sweet flag. It smells warm, spicy, earthy, and faintly sweet — a root note rather than a flower — and it is the one classical incense material whose regulatory story matters as much as its aroma, because the rhizome's oil can carry β-asarone.

## Key facts

| Attribute | Entry on the spec | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese name | 菖蒲 (chāngpú), or 水菖蒲 to specify the wetland species | 石菖蒲 is a different plant with different standing |
| Species | *Acorus calamus* L. | genus-level naming hides the distinction |
| Family | Acoraceae | not an arum, despite the spadix |
| Plant part | the jointed rhizome | leaf and flower serve identification, not the trade |
| Leaf | sword-shaped, to roughly 1.5 m | field recognition before lifting |
| Scent | warm spice, damp earth, sugary edge, bitter tail | the only quality signal available without a laboratory |
| β-asarone | volatile phenylpropanoid, level set by cytotype | it drives every regulatory and safety question |
| Legal status | food barred in the US since 1968; EU caps in food categories | decides what may be sold where, for eating |
| Festival role | hung with mugwort at 端午 | explains the culture, not the formula |
| Handling | no ingestion; ventilate the smoke | the practical boundary |

## The short version

- **A stem, not a root.** What gets dug, dried, and sold is the horizontal stem running under the mud; leaves and flowers play no part in the trade.
- **Two names, one plant — and one name, several plants.** English "sweet flag" and Chinese 菖蒲 both point here, while 石菖蒲 points at a narrow-leaved relative with separate standing.
- **The legal file is about food.** American prohibition and European caps govern what may be eaten; they say nothing about what may be burned, and nothing about whether burning is wise.
- **Chemistry follows cytotype.** Diploid, triploid, and tetraploid populations carry different asarone loads, so species identity alone will not predict a lot.
- **It works from underneath.** As a blending material it supplies weight, dryness, and a bitter seam, and it is almost never the note a fragrance is remembered for.

## A wetland plant with a root at the centre

**Direct answer:** *Acorus calamus* is a perennial herb of marshes, pond edges, and slow water, with sword-shaped leaves and a thick horizontal rhizome. The aromatic part is that rhizome — a stem that grows sideways under the mud, not a taproot and not a tuber.

The plant reads as a grass until you look closely. Leaves rise from the rhizome in a stiff fan, flat and blade-like, typically somewhere between half a metre and a metre and a half long and only a centimetre or so wide; the Flora of China treatment records the full span of that range. The flower is not a showy bloom but a spadix: a finger-like spike of tiny flowers, typical of the arum alliance, tucked beside a leaf-like spathe.

Taxonomy is where the plant gets interesting. *Acorus* was long parked in the arum family, Araceae, on the strength of that spadix. Molecular work moved it out, and it now sits in its own family, **Acoraceae**, in its own order at the base of the monocots. For a reader, the practical upshot is modest: the plant is not a grass, not an arum, and not a sedge, though it grows among all three.

The rhizome itself is the reason the plant is in the incense cabinet. It is horizontal, jointed, and fleshy, with a warm, spicy smell that strengthens when the fresh stem is cut and dried. That smell is a defence chemistry: aromatic compounds stored in the tissue, released when the plant is damaged.

Handling a dried rhizome teaches the trade lesson faster than reading about it. The joints record age and handling, and a broken end is where mould starts; the scent is present the moment the tissue is cut and strengthens as it dries. Leaves confirm the genus in the field but never reach a buyer, and the spadix explains the taxonomic history without touching the price. Smell is the one signal assessable without instruments — and the one a careless dryer can destroy.

## 菖蒲 is a word, not a species

**Direct answer:** In Chinese, 菖蒲 is used for *Acorus calamus*, while 石菖蒲 names a narrower-leaved *Acorus* that is the species in the pharmacopoeia. The two are related, both aromatic, and not interchangeable — which is exactly the kind of gap that a single English word hides.

The distinction deserves more attention than it usually gets, because it is a live confusion in the trade rather than a botanical footnote.

- **水菖蒲 (shuǐ chāngpú)** — "water calamus," *Acorus calamus*, the wetland plant with broad leaves and a thick rhizome. This is the material this page describes.
- **石菖蒲 (shí chāngpú)** — "stone calamus," a narrow-leaved *Acorus* (treated as *A. tatarinowii*, and by some taxonomists within *A. gramineus*) that grows on rocks and stream banks and is the species named in the Chinese pharmacopoeia.
- **菖蒲** unqualified — usually the festival and garden plant, *A. calamus*, though context decides.

The gap matters for two reasons. First, the chemistry is not the same: the β-asarone story attached to *A. calamus* does not transfer automatically to a narrow-leaved species, and a claim about one says nothing certain about the other. Second, the aroma differs — the water plant is heavier and more resinous in the rhizome, the stone plant finer and more delicate.

Japanese practice adds a third layer. There, 菖蒲 (shōbu) is *A. calamus*, and the plant is tied to the fifth day of the fifth month, when bundles go into the bath as 菖蒲湯. The same word, a different regulatory culture, and a different set of customs around the same species.

For anyone sourcing: ask for the species name in Latin, not the common name in any language. 菖蒲 on a label is a starting point for a conversation, not an answer.

## β-asarone: the molecule that shapes the category

**Direct answer:** β-asarone is one of the asarones, a small aromatic compound built on a benzene ring with three methoxy groups and a short hydrocarbon side chain. Its *cis* geometry is what distinguishes it from the *trans* isomer, α-asarone, and its concentration is what distinguishes one *Acorus* population from another.

Three properties of the molecule drive everything that follows.

**It evaporates.** The compound is light enough that warmth lifts it out of plant tissue. In a censer, that means it joins the plume instead of staying behind in the ash — a point that matters more than any headline about the compound itself.

**It travels with a sibling.** α-asarone usually appears alongside it in the oil, and the two are not the same substance. Toxicology has concentrated on the β form. A document that lists "asarone" as a class, without saying which isomer or how much, tells you that someone measured something and not what they measured.

**Its amount follows the plant's genetics.** *Acorus calamus* includes diploid, triploid, and tetraploid populations, and the aromatic chemistry tracks that division. Diploid material has a long-standing reputation for low or undetectable β-asarone; triploid and tetraploid plants are the ones usually described as rich in it. This is where the shorthand "diploid is safe" comes from, and the shorthand is where careful thinking tends to stop.

| Cytotype | Where it is often reported | β-asarone reputation | What it means for a buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diploid | parts of Asia | low or negligible in many reports | the form most often cited as preferred; still needs a specification |
| Triploid | much of Europe | reported high | the material behind most restriction stories |
| Tetraploid | parts of South and East Asia | reported high | common in some trade channels |

Two cautions sit under that table. First, "reported" is doing real work: cytotype is a genetic fact, while β-asarone level is an analytical result, and the second does not follow from the first with certainty. A diploid population can still vary, and a supplier's claim about ploidy is not a measurement. Second, the shorthand collapses a distribution into a binary. Low is not zero, and a low-asarone lot used at high dose is not the same exposure as a trace of a high-asarone one.

That is why the only defensible position for a serious buyer is a specification with an analytical number attached, from a laboratory, on a lot basis. Everything else is inference.

## What the restrictions actually cover

**Direct answer:** The United States prohibited calamus in food in 1968, and the European Union sets maximum β-asarone levels for specified food categories. Both are rules about ingestion. Neither is a statement about incense smoke, in either direction.

The American rule is the one most often quoted, and almost always in a shortened form. What the FDA did in 1968 was to remove calamus from the list of substances allowed in food. That is a narrow instrument: it did not outlaw the plant, forbid its cultivation, or reach into cosmetics, incense, or garden use. Sellers who call the material "banned" have compressed one food rule into a general verdict.

Europe took a different route. Rather than excluding the plant, European flavouring law puts a ceiling on β-asarone in named food categories. The logic is familiar from other botanicals with a toxicological flag: the material stays available, its characteristic constituent is bounded, and the operator carries the burden of showing the number.

| Market | Instrument | Covers | Silent on |
|---|---|---|---|
| US | food prohibition, 1968 | what may be eaten | incense, cosmetics, growing the plant |
| EU | ceilings on β-asarone in named food groups | food and flavourings | smoke, which no EU text harmonises |
| elsewhere | national, often absent | variable | absence is not a finding of safety |

The table's third row is the honest one. Many markets have no specific calamus rule at all, and the absence of a rule is not evidence of safety; it is evidence of an unexamined question. A seller exporting to several countries should expect the question to be asked at each border and should be able to answer it with documents rather than assurances.

What follows from this is a communications discipline. Do not describe calamus as "approved" because a food rule does not reach incense. Do not describe it as "banned" because a food rule exists. Say what the rule covers, and let the reader draw the conclusion.

## Why burning is not a loophole

**Direct answer:** β-asarone is volatile, so heating the rhizome moves it into the smoke rather than destroying it. The food restriction therefore does not become irrelevant at the incense burner — it simply stops being the right instrument for judging the exposure.

Follow the physical path. A stick burns at the tip; heat travels ahead of the coal; the rhizome powder in that heated zone releases its volatile fraction. β-asarone, being volatile, joins the smoke stream. Some of it may be altered by the flame, and some fraction of the smoke is particle-bound rather than gaseous, but the compound does not politely disappear because the route of exposure changed.

The chain: **heat reaches the rhizome → the volatile oil distils out → β-asarone enters the smoke → the smoker inhales it → the exposure is respiratory, not dietary.** Each arrow is a different physical step, and the conclusion is not "dangerous" or "safe" — it is "different route, different evidence required."

That is the boundary this page holds. A food limit cannot be converted into a smoke limit: the exposures differ in dose, duration, and combustion chemistry. The inhalation question is not settled by the ingestion literature, and no supplier, blog, or page like this one should pretend otherwise.

What a reader can do with that is modest but real. Treat calamus as a material to use knowingly: ventilate, keep the burn small, do not sit in a closed room with a coil, and do not treat the tradition as a safety study. Our [safety guide](/safety/) applies the same frame to every aromatic on the site.

## In the blend: the root that adds body

**Direct answer:** Calamus is a warm, dry, spicy root note that sits under the middle of a blend rather than on top of it. It adds weight and a bitter-herbal edge, and it is used in small amounts because the material can dominate a formula's lower register with very little.

The aroma has three layers. First, warmth: a spice impression closer to ginger and cardamom than to anything floral. Second, sweetness: a faint sugary undertone that keeps the spice from reading sharp. Third, earth: a damp, root-cellar smell that is either the material's character or its defect, depending on how clean the lot is.

That combination does a specific job in 合香. It gives the base something to sit on. A blend of sweet resins and soft woods can read as if it is floating — pleasant, weightless, without a floor. A small amount of calamus supplies the floor. It is the same structural role that orris or cyperus might play in a different formula, with a completely different scent.

There is a practical consequence that surprises people new to the material. Calamus is more noticeable in the room than in the first sniff of the stick. Its volatiles are heavier and slower than a top note's, so the warmth arrives after the opening and lingers once the sweets have gone — useful for extending a blend's tail, awkward in one that wants to end clean.

| Position in the formula | What calamus contributes | Risk if overused |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | very little — it is too slow | wasted material; the note never registers |
| Middle | warmth, body, a bitter-herbal seam | the blend turns heavy and "rooty" |
| Base | a floor for sweet resins; a longer tail | a cellar note that reads as staleness |
| Solo | a dry, austere, medicinal smell | few people want a room that smells of it alone |

## Pairing: what works beside it

**Direct answer:** Calamus pairs best with materials that are either sweet and heavy or dry and herbal, and worst with anything delicate. Its function is to add weight, so a neighbour that already has weight can take it; a neighbour that depends on lightness cannot.

The pairing logic follows from the note's position. Because calamus is slow and heavy, it works with resins that need grounding and with herbs that share its dry register. Angelica and cyperus are the obvious neighbours: both are rooty, both carry a bittersweet seam, and both sit low enough that calamus reinforces rather than competes. Mugwort belongs in the same company — dry, bitter-green, herbal — which is one reason the two herbs share a festival.

Sweet resins pull in the other direction. Benzoin and storax have a sugared weight that can float; a small amount of calamus gives them a floor. Push it and the blend turns muddy, which is a different fault from bitterness: the resins lose their shape rather than gaining depth.

Sandalwood is workable but not ideal. The cream and the earth can coexist, and some formulas want exactly that contrast, though the wood gives up some of its polish in the exchange.

Florals are where the material fails. A rose or an osmanthus accord depends on a bright, lifted top, and calamus smothers it before it opens. Citrus fails for a different reason: it is gone before calamus arrives. The two can share a stick, but they cannot be a pair.

One editorial note on tradition. 菖蒲 and 艾 (mugwort) are linked in festival practice, not in classical blending theory, and the pairing's logic is seasonal and symbolic as much as olfactory. Reading the festival pairing as a formula instruction is a category error — a pleasant one, since the two do smell well together, but an error all the same. See [mugwort](/ingredients/mugwort/) for the other half of that pair.

## Format matters: stick, cone, coil, loose powder

**Direct answer:** The same calamus behaves differently in a stick, a cone, a coil, and a censer of loose powder, because each format sets its own airflow and burn temperature. The root note is heavy, so it tolerates slow formats better than fast ones.

A stick burns along a narrow front with air on one side. A cone burns from the apex with air on all sides, so it runs hotter and faster, and the volatile fraction leaves sooner. A coil smoulders at a lower temperature for hours, which suits a heavy root note: the material has time to release without being pushed. Loose powder burns according to how the heap is built — dense at the centre, open at the edge — and rewards a practised hand.

The practical reading:

| Format | Burn character | What calamus does | What to expect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stick | steady, moderate heat | releases slowly, sits under the sweets | the warmth arrives mid-stick and stays |
| Cone | hot, fast, strong updraft | gives up its volatiles early and hard | a heavier, smokier opening; less nuance |
| Coil | slow, low temperature | unfolds gradually over a long burn | the best showcase for a clean root note |
| Loose powder | variable, hand-controlled | depends entirely on the heap | either delicate or smothered, rarely in between |

The editorial judgment is that calamus is a **slow-format material**. It rewards patience and punishes heat. A maker who blends it into a fast-burning cone and judges the material by that result will conclude it is muddy and crude, when the format is what made it so. The same lot in a coil reads warm, dry, and composed.

Format is therefore a fair question for a supplier, and a loose-powder sample says little about how the material behaves in a pressed stick. Powder shows the aroma; the format shows the architecture.

## Reading the paperwork

**Direct answer:** For calamus, three items carry the weight: species, β-asarone figure, and the method behind the figure. A fourth — cytotype — is useful context but is not a substitute for the measurement.

A specification that says "calamus rhizome, food grade" tells you almost nothing. The useful version names the species (*Acorus calamus*, not merely *Acorus* sp.), states the plant part (rhizome), gives a β-asarone result with units and a method, and identifies the lot. Moisture, ash, and foreign-matter figures are ordinary quality data and belong there too, but they do not answer the question that matters most.

| Field | Weight it carries | Usual gap |
|---|---|---|
| Species and part | separates water calamus from its relatives | genus-only naming |
| β-asarone result | the number a regulator will ask for | a qualitative "low" with no value |
| Analytical method | a number without a method is not verifiable | no method stated |
| Lot and date | ties the paper to the bag | batch codes that cannot be traced |
| Moisture | predicts storage stability | omitted on powder lots |

The test of a supplier is not whether a certificate exists but whether anyone there can explain it. A seller who knows which method produced the asarone figure, and its detection limit, has read what they are forwarding. One who passes along a PDF unread is a channel, not a source.

## Processing: lifting, drying, cutting, grinding

**Direct answer:** Calamus is lifted, cleaned, dried, and either kept whole, sliced, or ground. Drying is the step that decides whether the lot keeps its warm spice or develops a musty, cellar character that no later handling repairs.

The sequence is short and unforgiving.

**Lifting.** The rhizome is dug rather than pulled, usually in late season when the aromatic content is highest. Fresh rhizomes are wet, heavy, and fragile; bruises and broken ends are the first place mould will start.

**Cleaning and trimming.** Soil, fine roots, and damaged tissue come off. This is also where a lot's cleanliness is decided — grit that survives to the grinder ends up in the powder, and no downstream step removes it.

**Drying.** The critical stage. The rhizome has to lose moisture fast enough to outrun mould and slowly enough not to cook the volatiles. In practice that means thin layers, moving air, and shade rather than strong sun. A producer who piles wet rhizomes in a heap creates the exact conditions for the failure mode this material is famous for.

**Cutting and grinding.** Whole rhizomes and slices are stable and inspectable. Powder is convenient and lossy: grinding exposes fresh surface, friction adds heat, and the volatile fraction leaves. A powder that smells powerfully in the bag can smell thin in the stick, because the bag is where the aroma went.

The mechanism chain worth memorising: **wet rhizome stacked in a heap → moisture trapped, temperature rises → mould establishes → musty, cellar-smelling tissue → drying later cannot remove the note, because the damage is chemical, not superficial.** This is why a musty lot is not a bargain. It is a different material from the one the formula was written for.

A second chain covers the powder: **fine grinding → fresh surface plus friction heat → volatile oil lost to the room → flat powder → a stick that underperforms its formula on paper.** Both chains end in the same place: the material's value is volatile, in the literal sense, and every handling step either protects it or spends it. The wider process sits inside [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/), and the powder side specifically in [incense powder and resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## Quality: what a lot should pass

**Direct answer:** Calamus quality rests on species and cytotype documentation, cleanliness, dryness, and the liveliness of the warm-spicy aroma. Some of that can be judged at the bench; the parts that matter most legally require a laboratory.

Four things can be judged without instruments. Species identity, partly: the rhizome's shape and the leaf's width narrow the field, though a narrow-leaved *Acorus* can pass a casual look. Cleanliness, fully: grit, foreign root, insect damage, and mould at the joints are visible to anyone who breaks a piece open. Moisture, roughly: a rhizome that bends rather than snaps is carrying water that will cause trouble in a sealed bag. Aroma, decisively: a clean lot smells of warm spice and wet earth, while a spoiled one smells of cellar, hay, or nothing at all.

Three more need a laboratory. The β-asarone figure is a number, and no amount of sniffing produces it. Cytotype is a genetic fact, and a supplier's claim about ploidy stays a claim until someone tests the tissue. Species confirmation, in doubtful cases, needs a botanist or a sequence.

The asymmetry is the point. The bench checks tell you whether the material is sound; the laboratory checks tell you whether it is the material you are permitted to sell in a given market. A lot can pass every bench check and still fail the only test that matters at a border.

## Breaking a piece open

**Direct answer:** The cheapest quality test on calamus takes ten seconds and costs one rhizome: snap it, smell the broken face, look into the joints, and note whether the interior is bright or dull.

A sound piece breaks with a short, dry crack. The exposed surface is pale, slightly fibrous, and immediately fragrant — the warm spice note arrives before you have lifted it to your nose. The joints, where the stem segmented as it grew, should be clean inside; grit and dark specks collect there first, and so does the earliest mould.

A piece that bends instead of snapping is holding water. A piece whose interior looks dull, brown, or mottled has aged or been stored badly. A piece that smells of cellar, hay, or damp cardboard is already lost, whatever its outside looks like. Those three failures are visible and smellable, and none of them needs a laboratory.

The test does not replace analysis. It cannot give you the cytotype, the asarone figure, or the species on the invoice. It can tell you, in the time it takes to open a sack, whether the lot is worth sending for analysis at all.

## Identification: calamus among the aromatic roots

**Direct answer:** Calamus is one of several aromatic rhizomes that reach an incense bench, and the confusions are practical rather than exotic. Orris root is the most common mix-up in perfumery writing; 石菖蒲 is the most common in Chinese trade; cyperus and angelica are the neighbours in a 合香 cabinet.

Each has a different aroma, a different chemistry, and a different regulatory position. None is a drop-in substitute for another, and the differences are large enough to detect without a laboratory if you know what to look for.

| Material | Plant | Family | Aroma | Regulatory note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calamus / sweet flag | *Acorus calamus* | Acoraceae | warm, spicy, earthy, slightly bitter | food prohibited in the US; β-asarone limits in the EU |
| 石菖蒲 (stone calamus) | *Acorus tatarinowii* (or *A. gramineus* complex) | Acoraceae | finer, drier, less resinous | pharmacopoeial drug; distinct species |
| Orris root | *Iris* spp. | Iridaceae | violet-sweet and powdery | perfumery stock; unrelated chemistry |
| Cyperus (香附) | *Cyperus rotundus* | Cyperaceae | nutty, earthy, faintly bitter | a sedge tuber; different structure |
| Angelica | *Angelica* spp. | Apiaceae | musky, green-sweet, rooty | a different family and a different aroma profile |
| Ginger | *Zingiber officinale* | Zingiberaceae | hot, citrusy, pungent | culinary spice; sharp where calamus is dry |

The pattern in the table is that "aromatic root" is a trade category, not a botanical one. Four families appear in six rows, and the only thing they share is a smell that lives underground and survives drying. A buyer who reasons from the category rather than the species will eventually substitute across a regulatory boundary without noticing.

Orris earns its own paragraph. English perfumery copy has muddled the pair for a century, and the muddle survives because the two materials really do share a job description: underground stem, dried, ground, used to carry and hold other scents. Past that shared job, nothing lines up. Iris rhizomes are a slow field crop, lifted after years in the ground and prized for a powder that smells of violets and clean linen. Calamus comes out of marsh mud and smells of spice and wet earth. Swapping one for the other changes a formula; entering one on a customs line as the other changes a file.

## The European chapter

**Direct answer:** Calamus was a European household aromatic long before it was an Asian incense material in Western hands — strewn on floors, candied, and used to bitter drinks. The food prohibition of 1968 ended most of that, and the ending is part of why the plant now reads as exotic to English-speaking readers.

The trail runs through apothecary shops rather than temples. Dried rhizome was kept as a strewing herb, scattered where floors were bare and air was stale; the warm, spicy smell covered what needed covering. It went into confectionery and into bitter herbal drinks — the vermouth and bitters tradition, where a pungent root was an asset.

That history explains the plant's wide distribution. A herb valued in kitchens, stillrooms, and pharmacies travels with the people who use it, escapes cultivation, and naturalises along ditches and slow rivers. Much of the *Acorus* now growing across Europe and North America arrived that way rather than by botanical expedition.

The American food prohibition in 1968 cut the culinary thread. Calamus came off the permitted list, and a generation of formulations were reformulated or abandoned. In the decades since, the plant has drifted out of the English-language pantry and into the category of "herbal curiosities" — which is where most Western readers now meet it, and why the Chinese and Japanese material feels like a separate subject rather than a continuation.

It is not separate. The rhizome that hangs beside a door at 端午 once lay scattered on a European floor, and the aromatic logic is the same in both settings: warm, dry, persistent, cheap. What changed is the regulatory frame, not the plant.

## The Dragon Boat pairing

**Direct answer:** 菖蒲 is tied to the Dragon Boat Festival (端午节) on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, when bundles of calamus and mugwort are hung at doorways. The practice is cultural and traditional, and it is worth understanding as such rather than as a pharmacological instruction.

The image is specific: calamus leaves are long and sword-shaped, so the bundle is read as a blade — 菖蒲剑, "calamus sword" — hung point-up beside a bundle of mugwort. The pairing is a folk defence against the season's pests and ill humour, expressed in the vocabulary of a household custom rather than a prescription.

Three traditions cluster around the plant:

- **Hanging.** Bundles at the door, usually with mugwort, at the festival.
- **Wine.** 菖蒲酒, calamus-steeped wine, is a traditional festival drink in parts of China. Given the β-asarone question, this is a tradition to understand rather than to revive; the regulatory position on ingestion is clear.
- **Bathing.** In Japan, 菖蒲湯 — a bath with calamus leaves — is the same festival's counterpart, on the fifth day of the fifth month.

For an incense reader, the interesting part is how a plant's cultural role and its sensory role diverge. Calamus is hung for what it symbolises and for the smell it releases as it dries, not because anyone weighed its chemistry. The festival is a reminder that aromatic plants were household technology long before they were commodities — and that the household use was not a toxicity study.

We describe these practices as tradition. We do not present them as health measures, and we do not recommend ingesting calamus in any form.

## Where it comes from, and why lots differ

**Direct answer:** The rhizome is lifted from marsh margins, pond edges, and irrigation channels, usually by hand, and usually as one harvest among many in a working landscape. That origin explains the thing buyers notice first: two bags under the same name can smell like different materials.

Start with the ground. *Acorus* wants its feet wet — shallow water, saturated soil, the silty fringe where a stream slows. Such places are everywhere and nowhere: everywhere geographically, nowhere commercially, because nobody plants a marsh for its rhizomes. The crop appears where water already lingers, which means the harvest follows the map of wet ground rather than the map of demand.

Land use then sets the terms. Drainage schemes, flood works, housing, and runoff all act on the same fringe. A patch that yielded well ten years ago may now be a straightened ditch with a different water chemistry; the plants that persist there may be smaller, or replaced by something else. A buyer inheriting a supplier's "same origin" claim is inheriting a claim about a place that may have changed.

Labour sets the price floor. Digging is manual or near-manual work in mud, followed by washing and drying in small batches. Hours per kilo are high, and the work is seasonal. That is why good material is never cheap, and why the cheap material on offer is usually answering a different question.

Consistency is the last variable and the one that costs formulators most. Wild and semi-wild populations differ in age, cytotype, and growing conditions; smallholders dry in different ways. A fragrance house that builds a signature around one lot's particular warmth will find the next lot slightly elsewhere. The fix is either a supplier who blends to a specification or a formulator who can nudge the formula each season.

None of this makes the material scarce. It makes it **irregular** — a different problem with a different cost. Scarcity shows up on the invoice; irregularity shows up in re-formulation, in rejected batches, and in the slow drift of a product away from what it used to smell like.

## The forms it travels in

**Direct answer:** Whole rhizomes, cut slices, ground powder, and the occasional tincture. The choice is about handling and verification rather than about rank, though it decides how much of the aroma ever reaches a stick.

Whole pieces are the most legible. You can see the joints, break one open, smell the fresh interior, and judge whether the batch was dried with care. They keep well and travel badly — bulky, brittle, and slow to weigh into a blend.

Slices are the compromise most traders settle on. Cross-sections dry faster than whole pieces, weigh easily, and still show colour and structure. Their weakness is exposure: every cut face is a door for air, and a sack of slices left open in a warm room goes flat before its time.

Powder is what production actually uses, and it is where identity disappears. A ground sample offers smell and colour and nothing else; the shape that told you it was *Acorus* is gone. A musty rhizome and a sound one can look identical once milled. Buyers who care should either grind their own or buy from a supplier who documents the input lot.

Tinctures — rhizome steeped in alcohol — belong to small-scale perfumery rather than stick production. They disperse beautifully at low dose, and bring the solvent problem with them: the alcohol must leave before the stick is formed, and it carries aroma away with it.

The through-line is that each step away from the whole rhizome trades verifiability for convenience. A buyer is always choosing where on that line to stand.

## Swaps, dilutions, and how to catch them

**Direct answer:** The plant is cheap enough that nobody profits from faking it with inert material, but three swaps are common: a related *Acorus* standing in for water calamus, another aromatic root standing in for calamus, and a spoiled batch disappearing into powder.

The first swap is the taxonomic one. Narrow-leaved plants in the same genus come to market under the general Chinese name, and the difference may be invisible once sliced. The aroma is close; the regulatory position is not necessarily the same. Only a species name and, where it matters, a test settle it.

The second is the category swap. Powders are where this happens: a scenting or bulking root blended into a ground lot, exploiting the fact that a powder offers no silhouette. It is more familiar in the spice trade than in incense, which is exactly why incense buyers rarely look for it.

The third is the quiet one. Sound rhizomes mixed with musty or insect-damaged pieces, then milled so the evidence disappears. Nothing about the bag announces it; the smell does, if anyone smells the powder before signing.

What defeats all three is unglamorous. A Latin binomial on the paperwork. A lot code that ties the bag to a harvest. A specification with real numbers. A sample kept in a sealed jar, dated, against which the next delivery can be compared. None of it requires a laboratory; all of it requires a buyer who asks.

The failure mode I see most often is not fraud but drift — a supplier who substitutes quietly because the original source dried up, and a buyer who does not notice until the finished sticks smell unfamiliar. A retained sample turns that from a mystery into a conversation.

## Traditional use, kept at a distance

**Direct answer:** Calamus has a long record in Asian and European herbal traditions, including festival use, strewing, and bitter preparations. Those are historical facts about human practice, not evidence that the material treats anything.

Two chapters run in parallel. The European one, traced above, is a story of strewing, confectionery, and bitters that ended with a food rule. The Asian one runs through festival custom and materia medica, with 石菖蒲 as the pharmacopoeial species and 水菖蒲 as the wetland plant. The words used for the plant are precise inside their own frame, and that frame is analogy rather than experiment.

We treat all of it as tradition. Where a claim would need a trial to support, we say so, and we do not repeat the claim as if the trial existed. The one thing about calamus that is documented in the modern literature is the β-asarone concern — and that is toxicology, not therapy.

## Reading a rodent study

**Direct answer:** The phrase "carcinogenic in rodent studies" is a summary, not a verdict about a person sitting near a censer. It is worth knowing what such a study actually shows, because the gap between the finding and the everyday question is where most of the confusion lives.

Three features of the standard design matter. First, the animals are dosed high — often at levels far above any realistic dietary exposure, because the test is built to detect an effect, not to model a lifestyle. Second, the route is usually oral, through feed, which is a different pathway from inhaling smoke. Third, the endpoint is often a tumour type in a specific organ at a specific dose, and extrapolating from that to "this material causes cancer in humans" is a leap that regulators handle with margins and categories, not with slogans.

None of this makes the finding unimportant. It is exactly why β-asarone is bounded in food and why calamus came off the American permitted list. The toxicology did its job: it flagged a constituent, and the rules followed. What it did not do is answer the incense question, which would need inhalation data that the food file does not contain.

Both sides abuse that asymmetry. "The rodent study doesn't apply to smoke" is technically right and rhetorically convenient; "rodent carcinogen, therefore the smoke is dangerous" skips the same step in the other direction. The honest position sits between them: a real flag, a different route, an evidence gap, and a reason to keep the burn small and the room aired.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Calamus is not for ingestion, and its smoke belongs in a ventilated space like the smoke of any burning plant material. The β-asarone question is an additional reason for care, not a substitute for the ordinary precautions.

Four points, kept separate because they are separate:

1. **Eating.** The US bars calamus from food, and the EU bounds β-asarone in defined food categories. Festival preparations exist in the record; this page does not recommend them, and that is the firmest line here.
2. **Burning.** Plant smoke carries fine particles whatever the aromatic. Keep the burn small, open a window, hold the stick away from your face, and do not read a pleasant smell as proof of clean air. The [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case.
3. **Dust.** Dried rhizome and its powder irritate. Grind with a mask in moving air, and do not treat the powder as a kitchen ingredient.
4. **Keeping it.** Dry and sealed. Damp material moulds, and a mouldy lot cannot be rescued.

Nothing here is medical advice, and nothing here certifies an exposure as safe. The purpose is to describe a material, not to clear it.

## FAQ

**Is calamus safe?** Split the question by route. Eaten, the plant is closed to food use in America, and Europe bounds its asarone content in defined food categories, because the oil can carry a compound that produced tumours in rodent work. Burned, the general case applies — fine particles, dose, ventilation — with one material-specific twist: β-asarone evaporates rather than decomposing, so it can ride the smoke. That is not a finding that burning is harmless, and it is not a finding that burning equals eating. Neither this page nor a supplier can settle the inhalation question, and anyone who claims otherwise is overselling.

**Is calamus the same as sweet flag?** Yes — same plant, two names. *Acorus calamus* is the botanical name; "sweet flag" is the common English one, and 菖蒲 is the Chinese. The aromatic part is the rhizome, the jointed underground stem, which is dug, dried, and either sliced or ground. The plant itself is a wetland herb with sword-shaped leaves, easily taken for a grass at a distance.

**Is calamus the same as 石菖蒲?** No, and the difference is worth knowing. 水菖蒲 is *Acorus calamus*, the broad-leaved wetland plant described on this page. 石菖蒲 is a narrow-leaved *Acorus* — treated as *A. tatarinowii*, and by some taxonomists within *A. gramineus* — that grows on rocks and stream banks and is the species named in the Chinese pharmacopoeia. They are related, both aromatic, and not interchangeable in either a formula or a compliance file.

**Does burning destroy β-asarone?** No — not in any dependable way. The molecule is small and evaporates readily, so warmth lifts it out of the tissue and into the plume; a portion meets the flame and changes, a portion does not, and what reaches a person is a mixture nobody has characterised on this page. The takeaway is structural rather than numerical: a rule written for plates cannot be transplanted to a censer, because the pathway differs and so does the evidence that would be needed. A seller who says the flame "burns it off" has offered a slogan, not a measurement.

**Why does my calamus smell musty?** Most likely because mould got there first. A rhizome that dries slowly, or that sits damp in a sack, gives fungi a window; they alter the tissue and leave a cellar smell behind. Air the pieces for a week and the note will still be there, because it is not perfume sitting on the surface — the material itself has changed. Once that happens, the batch is what it is. Drying practice upstream decides the question, which is why it is fair to ask a supplier how the rhizome was handled after lifting.

**What does calamus smell like?** Warm, spicy, earthy, and faintly sweet, with a bitter edge that keeps it from reading like a dessert spice. It is a root note rather than a floral or a resin, closer to ginger and cardamom in its warmth than to anything sweet and powdery. In a blend it sits low, adds body, and outlasts the brighter notes.

## Key Takeaways

- **It is a rhizome, not a root spice.** The traded part is the jointed underground stem of a wetland monocot; the leaves are sword-shaped and the flower is a spadix.
- **One English name, several Chinese ones.** 水菖蒲 is this plant; 石菖蒲 is a narrow-leaved relative with its own pharmacopoeial standing. Latin binomials settle what common names blur.
- **The aroma sits low.** Warm spice, damp earth, a sugary middle, a bitter tail. It gives a blend a floor and takes the shine off anything delicate.
- **β-asarone is the governing fact.** A volatile phenylpropanoid, carcinogenic in rodent work, its level tied to cytotype rather than to the label.
- **Food rules are not smoke rules.** The US prohibition and the EU caps address eating. Heating pushes the compound into vapour; it does not remove it, and no one should pretend the file transfers.
- **Buy the paperwork, not the story.** Species, plant part, asarone figure with method, lot code, retained sample. That set is what survives a bad season or a new supplier.

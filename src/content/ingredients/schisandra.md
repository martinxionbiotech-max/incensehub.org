> **Direct answer:** 五味子 (wǔwèizǐ) is the dried ripe fruit of *Schisandra chinensis*, a woody climber in Schisandraceae that grows through northeastern China, Korea, and the Russian Far East. The name means five-flavour berry, and it describes the mouth rather than the nose. That single fact explains the rest: a fruit celebrated for centuries in pharmacy, and almost silent in a burner.

## The vine, the fruit, and the drug

> **Direct answer:** The plant is a deciduous woody climber, not a bush. It twines up trees and trellises, flowers in small clusters, and hangs its fruit in spikes close to the stem. The drug is the ripe berry, picked red, dried whole, and traded with the seeds still inside.

Growth habit matters more than it looks. A vine invests in stems and support before it invests in fruit, so the crop arrives on last year's wood and the harvest is spread along the plant rather than gathered at the tips of branches. The berries hang in a spike, which means they ripen unevenly along that spike and dry unevenly afterwards.

Each berry is small and round to slightly flattened, with a thin skin over a fleshy layer and a few kidney-shaped seeds. The structure governs the drying: flesh holds water, skin resists giving it up, seeds sit protected in the middle. Fruit dried too hard wrinkles and case-hardens; fruit dried too slowly in humid air grows mould before it is dry.

*Schisandra* is a genus with several Chinese members, and *S. chinensis* is the one the classical name denotes. Other species are collected and sold under their own names, which is the source of a confusion treated later on this page. The binomial is the only reliable anchor.

The native range runs through northeastern China, the Korean peninsula, and the Russian Far East, and Chinese material has historically come from the north and northeast. That geography explains why the old texts speak in compass terms, and why the modern species split borrowed the same words.

## A name built on taste

> **Direct answer:** 五味子 breaks into 五味 (five flavours) and 子 (fruit or seed). The label records what happens in the mouth: sour, sweet, salty, bitter, pungent, all reported in one fruit. Naming a material for taste is unusual in a fragrance tradition that names things for smell, colour, or place.

The classical account is specific about where each sensation sits. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 草之七, preserves Su Gong's (苏恭) explanation:

> 五味，皮、肉甘、酸，核中辛、苦，都有鹹味，此則五味具也。

Skin and flesh carry sweet and sour; the seed carries pungent and bitter; the whole fruit adds a salty note, and with that the set is complete. The same passage notes that the older *Shennong Bencao Jing* listed only sour, and explains the difference through the five phases, with wood placed first. That older text also ranks the drug among its top-grade (上品) materials.

| Taste | Where the text locates it | An editorial, modern reading |
|---|---|---|
| Sour | Skin and flesh | Organic acids in the flesh |
| Sweet | Skin and flesh | Sugars in the flesh |
| Bitter | Seed | Lignans and related seed compounds |
| Pungent | Seed | Volatile constituents |
| Salty | The whole fruit | Least chemically specific; probably minerals and mouthfeel |

The final row is the one that repays attention. Four tastes map onto something a chemist can name; saltiness does not have a single obvious carrier in the fruit, and the honest reading is that the phrase describes a sensation rather than a compound. That does not make the classical account wrong. It makes it a description of eating rather than a composition table.

For anyone arriving through incense, the consequence is direct. A taste name selects for a sense that smoke never reaches. The berry can be vivid on the tongue and nearly absent in the air, and both statements are true at once.

## Why the nose barely registers it

> **Direct answer:** The fruit is built for eating: sugars, acids, and heavy lignans dominate, and the volatile oil that could travel in smoke is a small fraction. Smoke carries volatiles. A material rich in taste and poor in volatiles cannot make a loud note, no matter how famous it is.

The mechanism is not mysterious. A ripening berry loads its flesh with sugars and organic acids; those molecules are heavy and non-volatile, and they stay put unless heat breaks them apart. The aromatic fraction — the part light enough to rise with hot gas — is small beside what a resin or a citrus peel carries.

Follow the chain: little volatile oil → little material available to vaporise → a thin signal in the smoke → the berry reads as background even at a substantial dose. Nothing is wrong with the fruit. It is simply not built for the nose.

| Fraction | Rough character | Behaviour in heat |
|---|---|---|
| Sugars | Sweet, water-soluble | Caramelise and char; burnt-sugar notes |
| Organic acids | Sour, water-soluble | Mostly remain; some flash off as sharp notes |
| Lignans | Bitter, non-volatile | Stay in the residue |
| Volatile oil | Aromatic, small share | Travels in smoke; the only real incense signal |

That is why more berry does not fix a weak berry. Past a point the sugars take over the burn and the smoke turns to burnt caramel, which reads as a fault rather than as extra fruit. The useful window is narrow and sits low.

Drying decides how much of that window survives. Slow shade drying, which the old texts specify, keeps more of the volatile fraction than fast heat, because fast heat drives aromatics off along with water. A well-dried berry smells of dried fruit and tea. A badly dried one smells of very little, or of the inside of a cupboard.

## What it does in a blend

> **Direct answer:** It is an accent: a tart-sweet, dried-fruit note that softens a sharp edge and adds a faint tea-like warmth. It is not a base, not a fixative, and not a stand-in for a real fruity material such as citrus peel or a floral absolute.

Its best work happens in small amounts, where the acidity lifts a flat heart and the fruit takes the sting out of a hard spice. It sits comfortably over resins without fighting them, provided the dose stays low, and it pairs naturally with flowers and sweet woods.

| Situation | Contribution | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Floral heart | A tart-sweet lift | Too much reads as dried fruit, not flower |
| Sweet spice blend | Softens the sharpness | Can mute the spice's top |
| Resinous base | A faint fruity top | Barely detectable; often wasted |
| Woody core | Very little | The wood dominates |
| Medicinal-style blend | Reinforces a tonic character | Turns muddy |

The practical judgment is that the berry earns its place when a blend needs a soft fruit note and nothing louder is on the shelf. A formulator with better fruity materials will usually reach for them. A formulator working inside a classical palette may use the berry for historical resonance as much as for scent, and that is a different reason with its own logic.

That is an editorial position, and it should be labelled one. Prestige in pharmacy does not transfer to the burner. A material can matter enormously in one domain and modestly in another, and pretending otherwise leads to over-dosing a note that cannot carry the weight.

## The classical layers, read separately

> **Direct answer:** The documentary trail runs from an origin note in the 別錄, through Tao Hongjing's ranking of sources and Su Song's description of the plant, to Li Shizhen's colour grading. Each layer answers a different question: where it grew, which supply was preferred, what the plant looked like, how the trade sorted it.

Reading the layers apart is more useful than folding them into one phrase about traditional use.

**The origin note.** The 別錄 places the drug in the Qishan valleys and in Daijun, says the fruit is taken in the eighth month, and specifies drying in shade. That is agricultural geography: a district, a season, a method.

**The ranking of sources.** Tao Hongjing (陶弘景) puts Goryeo (高麗) first — fleshy and sweet-sour — with Qingzhou and Jizhou behind it, where the fruit runs too sour. This is a trade judgment about which supply was worth buying, and it shows a graded commodity rather than a wild harvest.

**The plant description.** Su Song (苏颂) reports it as plentiful in Hedong and Shaanxi and present around Hangzhou and Yue; fruit in the seventh month, clustered at the stem ends, about pea-sized, green when unripe and red-purple at maturity, used raw and sun-dried with seeds left in. That is a recognisable portrait of the vine.

**The colour grade.** Li Shizhen (李时珍) records that southern fruit is red and northern fruit is black, and that tonic preparations preferred the northern. This is sorting by appearance inside the trade.

| Layer | What it records | What it is evidence of |
|---|---|---|
| 別錄 origin note | District, harvest month, shade drying | Early collection and drying practice |
| Tao Hongjing | Ranking of producing regions | A graded trade in the fruit |
| Su Song | Plant and fruit description | Botanical identification |
| Li Shizhen | North–south colour grading | Commercial sorting, not species |
| Wang Ji | Different uses for north and south | Traditional clinical reasoning |

Wang Ji (汪机) adds that the two forms were assigned to different uses, with the northern preferred for one pattern and the southern for another. That belongs to the clinical tradition. This page reports it as part of the documentary record and draws no medical conclusion from it.

## Two north–south stories

> **Direct answer:** The classical note is about colour within one drug: southern berries red, northern berries black. Modern Chinese usage uses the same two words for a species split: 北五味子 is *Schisandra chinensis*, 南五味子 is *Schisandra sphenanthera*. Two different distinctions share one vocabulary, and merging them is the commonest error in the literature.

This is the most useful clarification on the page, because the collision happens in translation. A reader who meets "northern schisandra" in a modern text may be looking at a species name. A reader who meets "northern berries are black" in a classical text is looking at a colour grade.

| Usage | What north and south mean | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Classical (Li Shizhen) | Red southern fruit, black northern fruit | Appearance and trade form |
| Modern pharmacopoeial | 北五味子 = *S. chinensis*; 南五味子 = *S. sphenanthera* | Species identity |
| Modern commercial | Regional origin claims | Geography and supply |

The classical text never names *S. sphenanthera*, and the modern species split does not rest on colour. Treating the colour note as a species statement is retrofitting: it reads a modern category back into an older text and then claims the text supports it.

What follows for a buyer is a simple rule. Ask for the binomial, and treat "north" and "south" as ambiguous until it is given. A supplier who says "northern berries" has made a colour-and-origin claim, not an identity claim.

There is a second consequence for readers of the old literature. Praise for northern material belongs to a colour-graded trade; the modern preference for *S. chinensis* belongs to a species-graded market. The two often point the same way, which is why the confusion survives, but they are not the same claim and a careful text keeps them apart.

## Five as a schema

> **Direct answer:** The five flavours work as a classical organising scheme as much as a sensory report. Five is the number of the five phases, and the account arranges the fruit's tastes so that the set comes out complete. Reading the name as a laboratory result claims more than the text does.

This is an editorial reading and should be marked as one. The passage is precise about location — sweet and sour in the flesh, bitter and pungent in the seed, salty in the whole — but completeness is also rhetorically satisfying in a way that five-phase cosmology rewards.

None of that makes the observation false. The flesh really is sour and sweet; the seed really is bitter and pungent. What the scheme adds is a claim of completeness: all five tastes meeting in one fruit, which is a statement about significance as much as about chemistry.

For an incense reader the point is simpler. A name built on taste tells you where the material's value sits, and it is not the burner. The berry's prestige belongs to the pharmacy and the tonic tradition; its aroma is a quiet fruit note that most blends can live without.

## What the Latin name describes

> **Direct answer:** *Schisandra* is botanical Greek for a split stamen, and the epithet *chinensis* means Chinese. The scientific name therefore describes a flower part and a country. The Chinese name describes a mouth. Neither name says anything about smell, which is why both can be accurate and still useless to a perfumer.

The genus name is usually explained as a compound of Greek roots for splitting and for the male part of a flower, a reference to the separated anthers that distinguish the genus. That is a taxonomic observation, made with a lens. It has nothing to do with the fruit's aroma, its taste, or its trade.

Set the two naming systems side by side and the pattern is clear. One names the plant by flower anatomy, the other names the drug by sensory experience in the mouth. A third naming system — the trade's — names it by colour and origin, which is where 北 and 南 enter. Three systems, three questions, and only one of them is about scent.

| Naming system | What it encodes | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical Latin | Flower structure, then geography | Which plant is this? |
| Chinese drug name | Taste, five flavours | What is it like to consume? |
| Trade language | Colour, district, grade | Which consignment is this? |

This is why a spec sheet needs more than one field. A binomial answers the botanist's question. A drug name answers the trader's. Neither answers the burner's, which is why the sensory checks on this page exist at all.

## Handling: harvest, drying, preparation

> **Direct answer:** Pick ripe, dry whole, keep the seeds in. The classical tradition adds splitting, honey-steaming, and a rice-water soak, but those belong to internal medicine. For incense the plain dried fruit is the material.

The old record is unusually precise about handling. The 別錄 gives an eighth-month harvest and shade drying. Su Song notes the fruit used raw and sun-dried, seeds retained. Lei Xiao (雷斅) describes splitting the berry with a copper knife, steaming it steeped in honey, then steeping it overnight in rice water before drying. Li Shizhen adds that it was cooked for tonic use and used raw otherwise.

| Step | Classical practice | What it means for incense |
|---|---|---|
| Harvest | Ripe fruit, eighth month in the old calendar | Ripe fruit carries the most sugar and colour |
| Drying | Shade first; sun drying in later practice | Slow drying keeps more volatiles |
| Seed handling | Seeds left in | The seed holds the bitter lignans |
| Preparation | Splitting, honey-steaming, rice-water soak | Pharmacy processing; changes the nose |

For incense, the plain dried berry is the starting material. The honey-steamed form is darker, sweeter, and cooked-fruit in character; it is a legitimate aromatic variant when a source states it, but it is a preparation rather than the base drug, and a spec sheet should say which one is in the bag.

Grinding exposes the fruit's limits. A fine powder releases what little volatile it holds in a rush, so the note appears and vanishes. A coarse grind stretches a thin signal over a longer burn. Neither is satisfying in quantity, which is why the berry usually appears as a minor component rather than a featured one.

## Forms and storage

> **Direct answer:** Whole dried fruit is the reference form; powder is a convenience with a short life; honey-steamed fruit is a separate material with a different nose. Storage is mostly a fight against moisture, with light and heat as junior partners.

| Form | Use | Storage behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Whole dried berry | Reference form, weighing, blending | Holds shape and aroma longest |
| Powder | Blending, dosing | Loses its faint aroma quickly; cakes in humid air |
| Honey-steamed berry | Traditional preparation | Darker and sweeter; shorter shelf life |

Dried fruit is hygroscopic. A berry that picks up water softens, darkens, and becomes a surface for mould. Airtight, cool, dry, dark covers the whole problem, and the container matters less than the discipline of sealing it.

Insect pressure is real with sweet dried fruit. Webbing, fine dust, and small holes are the signs, and an infested lot should be rejected rather than salvaged. Freezing stops an active infestation; it does not restore the fruit.

The faint nose makes decline easy to miss. An old sample can still look acceptable and still smell of dried fruit while the fresh brightness has gone. Dates on labels and a retained sample are the only reliable way to track that slide.

## Reading a lot: checks and three profiles

> **Direct answer:** Judge species, ripeness, dryness, cleanliness, and smell, in that order of confidence. Good material is plump, deep red to purple-black, dry without being brittle, clean, and quietly fruity. Poor material is pale, shrivelled, musty, or full of stems and sand.

| Check | Good sign | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Species | A stated binomial, *S. chinensis* for 五味子 | Unnamed *Schisandra* material |
| Colour | Deep red to purple-black, even | Pale or bleached fruit |
| Plumpness | Full, skin wrinkled rather than collapsed | Shrivelled, hollow berries |
| Dryness | Dry to the touch, no clumping | Soft, sticky, or damp |
| Cleanliness | No stems, sand, or foreign berries | Field debris, grit |
| Smell | Faint fruit and tea, clean | Musty, sour, or flat |

Colour needs a caveat, and the classical grading is the reason. Li Shizhen's note treats black and red as two trade forms; a modern buyer should not read dark fruit as automatically better. Colour reflects ripeness and drying as much as origin, and a very dark, dull berry can be old rather than ripe.

Plumpness is the best single indicator of harvest timing. Fruit picked before ripeness is thin and pale; fruit dried too hard is brittle and dusty. The target is a berry that feels solid and slightly yielding, with a skin that has wrinkled rather than collapsed.

Smell is the weakest check, which is why it comes last. The aroma is faint by nature, so a quiet sample is not automatically a bad one. What the nose detects reliably is fault: mustiness, sourness, or a papery staleness that no amount of blending will cover.

Three profiles cover most of what arrives.

**Lot A — pale and shrunken.** Small, thin, light red to pink. Crushed, it smells of hay and little else. This is fruit picked early and dried hard: sugars never developed, volatiles are minimal. It is not dangerous, it is just useless. Reject it for any job where the berry is meant to contribute, and do not accept a discount as compensation.

**Lot B — dark and sticky.** Deep in colour, slightly clumped, and the sack smells sweet-sour with a cellar edge. This is moisture damage: water got in, sugars migrated to the surface, mould has started somewhere in the mass. The look is deceptively good, because dark reads as ripe. Reject it; the note cannot be blended out.

**Lot C — plump, deep red, clean.** Full berries, skin wrinkled rather than collapsed, even colour, a quiet dried-fruit tea in the sack. This is the working standard. It will still be faint in the burner, but it behaves predictably and keeps well if held dry.

| Profile | Look | Smell | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Underripe, over-dried | Pale, thin, shrunken | Hay, dust, faint | Reject for aroma use |
| Damp, damaged | Dark, sticky, clumped | Sweet-sour, cellar | Reject entirely |
| Ripe, sound | Plump, even red-purple | Dried fruit, tea, clean | Accept; store dry |

The lesson is that colour misleads in both directions. Pale can mean unripe; dark can mean damaged. The reliable combination is plumpness plus a clean, quiet smell, judged at the sack rather than at a sample dish.
## When a blend wants fruit

> **Direct answer:** The berry is rarely the best tool for a fruity note. Brightness points to citrus peel, floral sweetness to a flower, plain sweetness to licorice. Schisandra earns its place when the note should be dried-fruit and faint, not when it should be loud.

| The job | Better tool | Why the berry is second choice |
|---|---|---|
| Bright fruity top | Citrus peel | Higher volatile load, clearer lift |
| Floral sweetness | Peony or another flower | The fruit reads dried, not floral |
| Sweetness without fruit | Licorice | Stronger, cleaner sweetness |
| Warm aromatic fruit | Amomum villosum | More volatile, more assertive |
| A tart accent under a floral heart | Schisandra | The one job it actually does |

The logic is about register rather than strength. A formulator who reaches for the berry to brighten a blend will be disappointed and will over-dose trying to compensate. A formulator who uses it to take the edge off a sharp heart finds it works at a dose so small it is almost invisible.

There is a historical reason the fruit persists in formulas despite this. Inside a classical palette the available fruit materials were few, and a dried berry carrying five tastes had symbolic weight that a citrus peel did not. That symbolism is part of the material's identity. A blend that uses it for resonance rather than for aroma is making a defensible choice, provided the choice is stated and the dose is honest.

## A test worth running on a faint material

> **Direct answer:** Faint materials need a different test from loud ones. You cannot smell a small dose in the jar and learn much; you have to burn three doses against a blank and find the point where the fruit disappears and the char begins.

The method is deliberately simple.

1. **Build a blank.** Base and binder only, formed and dried the same way as the samples.
2. **Build three samples.** The same base with the berry at a low, middle, and high dose. One variable, three points.
3. **Burn them apart, on separate days or in separate rooms.** A faint note is easily masked by the previous stick.
4. **Look for two thresholds.** The dose where the fruit first becomes perceptible, and the dose where burnt sugar takes over. The useful range sits between them.
5. **Write down what disappeared first.** If the fruit vanishes after the opening, the dose is too low for the format, or the format is wrong for the material.

The test answers a narrow question — where the window is — and that is exactly the question this fruit raises. It cannot tell you whether the lot is *S. chinensis*, whether it was dried slowly, or whether the supplier's paperwork is accurate. Those require documents and a reference sample.

One caution belongs with the method. A faint material invites the nose to work harder, and a nose searching for a note will find one. Testing against a blank is the discipline that keeps the search from inventing its result.

## Taste, flavour, aroma: three words, three jobs

> **Direct answer:** Taste happens on the tongue, aroma happens in the nose, and flavour is what the brain builds from both. The classical five-taste scheme is a statement about the first. Incense only has access to the second. Confusing the three is the root of most bad claims about this fruit.

The distinction is not academic. When the berry is eaten, volatile molecules travel from the mouth up into the nasal cavity, so aroma contributes to what a person calls flavour. That is why a "five-flavour" description can be both a taste report and, indirectly, a smell report — the two are stitched together in perception.

Smoke removes the mouth from the equation. What reaches a reader is airborne only, and the heavy, water-soluble fractions that dominate the fruit's taste stay in the ash. A material can therefore be a five-flavour experience at the table and a one-note whisper in the air, without contradiction.

| Question | Sense involved | What the berry delivers |
|---|---|---|
| What does it taste like? | Tongue and mouth | Sour, sweet, bitter, pungent, a salty impression |
| What does it smell like? | Nose, from the jar | Dried fruit, tart, faint tea and wood |
| What does it do in smoke? | Nose, from combustion | A thin tart-sweet accent, if dosed well |
| What is "flavour"? | Both, combined | A construct, not a property of the fruit |

This is where marketing language usually slips. "Five-flavour" is a name, not a specification, and it says nothing about volatile yield. A spec sheet that repeats the name without a binomial has told you about a word, not about a material.

## Describing it honestly

> **Direct answer:** A label can state identity, part, origin, and sensory character. It cannot state a health benefit, a cure, or a fragrance strength the material does not have. The honest vocabulary is narrower than the marketing vocabulary, and the gap is where most bad copy lives.

Start with what is checkable. The plant, the part, the district, the year, the form, and a plain sensory description are all defensible, because each can be traced to a document or to a nose. "Dried ripe fruit of *Schisandra chinensis*, northern Chinese material, whole, tart and tea-like" says a great deal without claiming anything it cannot support.

Then look at the words that overreach. "Detox" is a promise with no definition. "Five-flavour" is a name, not a measurement, and using it as though it were a specification borrows authority the phrase does not carry. "Traditional use" is accurate only when the sentence also makes clear that tradition is the source, not proof.

| Claim | Why it fails | What to write instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Detoxifying" | No agreed meaning; implies an effect | Describe the part and the preparation |
| "Five-flavour formula" | A name presented as a spec | "Named for five tastes recorded in the *Bencao Gangmu*" |
| "Boosts" a body system | A health claim this page cannot support | Omit it entirely |
| "Strong fruity aroma" | Contradicted by the material | "A faint tart-sweet accent" |
| "Ancient remedy" | Blurs record and evidence | "Recorded in classical materia medica" |

The same discipline applies to photographs and alt text. A picture of glossy fresh berries sold as dried material is a small deception that compounds into a large one when the buyer opens the sack. Describe what ships.

## What the record supports

> **Direct answer:** The record supports identity, a documented classical literature, and a set of sensory observations. It does not support medical claims, and it does not turn the fruit into a leading incense material.

- **Identity.** *Schisandra chinensis*, Schisandraceae, a woody climber; the dried ripe fruit is the drug.
- **Literature.** The 別錄, Tao Hongjing, Su Song, Li Shizhen, and Wang Ji each contribute a documented layer.
- **Chemistry.** Lignans, organic acids, sugars, and a small volatile oil, with the lignans the most studied fraction.
- **Sensory character.** A faint, tart-sweet, tea-like fruit note that reads as an accent at most.
- **Medical status.** Traditional use is reported as tradition. No efficacy claim is made here.

The gap between "documented" and "demonstrated" carries most of the weight in that list. A classical text documents what was believed and traded; it does not demonstrate a clinical effect. A chemistry paper documents compounds; it does not demonstrate that burning them does anything beyond producing smoke.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The risks are the ordinary ones for botanical incense — smoke, particulates, ventilation — plus the handling care that dried fruit and fine powder require. The fruit's medicinal reputation changes none of that.

- **Smoke and particulates.** Burn in a ventilated space, keep sessions short, and do not read a sweet smell as evidence of clean air.
- **Sugar-rich material.** The fruit chars readily; a heavy dose produces acrid notes along with more visible smoke.
- **Dust.** Powdered fruit is fine and goes airborne easily. Decant gently and wear a mask when working in quantity.
- **Storage hazards.** Damp fruit supports mould. Discard a musty lot rather than blending it away.
- **Household exposure.** Children, pets, and anyone with respiratory sensitivity should not be in the room during a heavy burn.

The [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case. The material-specific addition is that its sugars make it smoke more freely than a dry wood or resin at the same weight, so a small trial burn is worth doing before a full batch.

## FAQ

**What is schisandra (五味子)?** The dried ripe fruit of *Schisandra chinensis*, a woody climber in Schisandraceae native to China, Korea, and the Russian Far East. The name refers to five tastes — sour, sweet, salty, bitter, pungent — and the aroma is a faint dried-fruit note.

**Why "five-flavour berry"?** The *Bencao Gangmu*, 草之七, preserves Su Gong's account: sweet and sour in the skin and flesh, pungent and bitter in the seed, a salty note in the whole. Five tastes meet in one fruit. The name describes the mouth, not the air.

**Is 北五味子 the same as the classical northern berry?** Not the same claim. The classical note is a colour grade — red southern, black northern. Modern 北五味子 names *S. chinensis*, while 南五味子 names *S. sphenanthera*. The words match; the meanings do not.

**Does it work as an incense material?** As a minor accent, yes. Its volatile fraction is small, so it reads as a faint tart-sweet note; pushed harder it produces burnt sugar rather than more fruit. It is not a leading 香材.

**How should the fruit be stored?** Whole, airtight, cool, dark, and dry. It is hygroscopic and sugar-rich, so moisture is the main enemy. Powder loses its faint aroma quickly and is best used soon after grinding.

**Why is the smell so much weaker than the taste?** Because the two draw on different fractions. Sugars, acids, and lignans dominate the taste and are heavy or non-volatile; the aromatic oil that could travel in smoke is a small part of the fruit.

## Summary

五味子 is the dried fruit of *Schisandra chinensis*, a woody vine of the Schisandraceae, and its name records five tastes meeting in one berry. That name is the key to its place in incense. The fruit is rich in the fractions that build flavour and poor in the fraction that builds smoke, so it reads as a faint tart-sweet accent and never as a leading material. The classical record adds an origin note, a ranking of sources, a plant description, and a north–south colour grade. Modern usage adds a separate species distinction between 北五味子 and 南五味子, which should not be folded into the older colour note.

## Evidence & Sources

### Botanical identity
**What the record shows:** the Flora of China entry for [*Schisandra chinensis*](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200008486) describes the species and its Chinese range; the [GBIF record](https://www.gbif.org/species/7696841) confirms the accepted name and its family placement. **Status:** two taxonomic sources agree on identity.

### Classical literature
**What the record shows:** 草之七 of the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) carries 五味子 through Su Gong, with the five-taste explanation, and preserves the colour grading, the ranking of sources, and the plant description. **Status:** a documented historical text; its medical reasoning is traditional.

### Authenticity and disambiguation
**What the record shows:** 五味子 (*S. chinensis*) is distinct from 南五味子 (*S. sphenanthera*) and from other *Schisandra* species, and the classical compass note is a colour grade rather than a species statement. **Status:** practical screening supported by taxonomy and the classical text.

### Safety
**What the record shows:** burning botanical material yields smoke and particulates, and sugar-rich fruit chars readily, adding acrid notes at high dose. **Status:** established.

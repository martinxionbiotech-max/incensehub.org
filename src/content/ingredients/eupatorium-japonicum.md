## Key facts

**Direct answer:** 泽兰 here means *Eupatorium japonicum* in Asteraceae, a 50–200 cm perennial of East Asian open ground that Flora of China records as 白头婆; the classical 泽兰 of the *Bencao Gangmu* is read today as the mint-family *Lycopus lucidus*.

- **Chinese label:** 泽兰 (zélán); the Flora of China equivalent is 白头婆 (báitóupó).
- **Species:** *Eupatorium japonicum*, one of the daisy-family (Asteraceae) aromatics.
- **Form:** a herbaceous perennial reaching 50–200 cm, native to China, Japan, and Korea.
- **Material:** the aerial herb, dried — leaf and stem, with no root or resin component.
- **Scent:** green and herbal, lightly aromatic, with a restrained sweetness.
- **Naming status:** contested; the classical description is read as *Lycopus lucidus*, while this page follows the *E. japonicum* identification.

The list is short on purpose. What matters to a buyer is not a row of attributes but the relationships between them: family, genus, part, and the other plant the label has covered.

## What the two characters describe

**Direct answer:** 泽 names wet ground and 兰 names a fragrant herb, so the compound reads as "the fragrant herb of the marshes" — a habitat-and-scent description rather than a species name.

That reading is a gloss on the characters, not a botanical diagnosis, but it explains a great deal about how the name behaves. A label built from a habitat and a scent is portable. Any plant that grows in damp places and carries a pleasant smell can satisfy it, and regional usage can attach the label to a different plant without anyone acting in bad faith. Descriptive names do not fail because people are careless. They fail because the description was never a specification in the first place.

The consequences show up in three places. In a market list, 泽兰 can be filled by whatever the region calls 泽兰, so two lots from two provinces may not be the same material. In a classical text, the name points at a plant described by morphology, which a modern botanist then has to match to a species. In a formula, the name gives no clue about which of those plants the author intended, because the author was working from a different frame of reference entirely.

There is a useful test for this kind of label. Ask what the name would have to lose before it became a specification. For 泽兰, it would have to lose the habitat, the scent language, and the regional usage, and gain a binomial, a part, and a harvest record. That is exactly the information a buyer should request.

The characters are a search key. They are not an identity.

## The classical record and the square stem

**Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu*, in the herb section 草之三, describes 泽兰 as a waterside plant with 方莖紫節 — a square stem and purple nodes — and modern botanists read that combination as the mint-family *Lycopus lucidus*, not as a *Eupatorium*.

The description is more specific than it first appears. A square stem is a familiar trait of the mint family (Lamiaceae), where the stem is typically four-angled rather than round. Purple nodes at the leaf joints add a second structural detail. Growing by water adds a habitat. Put the three together and the picture is a damp-ground mint relative, which is what *Lycopus lucidus* (地笋) is.

Asteraceae stems are generally round or ridged, not square, and the *Eupatorium* herbs are upright meadow and woodland-edge plants rather than waterside ones. So the classical description and the modern *E. japonicum* diverge on exactly the traits a field botanist would use first. This is not a matter of interpretation at the margins. The text is describing a different plant.

The entry also carries its own warning. The *Bencao Gangmu* notes that 泽兰 and 兰草 were treated as one thing in two kinds — 其與蘭草為一物二種 — which is an explicit classical acknowledgment that the naming was already unsettled when the entry was compiled. The confusion is not a modern projection onto an innocent text. The text says so itself.

What follows from this is an editorial position. A classical description is evidence about how a plant was understood, not a certificate of identity. It can tell a reader which traits mattered to the author, which habitat the author had in mind, and which neighbouring names were already in play. It cannot tell a buyer which species is in the sack. Those are different kinds of question, and mixing them is how a naming problem becomes a purchasing problem.

## The plant this page describes

**Direct answer:** *Eupatorium japonicum* is a herbaceous perennial 50–200 cm tall with short rhizomes, erect purplish-red stems, and opposite leaves 6–20 × 2–6.5 cm, native to China, Japan, and Korea.

Start with the underground part: short rhizomes, which give the plant a clumped rather than a creeping habit. Above them rise erect stems with a purplish-red cast. The leaves are borne in opposite pairs, and the ones botanists measure — from the middle of the stem — run 6–20 cm by 2–6.5 cm, in shapes that range from elliptic through ovate-elliptic to lanceolate, with pinnate veins and scattered yellow glands. Open ground and half-shade are its habitats, and its range covers China, Japan, and Korea. What is collected is everything above ground: leaf and stem, with no root and no resin.

Learning the morphology pays because it is the one identity check that does not rely on the seller's word. Three traits work as a set: opposite leaves exclude plants with alternate arrangements; composite flower heads, when the plant is in bloom, put it in Asteraceae; and the yellow glands on the leaf surface are a visible aromatic structure. None of the three is decisive alone, and together they are much harder to fake than a colour.

The glandular dots deserve a note of their own. They are one of the reasons the dried herb still smells of something after weeks in a jar. A leaf that has lost them — through over-drying, rough handling, or age — has lost much of what made it worth using. That is a freshness observation, not an identity observation, and the distinction matters later in this page when quality grading comes up.

The botanical record is held by [Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200023936) and by the [GBIF entry for the species](https://www.gbif.org/species/5403118).

## Reading the botanical description

**Direct answer:** The Flora of China entry is a compressed technical description, and each term in it answers a practical question — how the plant grows, how to recognise it, and which part is harvested.

A reader who does not work with botanical keys can still use the description, provided the vocabulary is unpacked.

**Perennial, herbaceous.** The plant lives for more than two seasons and dies back to the ground rather than carrying a woody trunk. That matters for harvest: the material is regrowth, cut and dried each season, not a timber.

**Short rhizomes.** A rhizome is a horizontal underground stem, not a root. "Short" describes a clumping habit rather than a spreading one, which is one reason the plant can be collected in stands rather than as scattered individuals.

**Erect, purplish-red stems.** Erect means upright rather than trailing. The colour is a pigment in the stem surface, useful as a field mark and useless as a species test on its own.

**Opposite leaves.** The leaves sit in pairs at each node, one on either side of the stem. This is a structural trait that narrows the field quickly, because many herbs are alternate.

**Median stem leaves.** Botanists measure the leaves from the middle of the stem, not the smallest ones near the base or the newest ones at the tip. The range 6–20 × 2–6.5 cm describes that middle population, and a single leaf outside the range proves nothing.

**Elliptic, narrowly elliptic, ovate-elliptic, lanceolate.** These are shape words. An elliptic leaf is widest near the middle and tapers at both ends; a lanceolate leaf is longer and narrower, widest below the middle. The list is a range of acceptable shapes, not four different plants.

**Pinnate venation.** One main vein runs the length of the leaf with side veins branching from it, like a feather. The pattern is visible against a light and is one of the cheapest checks a buyer can make.

**Yellow glandular dots.** Small glands visible on the leaf surface. They are the visible part of the aromatic apparatus, and their presence is why the dried herb still carries a smell.

**Aerial herb.** The harvested part is everything above ground — leaf and stem together. No root, no resin, no distilled oil.

Read this way, the description is not a wall of jargon. It is a checklist, and most of it can be applied with a hand lens and a little patience.

## 泽兰 and 佩兰: one genus, two species

**Direct answer:** 佩兰 is *Eupatorium fortunei*, a separate species in the same genus; the two share the fresh herbaceous Asteraceae character but are not interchangeable as materials.

Same genus means shared architecture: opposite leaves, composite heads, a green aromatic profile, a similar drying behaviour. Different species means different proportions of whatever makes the scent, and a different behaviour in a blend. The practical difference in aroma is small but real — 泽兰 reads a touch less sweet than 佩兰, with the sweetness sitting further back behind the green note.

The confusion has a mechanism, and it runs through the supply chain rather than through botany:

- Two species share a genus → their vernacular names overlap in regional speech → a supplier fills an order for "泽兰" with the local aromatic *Eupatorium* → the buyer receives a different species than the sample was approved against → the finished blend shifts in its top note, and the formula gets blamed for a sourcing error.

That chain is worth stating plainly because it is the most common way a naming problem turns into a product problem. Nothing in it requires dishonesty. Each step is locally reasonable. The error is structural.

The fix is procedural. A purchase specification should carry the binomial — *Eupatorium japonicum* for 泽兰, *Eupatorium fortunei* for 佩兰 — plus the part and the form. A supplier who cannot work with a binomial is not necessarily selling the wrong plant, but the buyer has no way to tell. That uncertainty is the actual cost.

## Aroma: a green note that supports

**Direct answer:** 泽兰 smells fresh, herbaceous, and lightly sweet, with the clean green character of the *Eupatorium* herbs; it works as an accent and disappears under heavier resins and woods.

The scent is mild. There is a green, slightly grassy opening, a soft herbal middle, and a sweetness that arrives late and stays low. It is closer to a fresh leaf than to a resin, and it has none of the density that makes a base note. In a blend it behaves like a herb in a kitchen: it lifts what is already there and then gets out of the way.

That behaviour has a physical explanation. Dried leaf material has a very high surface area relative to its mass, so its volatiles are exposed to the heat of the char front almost immediately. The chain runs like this:

- Dried leaf → high surface area and low bulk density → the flame or ember front passes quickly → leaf volatiles flash off early → little aromatic material survives into the sustained smoke → the note reads as a brief green lift rather than a lasting presence.

The result is a material with a short aromatic arc. A formulator who wants 泽兰 to be perceptible for longer has to change the physics, not the percentage: a finer grind, a binder that slows the burn, or a base that holds heat and releases it gradually. Adding more leaf usually just makes the opening sharper.

In practice, 泽兰 belongs with other green and herbal notes — 藿香 (*Agastache rugosa*), 艾草 (*Artemisia argyi*), and the lighter citrus peels — where its job is to keep a blend from smelling heavy. Against sandalwood, agarwood, or a resin base, it is almost invisible. That is not a defect. It is a register, and the formulator's task is to use it where the register is wanted.

## From harvest to powder

**Direct answer:** The herb is harvested, dried whole or cut, and ground only if a powder is needed; there is no distillation or extraction step in the material described here.

The sequence is short, and each step has a failure mode:

1. **Cutting** — the aerial herb comes off the plant as leaf and stem together. The timing of the cut relative to flowering changes the leaf-to-stem balance and the aroma that comes with it.
2. **Drying** — moisture is brought down until the tissue is brittle and keeps, in whole or cut form. This is the step that decides whether the material stays green or turns to hay.
3. **Milling, only if needed** — powder is made at the point of blending rather than by default. Milling is a commitment: it exposes fresh surface and starts a faster clock on aroma loss.

Drying deserves the most attention because it is where quality is usually lost. The chain is easy to follow:

- Cut herb → moisture still held inside the leaf → slow or uneven drying → enzymatic browning and continued respiration → colour darkens and the fresh green note turns hay-like → the material is stable but no longer lively.

Fast, even drying interrupts that chain. The leaf keeps its colour, the glandular dots stay intact, and the aroma that survives is closer to the growing plant. The exact parameters depend on the equipment and the batch, so no single number belongs in a general guide. The observable target does: brittle, clean-green material that crumbles rather than bends.

Storage follows the same logic. Dried herb is hygroscopic, so a sealed container in a dark, cool place slows both moisture pickup and volatile loss. Ground powder should be used soon after milling, because the surface exposed by grinding is surface that will give up its aroma to the air. Whole or cut herb keeps longer and gives the formulator the option to grind close to the day of use.

## Identity checks that actually work

**Direct answer:** Identity is settled by morphology and a binomial, not by colour or aroma; freshness is a separate question, and the two should never be collapsed into one quality score.

The table below is a diagnostic ladder, not a grading scheme. Each row answers a different question, and the right-hand column is there to stop a single trait from being over-read.

| Check | What you observe | What it supports | Where it stops |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stem cross-section | Square and four-angled versus round or ridged | Family signal — square points to Lamiaceae | A single cut stem is not a species test |
| Leaf arrangement | Opposite leaves with pinnate veins | Consistent with both *Eupatorium* and *Lycopus* | Shared by many herbs; weak alone |
| Glandular dots | Yellow dots visible on the leaf surface | Aromatic Asteraceae trait | Visible on several genera; not species-specific |
| Flower heads | Composite heads, when present | Places the plant in Asteraceae | Only available at flowering |
| Label binomial | *Eupatorium japonicum* stated in full | A specification the supplier can be held to | Only as good as the supplier's own traceability |
| Colour and aroma | Clean green colour, fresh herbal scent | Freshness and handling | Says nothing reliable about species |

Read the table from the bottom up and the logic becomes clear. Colour and aroma are the first things a buyer can assess and the last things that should be trusted for identity. They are freshness signals. A dull, brown lot and a fresh green lot can be the same species; a fresh green lot and a fresh green lot can be two different plants.

The ordering matters because photographs reward colour. A supplier's photo shows green material, the buyer reads green as good, and the identity question never gets asked. That is a predictable failure, and the fix is to ask for the binomial and the part before looking at the picture.

What follows for grading is a boundary: there is no standardized ladder for 泽兰, and inventing one would be false precision. The decisive check is identity. After identity, the useful checks are cleanliness, colour, and aroma, in that order.

## How a leaf behaves in a stick

**Direct answer:** Leaf powder is not a drop-in replacement for wood powder in a stick; it wets differently, packs at a lower density, and gives up its aroma earlier, so the binder and the drying schedule have to change with it.

A stick is a small engineering problem. Powder, binder, and water are mixed into a dough, extruded through a die, dried, and burned. Each material changes both the dough and the burn.

The chain from leaf to stick runs like this:

- Leaf powder → thin, irregular particles with a large surface → more water is needed to wet the same mass → the wet stick feels correct but shrinks unevenly as it dries → surface cracks appear and the core burns faster than the skin.

A second chain explains the aroma:

- High leaf fraction → low bulk density → a more porous stick → air moves through the matrix easily → the burn front advances faster → the green note arrives early and the stick finishes sooner than a wood-based stick of the same size.

In practice, a leaf-heavy formula usually needs more binder, slower initial drying, and a shorter predicted burn. Those are adjustments a formulator makes at the bench, not numbers that can be copied from a page, because they depend on the die, the binder, the ambient humidity, and the leaf lot.

The blending consequence is worth stating. A leaf gives an opening; a wood gives a body. A stick built only from leaf smells bright for a minute and thin afterwards, and a stick built only from wood may never show the green note at all. Using both is how the arc gets both ends.

## What the leaf will not do

**Direct answer:** 泽兰 will not carry a base, will not survive a hot direct burn with its aroma intact, and will not substitute for a resin or a dense wood, and no proportion change alters those limits.

Three boundaries are worth marking, because each is a place where a formulator can waste material trying to force a result.

It will not provide depth. Depth comes from materials that release heavier compounds slowly, and a thin leaf does not have them in the quantity a base needs. Adding more leaf makes the opening sharper, not the base deeper.

It will not survive a hot burn unchanged. The volatiles that give the fresh note are the first thing to leave when the temperature climbs. A stick that burns hot will smell of scorched plant material rather than of 泽兰, and the fix is in the burn, not in the percentage.

It will not stand in for a resin. Resins are sticky, dense, and slow; leaves are dry, light, and fast. They can sit in the same formula, and they cannot do each other's work.

A formulator who accepts those limits will use the herb well. One who fights them will keep increasing the percentage and wondering why the blend gets harsher rather than greener.

## A bench protocol for a new lot

**Direct answer:** Three checks — structure, crush, and a small burn — separate a well-handled lot from a poor one before it enters a blend, and a retained reference sample makes the comparison repeatable.

Most buying decisions on leafy herbs are made from a photograph and a sentence. That is not enough, and the gap can be closed cheaply at a bench.

**Structure.** Take a handful and look at what is actually in it. Stem, leaf, and dust should be distinguishable. A lot that is mostly stem will smell weaker and burn differently from a leaf-heavy lot, because the aromatic tissue is concentrated in the leaf. Stem load is a formulator's variable, not a defect in itself, but it has to be known.

**Crush.** Rub a leaf between the fingers and smell immediately, then again after half a minute. Fresh material releases a green, sweet-herbal note; stale material releases hay or dust. The second sniff matters because the first is dominated by the burst that crushing produces.

**Burn.** Burn a pinch on a live coal or a heat-safe surface in a ventilated space. Watch how fast it catches, how much smoke it makes, what colour the ash is, and whether the residual smell is green or scorched. This tells a formulator more about blend behaviour than the dry aroma does.

| Test | What it reveals | What it cannot reveal |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Part ratio, cleanliness, stem load | Species |
| Crush | Freshness and volatile strength | Species, burn behaviour |
| Burn | Ignition, smoke, residual note | Storage stability over months |
| Reference comparison | Drift from the approved lot | Whether the reference itself was right |

The last row is the honest limit. A reference sample is only as good as the first decision. If the original lot was misidentified, every later comparison reproduces the error, which is why the binomial belongs on the jar and not only on the invoice.

## Where sourcing goes wrong

**Direct answer:** The common failure is not fraud but drift: an ambiguous label gets filled with whatever the region calls 泽兰, and the blend then behaves differently from the sample that was approved.

Drift is worth understanding because it is invisible in a single transaction. A buyer orders 泽兰 from a new region, receives clean green herb with a fresh smell, and approves it. The material is a genuine aromatic herb. It is simply not the one the formula was built on. Weeks later the finished product smells flatter or greener than the reference, and the search for a cause starts in the wrong place.

The chain has four links:

- Ambiguous label → regional interpretation of that label → a different species or a different part enters the lot → the blend's top note shifts → the formula is reworked to compensate → the real cause, a naming drift, is never recorded.

Breaking the chain is a documentation task. A buyer can ask for four things before a lot is approved: the binomial, the plant part, the drying method, and a retained reference sample. The first three are specifications. The fourth is the only one that survives staff changes. A reference sample with a binomial written on the jar is worth more than a page of description, because it can be compared against the next lot by smell and sight in under a minute.

There is a second failure mode that looks like quality but is really handling: herb that was clean and green at harvest arriving dull and dusty after storage. That is a supply-chain problem, not an identity problem, and it is fixed by storage and transit conditions rather than by changing suppliers. Keeping the two failure modes apart is half the work of buying this material well.

## When 泽兰 fits a brief — and when it does not

**Direct answer:** 泽兰 suits briefs that need a fresh green lift near the top of the burn; it is a poor choice when the brief asks for depth, sweetness, or a note that lasts.

The distinction is about register and physics rather than prestige. A material that is wrong for a brief is not a bad material; the mistake is in the matching.

| Brief | Fit | Why | Where else to look |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light, green, spring-like blend | Strong | Fresh herbal top note with low sweetness | — |
| Herbal register built around 藿香 | Good | Same green aromatic family; adds a softer edge | 藿香 for a more penetrating herbal note |
| Sweet, resinous, temple-style blend | Poor | Too thin to survive resins and woods | A floral or a resin at the top |
| Low-scent, smoke-forward aesthetic | Poor | Contributes little aroma and much ash | A neutral base with a trace of resin |
| Blend that must hold across a long burn | Weak | Short aromatic arc; the note is gone early | A wood or resin base to carry duration |

Two rows deserve a caveat. "Poor" does not mean unusable; a formulator can add a small proportion for a fleeting green opening and accept that it will not last. And "strong" does not mean forgiving: a leaf-heavy lot and a stem-heavy lot will behave differently at the same percentage, so the fit has to be re-tested when the lot changes.

In practice, the useful question is not "is 泽兰 good?" but "what job is this blend asking a leaf to do?" If the answer is a brief green lift, the material is well suited. If the answer is depth, the formulator is asking a leaf to behave like a wood, and no amount of leaf will do it.

## The leaf as an assistant, not a chief

**Direct answer:** In the classical 君臣佐使 frame — chief, minister, assistant, guide — a light leaf like 泽兰 is normally an assistant or a guide rather than a chief, and judging it outside that role misreads what it is for.

The framework is traditional, not a chemical model, and this page uses it as a vocabulary for roles rather than as a claim about how smoke behaves. Even so, the vocabulary is useful because it names something true about blends: materials do different jobs, and a material judged in the wrong job looks worse than it is.

A chief material carries the composition. It is present in a proportion large enough to define the smell, and it usually has the density to last. A minister supports the chief and shares the centre of the blend. An assistant modifies — brightening, softening, drying, or lifting — without taking over. A guide carries the aroma through the burn or points it in a direction. 泽兰, with its fresh green note and its short arc, belongs in the last two categories.

Three consequences follow. First, a small proportion can be the right proportion; adding more of an assistant does not make the blend more of it, it makes the blend unbalanced. Second, the material's quality has to be judged in a blend, not only in the jar, because its job is relational. Third, a leaf that fails as a chief has not failed as a material. It has been asked to do a job that its physics does not support.

The chain is worth stating because it explains a common disappointment:

- Light leaf → short aromatic arc → the note is concentrated at the start of the burn → the formulator notices it early and adds more → the opening becomes sharp while the middle stays unchanged → the blend reads as unbalanced rather than stronger.

That is why the classical vocabulary survives in practice. It encodes a division of labour that a bench trial rediscovers on its own.

## Traditional record, modern evidence

**Direct answer:** The old materia-medica entry is traditional belief, not proof; the botanical identity is settled, and no clinical claim is made here.

A reader can hold two thoughts at once. The *Bencao Gangmu* discusses a plant it calls 泽兰, and its description — waterside habitat, four-angled stem, purple joints — is the description of a mint-family herb. That is a fact about a text. The species this page catalogues, *Eupatorium japonicum*, is a daisy-family plant with a documented modern identity. That is a fact about a plant.

Mixing the two produces the usual errors: treating the old entry as a description of the modern species, or treating the modern species as a correction of the old text. Neither is right. The text described what its author saw and named; the modern species is what a different tradition of botany recognises. The useful question is not which one is true, but which one a buyer is holding.

What the record does establish is narrow and real: the name has been used for more than one plant, the confusion was acknowledged early, and the modern identification of the classical plant is *Lycopus lucidus*. Nothing in that establishes a therapeutic claim, and this page makes none.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Burning plant material produces smoke and particulate matter, so 泽兰 incense should be used in a ventilated space, away from children and pets, and the smoke should not be inhaled in concentration.

The dried herb is also a flammable material in its own right. Store it away from heat and open flame, and treat ash and embers as live until they are cold. Anyone with respiratory sensitivity should be cautious around any incense smoke, and the sensible default is a shorter burn in a larger room rather than a longer burn in a small one. See the site's [safety guide](/safety/). Nothing on this page is medical advice.

## FAQ

**What is 泽兰 (zelan)?** In this encyclopedia, 泽兰 is *Eupatorium japonicum*, an Asteraceae perennial native to China, Japan, and Korea, recorded by Flora of China under the Chinese name 白头婆. The aerial herb is the material, and its scent is fresh, green, and lightly sweet.

**Is 泽兰 the same as 佩兰?** No. The two sit in one genus but not one species — *Eupatorium japonicum* for 泽兰, *E. fortunei* for 佩兰. They share a fresh herbaceous character, which is exactly why trade confuses them, and a binomial specification is what keeps them apart.

Swapping one for the other in an existing blend is a formula change, not a purchasing shortcut: the species are close enough that the swap may smell plausible at first, and different enough that the top note and burn behaviour can shift. Rebalance and re-check identity before substituting.

**Why is the name 泽兰 contested?** Two descriptions are in circulation and they do not describe one plant. The old text gives a waterside herb with a four-angled stem and purple joints, which botanists place in the mint family as *Lycopus lucidus*. The daisy-family *Eupatorium japonicum* has also answered to the name regionally. A label with two referents is contested by definition.

**Does a square stem prove a plant is the classical 泽兰?** It supports the mint-family reading, and that is the direction the classical description points. It does not prove species on its own. A square stem plus waterside habitat plus purple nodes is a strong combination; any one of them alone is a hint, not an identification.

**How should I specify 泽兰 when buying?** Give the binomial (*Eupatorium japonicum*), the part (aerial herb), the form (whole, cut, or powder), and the drying method. Ask for a retained sample and label it with the binomial rather than the trade name.

## Summary

The plant this page calls 泽兰 is *Eupatorium japonicum*, a daisy-family perennial of East Asia recorded by Flora of China as 白头婆. It reaches 50–200 cm, carries opposite leaves with yellow glands, and yields a dried aerial herb whose scent is green, fresh, and only lightly sweet — a supporting note in a blend rather than a leading one.

The name is where the difficulty sits. A classical description of a waterside plant with a square stem and purple nodes points to the mint-family *Lycopus lucidus*, and the *Bencao Gangmu* itself records confusion with 兰草. That makes the label a documented case of a descriptive name that never settled on one species.

For a buyer, the rule is short: the binomial is the specification, the morphology is the check, and colour and aroma measure freshness rather than identity. The rest of the page is elaboration on those three points.

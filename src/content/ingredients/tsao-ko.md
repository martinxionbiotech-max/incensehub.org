**Direct answer:** 草果 (cǎoguǒ, tsaoko) is the dried fruit of a ginger-family plant grown in the hills of southern China and northern Southeast Asia — a ridged, red-brown pod full of dark seeds that smell of smoke, warm spice and camphor. In a censer it is a shadowing material, not a headline: it darkens a base, bridges spice and resin, and disappears if it is given the front of a blend. Three other fruits travel under names close enough to be mistaken for it.

## Key facts

**Direct answer:** 草果 is a dried aromatic fruit of the ginger family, used as a culinary spice across southwestern China and borrowed into modern incense as a smoky, camphoraceous 香药 accent.

Almost every practical question about 草果 comes down to one of two things: which fruit is actually in the bag, and whether the drying was good enough to preserve what the fruit had. ## What the fruit is, and why the pod matters

**Direct answer:** The trade material is the mature fruit, dried whole: a fibrous, longitudinally ridged pod around several chambers of dark, aromatic seeds. The pod and the seed do not smell the same, and that difference decides how you prepare it.

*Amomum tsao-ko* is a rhizomatous perennial of the ginger family, and like most of its relatives it holds its aroma in volatile oil rather than in resin or wood. The oil is concentrated in and around the seeds. The pod wall is thick and fibrous and carries far less of the aroma; ground together with the seed it adds a dry, tannic roughness, which is either useful structure or unwanted harshness depending on what you are building.

This is why the same sack of fruit can produce two different materials. A blend made from whole ground pods carries the pod's dryness along with the seed's aroma. A blend made from seeds only is rounder and more direct, with a shorter, cleaner tail. Neither is "correct". What matters is that the choice is deliberate, because the difference is large enough to change the balance of a formula.

The botanical naming has also moved. Flora of China treats the plant as *Amomum tsaoko*; GBIF has transferred it to *Lanxangia tsao-ko*. Two names, one plant — a synonym, not a substitute. When a supplier's paperwork cites either name, the useful check is not the spelling but the material: ridged pod, dark seed, smoky-camphor smell when cracked. See the [Flora of China entry](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=240001100) for the accepted description.

The practical consequence of the anatomy is a chain that repeats every time someone judges 草果 by sniffing an intact pod:

intact pod → thick fibrous wall → very little oil reaches the air → the cold smell is faint and mostly "dry spice" → the fruit is judged as weak → the pod is cracked and ground → seed oil is exposed → the true aroma appears, sharp and smoky, all at once.

That is why an uncracked fruit tells you almost nothing. The nose has to reach the seed.

## Smoke, camphor, spice: reading the aroma in layers

**Direct answer:** 草果 smells in three layers — a sharp camphoraceous lift, a warm spice middle, and a resinous, smoky base. The layers leave the censer in that order, which is why a blend can smell right when cold and turn heavy at the end.

The top layer is the part that most resembles other *Amomum* fruits: a cooling, almost eucalyptus sharpness that sits high in the nose and clears fast. The middle is warmer and rounder — the spice impression people reach for words like "clove" or "cinnamon" to describe, though 草果 is sweeter-smelling than either and less pointed. The base is where 草果 becomes itself: resinous, tarry, with a smoke that feels like an old fire rather than a fresh one.

Heat reverses the order in which you meet these layers. Volatile molecules with low boiling points leave early; the heavier, stickier fraction stays on the material and arrives late.

cold material → the three layers are read together → the blend smells balanced → the censer is lit → the camphor lift volatilises first → the middle thins → the resinous base is what remains → the final third of the burn is darker than the first.

This is the single most useful thing to know before blending with 草果. A formula that is judged only by the cold smell will usually be overdosed, because the part the nose approved — the bright camphor — is the part that disappears first. The part that lingers is the part that was hardest to notice.

There is also a distinction worth keeping clean: the smoke of 草果 is not the same as the smoke of combustion. One is a note in the material; the other is what happens when any plant matter burns. A lot that smells only of campfire, with no camphor and no warmth beneath, has lost the fruit and kept the surface. That is a defect, not a style.

## The name problem: four fruits and one borrowed word

**Direct answer:** "Black cardamom" is a category, not a species. It covers 草果 and Indian black cardamom, and the word "cardamom" also pulls in 白豆蔻 and 草豆蔻, so a label alone cannot tell you which fruit you have.

The confusion is not a small etymological curiosity. It changes what a buyer receives, what a formulator smells, and what a finished blend does. The four materials are all ginger-family fruits with aromatic seeds, and at a glance — brown, wrinkled, seed-filled — they can pass for one another.

| Material | Chinese | Botanical name | Useful visual signal | Aroma signal | Typical role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tsaoko | 草果 | *Amomum tsao-ko* (*Lanxangia tsao-ko*) | Large, ovoid, sharply ridged, red-brown pod | Smoke over camphor, warm and resinous | Dark accent in spice and incense blends |
| White cardamom | 白豆蔻 | *Amomum kravanh* | Rounder, paler, thinner-shelled | Bright camphor and eucalyptus, sweeter | Lifting top note |
| Katsumadai | 草豆蔻 | *Alpinia katsumadae* | Rounder, browner, seeds in a tight cluster | Camphor with a bitter edge | Culinary and traditional use |
| Sharen | 砂仁 | *Amomum villosum* | Small, rough to spiny, dark brown | Strong camphor, medicinal | Digestive tradition, minor incense use |
| Indian black cardamom | — | *Amomum subulatum* | Large, wrinkled, near-black, often smoke-dried | Smoke, but harsher and less sweet | Culinary; not the Chinese material |

Read the table by column, not by row. The visual signals overlap enough to fool a photograph; the aroma signals separate cleanly once the fruit is cracked. And the Chinese names separate even more cleanly than the English ones, which is why the trade in Chinese-speaking markets relies on 草果, 白豆蔻, 草豆蔻 and 砂仁 rather than on translations.

The 草果 / 草豆蔻 pair is the trap that catches careful people. The names share the character 草 ("grass"), both are ginger-family fruits, and both are sold dried and whole. They are not the same plant, and they do not behave the same in a blend: 草豆蔻 is more bitter and more openly medicinal, 草果 is sweeter in the middle and smokier at the base. A substitution made on the strength of a shared character produces a formula that is recognisably wrong to anyone who knows both. The site keeps a separate entry for [katsumadai](/ingredients/katsumadai/) for exactly this reason, and a parallel one for [cardamom](/ingredients/cardamom/).

Mislabeling also runs the other way. Because "black cardamom" sells, the phrase gets applied to *Amomum subulatum* as well, and a buyer who specified "black cardamom" in good faith has no contractual ground to complain about receiving it. The fix is procedural: order by botanical name, and make the name part of the purchase record rather than a note in an email.

## Why a cooking spice belongs in a censer

**Direct answer:** 草果 entered incense as a 香药 — an aromatic-medicine spice — not as a classical 香材. Its incense use borrows the same dark warmth that anchors a Yunnan braise, and it is honest to present it that way.

The 香药 category is old and broad. It covers aromatics that were traded as much for household and apothecary use as for the censer, and it explains why so many kitchen spices appear in Chinese incense writing without ever becoming canonical incense materials. 草果 sits in that group. Its fame is culinary and, in the traditional materia-medica sense, medicinal; its appearance in a censer is a later, practical extension.

That extension is not arbitrary. The reason a braise and a blend both reach for 草果 is the same reason: it gives weight. In food, weight means a savoury bass note under chilli and aromatics. In incense, weight means a resinous floor under brighter woods and flowers. The mechanism differs, though, and the difference explains most beginner failures.

In a pot, the aromatics infuse into fat and water at around the boil. The volatiles dissolve into the cooking medium, are held there, and reach the nose slowly and repeatedly as the dish is eaten. On a censer, there is no medium. The material is heated directly, its surface temperature climbs far past the boiling point of water, and the oil that would have dissolved into a stew instead flashes off or scorches.

braise → oil dissolves into fat → aroma is buffered and released slowly → the spice tastes round → censer → no solvent, direct heat → oil volatilises fast, then the surface chars → the same spice smells harsher and shorter.

What follows from this is a rule of proportion and placement rather than a number. 草果 goes in small, and it goes under something. Put it on top of a blend and it will dominate the first minute and muddy the last five. Bury it beneath a resin or a wood and it does the job it was chosen for.

## From whole pod to blend: the preparation sequence

**Direct answer:** Crack the fruit, decide whether the pod goes in, toast lightly or not at all, grind coarse, and use the powder while it still smells of the seed. Each step trades aroma intensity against aroma life.

The sequence matters more than any single step.

**Inspection.** Break a few fruits open before you commit a batch. The inside should be filled with dark, glossy seeds, not hollow shell, not dusty remnants, not webbing. A musty smell at this stage is not a style note; it is a reason to stop.

**Cracking.** A whole pod is a sealed container. Cracking it — a press with the flat of a knife, a mortar, a rolling pin — exposes the seed chambers and releases the oil. This is also the moment of truth: the aroma should arrive immediately and should be complex. If cracking produces only a dry woody smell, the lot is old.

**Pod in or out.** The pod wall adds body and a certain tannic grip, and it also adds dust and a rougher edge. For a smooth blend, seed only. For a rustic, smoky, "burning wood" effect, a portion of the pod is defensible. Keep the choice written down, because reproducing a blend without it is guesswork.

**Toasting.** Gentle dry heat drives off surface moisture and pushes some of the aroma out of the seed, which makes the material smell stronger in the jar. It also spends volatiles that would otherwise have gone into the censer. Toast cool and brief, or skip it; a dark roast turns the smoke note bitter and leaves nothing underneath.

**Grinding and sieving.** Grind in short pulses so the oil does not heat up in the mill. Sieve out the fibrous pod fragments if you want an even burn. Coarse powder releases slowly; fine powder releases hard and fades fast.

**Storage.** Whole pods keep far better than powder. Ground 草果 stales faster than most makers expect, and the first thing to go is the camphor lift, which leaves a flat, dusty smoke behind.

grinding → surface area multiplies → oxygen and heat reach the oil → the light fractions leave first → the powder smells "smoky but dull" → the blend is dosed higher to compensate → the base note becomes bitter.

That loop is the most common way a good material is ruined in a workshop: the powder faded, so more was added, and the extra dose brought tannin rather than aroma.

## Format matters: stick, cone, loose powder, warmer

**Direct answer:** The same 草果 behaves differently across incense formats, because each format changes how much heat reaches the material and how long the heat lasts. A dose that works in a stick can be too much in a cone and almost invisible in a warmer.

The variable is heat flux at the surface of the particle. In a stick, the herb is bound with a combustible powder and burns along a thin line; the coal front passes each particle quickly, so the aroma is released in a short, hot burst and then fixed in the ash. In a cone, the mass is thicker and the interior stays hot longer, which pushes the release toward the heavier, resinous end of the material. Loose powder on a coal behaves like a flash: almost everything leaves at once. An electric warmer, which runs far below combustion, draws out the light fraction slowly and often never reaches the smoky base at all.

stick → thin coal line, brief heat → top and middle notes dominate → 草果 reads as spice, not smoke → cone → thicker coal mass, sustained heat → base notes arrive → the same dose reads smoky and heavy → warmer → low, steady heat → only the light fraction leaves → the smoky character is largely absent.

What follows from this is a practical rule about dosing by format rather than by recipe. A formula developed on sticks and then produced as cones will taste darker, and the maker will usually blame the material. A formula developed on a warmer and then burned as a stick will seem to have gained a harsh note that was always present but never volatilised.

Placement matters nearly as much. 草果 buried inside a stick is shielded by the binder and the neighbouring powders, so it releases late and gently. The same amount dusted onto the surface releases immediately and dominates the opening. Neither placement is wrong; they are different instruments.

| Format | Heat profile | What 草果 contributes | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incense stick | Thin, fast coal line | Spice and warm middle; smoke understated | Dosing as if it were a cone |
| Incense cone | Thick, sustained heat | Full base, heavy smoke | Overdosing and getting tar |
| Loose powder | Direct, immediate | A quick smoky bloom | Judging the blend by its first ten seconds |
| Electric warmer | Low, steady | Light fraction only | Expecting the smoky base to appear |

The table is a reminder that "how much 草果" is not a single number. It is a number plus a format plus a position, and the three travel together.

## Grading 草果 without a laboratory

**Direct answer:** Grade on fill, seed condition and the smell released by cracking. There is no universal commercial ladder for 草果, so build a three-tier screen and reject anything musty, hollow or flat.

| Grade | What the fruit looks like | What the seed looks like | What cracking releases | Sensible use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Intact, heavy for its size, uniform red-brown, ridges sharp | Dark, glossy, oily, chambers full | Immediate smoke-camphor, warm middle, long resinous tail | Accent in a finished blend or a premium line |
| B | Intact but mixed sizes, some flattened ridges, slight colour variation | Present but drier, some shrivelled | Clear aroma that fades quickly on the air | General blending, test batches |
| C | Broken pods, loose seed, dust, foreign matter | Sparse, dull, dusty | Flat, dusty, sometimes musty | Reject for incense |

Three checks carry most of the weight.

*Weight in the hand.* A well-dried fruit that is full of seed feels dense for its size. A pod that feels light is either underfilled or over-dried until the seed shrank; both mean less aroma per gram and more pod per gram.

*The break.* A clean snap with a full interior is a good sign. A crush that produces dust and empty chambers is not. Look at the seed colour: dark and glossy suggests oil still present; pale and dry suggests it has gone.

*The smell after cracking.* This is the only test that matters in the end. The aroma should be layered — smoke, then warm spice, then camphor — and it should last on the air for a while. A single flat note, or a smell that vanishes in seconds, means the lot is tired.

What to reject outright: any musty or mouldy smell (a storage failure and a health concern, not a flavour), any chemical or petrol edge (contamination), any lot that smells of nothing until it is burned. Burning covers a multitude of faults, which is exactly why the cold check is the honest one.

## What a 草果 specification should state

**Direct answer:** A usable specification names the species, the part, the crop and the drying method, and states what the lot is for. Most disputes about 草果 trace back to a specification that named a colour and a country instead.

The common failure is a purchase order that says "black cardamom, dried, whole" and nothing else. That phrase describes at least two species and any number of drying regimes, so the supplier is free to ship whatever fits the words. A short, specific list closes most of the gap:

| Field | Why it belongs in the record | What goes wrong without it |
|---|---|---|
| Species | Separates 草果 from *A. subulatum* and from 草豆蔻 | A different fruit arrives, legally compliant |
| Part | Whole pod, seed only, or powder | Pod-heavy lots pass as whole fruit |
| Crop or lot | Distinguishes fresh stock from held stock | Old material is sold at new-crop pricing |
| Drying | Sun, shade or smoke; who dried it | The smoke note is assumed rather than checked |
| Moisture condition | Predicts mould risk in transit | Musty lots are discovered after payment |
| Intended use | Food, 香药, or incense | Culinary tolerance for dust and shell is applied to incense |
| Screening | Cracked-fruit aroma check, foreign matter | Quality is asserted, never demonstrated |

None of these fields requires a laboratory. They require a supplier who knows the material and a buyer who writes down what was agreed. The specification is also a useful discipline on the buyer's side: writing "species: *Amomum tsao-ko*" forces a decision that a phrase like "black cardamom" quietly avoids.

For the wider paperwork question — what a spec sheet and certificate of analysis should contain, and which claims are worth demanding — the site's guide to [incense spec sheets and COAs](/blog/incense-spec-sheet-coa/) covers the structure. 草果 is a small ingredient, and small ingredients are exactly where unspecified language does the most damage, because nobody notices until the blend is finished.

## Origin claims, and what they can't carry

**Direct answer:** 草果 comes from the mountains of Yunnan and Guangxi and from adjacent northern Southeast Asia. Origin is a real fact about the material; it is a weak proxy for quality, and treating it as a grade is a buying mistake.

The plant is native to the southern Chinese highlands — Yunnan and Guangxi are the names that matter in trade — with a range that crosses into northern Vietnam and Laos. The fruit is harvested, dried whole, and sold on. Beyond that, the specific mountain, the drying method and the storage history are usually invisible by the time a buyer sees a sample.

This is why origin labels do badly as quality signals. A famous growing area can produce a badly dried lot; a lesser-known area can produce an excellent one. The label describes geography, not care. The buyer who pays a premium for a place name without cracking a fruit is buying a story.

There is a fair use for origin, though. It tells you which trade the fruit came through and what style the seller is claiming. Those claims are worth hearing and worth testing: a lot that lives up to a strong origin claim will show it when cracked, and a lot that does not will show that too. Use origin to set expectations, then verify with the three checks above.

## Where 草果 sits in a formula

**Direct answer:** Treat 草果 as an assistant, not a chief. It darkens the base, bridges resin and spice, and gives a blend a floor; it does not lead, and it does not lift.

The 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) frame is a useful way to say this without inventing a rule. A chief material defines the blend. A minister supports it. An assistant modifies a quality — temperature, texture, duration. A guide carries the whole thing and fixes it in place. 草果 is an assistant with guide tendencies: it modifies the base and it can hold a blend together at the bottom, but it never defines the composition.

Operationally, that means three uses.

*As a darkener.* Under sandalwood or a sweet balsamic resin, a small amount of 草果 takes the shine off and gives the drydown a shadow. This is the most reliable use and the easiest to overdo.

*As a bridge.* Between a warm spice group (clove, cinnamon, galangal) and a resin group (frankincense, benzoin), 草果 covers the seam. It has enough spice to belong to the first and enough resinous smoke to belong to the second.

*As a smoke note without smoke.* For blends that want a suggestion of burning wood but should not smell like a fireplace, 草果 gives the impression at low dose. Higher doses stop suggesting and start insisting.

The cost side is real, and it is where most blends fail:

too much 草果 → the resinous base outlasts everything else → the drydown turns tarry → the blend reads as "burnt" even when nothing burned → the maker adds a top note to compensate → the top and base now disagree.

A simple pairing map, with the trade-off stated rather than hidden:

| If the blend needs | 草果 contributes | The cost |
|---|---|---|
| A darker drydown under sweet wood | Resinous floor, less sweetness | Can mute the wood's own top note |
| A seam between spice and resin | Warm middle with smoke | Doubles up if clove or cinnamon is already high |
| A smoke impression without smoke materials | Tar and old-fire suggestion | Turns bitter if dosed high or toasted dark |
| More "weight" in a thin blend | Body and duration | Weight is not complexity; it can flatten a delicate composition |

The honest summary is that 草果 is a tool with a narrow useful range. It does one thing well and several things badly, and the difference between the two is dose and placement.

## Safety, storage and the limits of the claim

**Direct answer:** The risks here are ordinary and real: combustion smoke, mould in badly stored fruit, and the temptation to treat a traditional aromatic as a medicine. None of them requires exotic precautions; all of them require attention.

Any burning aromatic produces smoke and airborne particulate, and 草果 is no exception: its own smokiness is a scent, not a filtration system. Work in moving air, keep your face out of the plume, and site lit material where children and animals cannot reach it. Our [safety guide](/safety/) sets out the general case.

Storage is the second risk. Dried fruit that has picked up moisture can grow mould, and mouldy 草果 smells musty before it looks bad. Keep it airtight, dark and cool, inspect periodically, and discard rather than "air out" a musty lot. The same rule applies to powder, which is more vulnerable because it has more surface exposed.

The third risk is linguistic. 草果 has a documented place in traditional aromatic medicine, and that language is easy to slide from "used traditionally" into "does this". This page does not make that slide. Traditional use is a historical fact about the material; it is not evidence of a clinical effect, and the site treats it that way throughout. If you want the material for its smell, the smell is what you can rely on.

## The historical record, read carefully

**Direct answer:** 草果 is well documented as a southwestern Chinese culinary spice and as a 香药, and it is absent from the classical incense compendium. Those three facts are the whole of the documented record, and they are enough.

| Layer | What is documented | What it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Culinary | A signature spice of Yunnan and Sichuan braising and stewing | Use as a food aromatic; smoky, warming character |
| 香药 | An aromatic-medicine spice in the broader materia medica | Traditional household and apothecary use |
| Incense canon | No entry in the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) | No classical authority for incense use |
| Botanical | *Amomum tsao-ko*, Zingiberaceae, Yunnan/Guangxi | Identity and disambiguation |
| Clinical | Nothing established here | No medical claims |

The absence from 香乘 is often read as a verdict. It is not. The compendium records a particular canon at a particular moment; a material can be useful, traded and well known without appearing in it. What the absence does mean is that anyone claiming a "classical 草果 incense recipe" is claiming something the record does not support. The material's incense life is modern, and saying so costs nothing.

## Common forms and what each is for

**Direct answer:** 草果 is sold whole, as separated seed, and as powder. Whole fruit is the safest buy, seed is the cleanest aroma, and powder is the most convenient and the shortest-lived.

| Form | Advantage | Drawback | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried fruit | Keeps longest; identity is visible | Must be cracked; pod adds harshness | Buying, storing, judging quality |
| Separated seed | Direct aroma; no pod tannin | Harder to verify; can be a blend of lots | Smooth, resin-forward blends |
| Coarse powder | Easy to weigh and disperse | Stales; dust | Test batches, short-run production |
| Fine powder | Strong immediate release | Fades fastest; can clog a burn | Incense cones, fixed formulas |

A note on the powder trade: pre-ground 草果 is the form most likely to hide a problem, because the visual signals — pod shape, seed colour, fill — are gone. If you buy powder, buy from a supplier who will tell you the species and the grinding date, and smell the jar before you accept the shipment.

## FAQ

**Is 草果 the same as black cardamom?** Only in the loose English sense. "Black cardamom" is a trade category that includes 草果 (*Amomum tsao-ko*) and Indian black cardamom (*Amomum subulatum*), which are different species with different aromas. 草果 is smokier and sweeter in the middle; *A. subulatum* is harsher and more purely smoky. Neither is white cardamom (白豆蔻).

**Can I use supermarket 草果 in an incense blend?** Yes, with two provisos. Culinary-grade fruit is not sorted for incense use, so check for mould, dust and a flat smell before committing it to a blend. And supermarket stock may be old, which shows up as a missing camphor lift when the fruit is cracked.

**Why does my 草果 smell strong in the jar but weak in the censer?** Because the strong part is the volatile top note, and it leaves early. A jar concentrates the headspace; a censer moves air past the material continuously. If the drydown is thin, the problem is usually dose and placement rather than the fruit itself.

**Does 草果 need to be toasted before use?** No. Toasting is optional and it is a trade, not an improvement: it drives off moisture and some volatiles, which makes the material smell stronger cold and weaker in the burn. If you toast, keep it light.

**How is 草果 different from 草豆蔻?** Different genus, different fruit, near-identical name pattern. 草果 is *Amomum tsao-ko*, smoky and resinous. 草豆蔻 is *Alpinia katsumadae*, more bitter and more openly medicinal. The shared 草 character is a naming accident, not a botanical link.

## Summary

草果 (*Amomum tsao-ko*, *Lanxangia tsao-ko* in some databases) is the dried, ridged fruit of a ginger-family plant from the Yunnan–Guangxi highlands. Its aroma runs from a camphoraceous lift through warm spice to a resinous, smoky base, and that order of departure — top first, base last — explains why it is so easy to overdose. In incense it is an assistant material: a darkener, a bridge, a floor. It is not a classical 香乘 ingredient, it is not Indian black cardamom, and it is not 草豆蔻. Buy it by botanical name, judge it by the smell released when the fruit is cracked, and use it in small amounts under something else.

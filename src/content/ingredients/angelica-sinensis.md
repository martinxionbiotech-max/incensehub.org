> **Direct answer:** Dong quai (当归, dāngguī) is the dried root of *Angelica sinensis*, an umbellifer native to China and cultivated chiefly in Gansu, Sichuan, and Yunnan. The *Bencao Gangmu* records it under the classical names 乾归, 山蕲, and 文无, and the materia-medica tradition calls it 女人要药. In aromatic work it is a sachet herb, not a burned incense staple — the *Angelica* that belongs in 合香 is its genus-mate 白芷 (*Angelica dahurica*).

A packet labelled "angelica root" can hold one of at least three different things, and only one of them is this plant. That ambiguity is the practical heart of the page. Everything else — the proverb of a name, the materia-medica fame, the quiet sweet scent — follows from getting the species right first.

## The plant behind the root

**Direct answer:** *Angelica sinensis* is a perennial umbellifer with a thick, aromatic taproot, divided leaves, and compound umbels of small white flowers. It grows as a cultivated crop in western and southwestern China, and the root is lifted and dried for use.

The carrot family is a large and confusing one, and *Angelica* sits inside it among plants that share a body plan: hollow or ribbed stems, finely divided foliage, and umbels that look like open parasols in summer. *Angelica sinensis* follows that plan, with a stem that carries a purplish cast and a root that is the commercial point — thick, fleshy, and heavy with the aromatic oil that gives the dried material its scent.

Wild populations exist, but the material of trade is grown. The crop is cultivated in Gansu, Sichuan, and Yunnan, provinces whose elevation and cool growing season suit a plant that wants moisture and a long season to build a root. Roots are lifted late in the year, once the aerial growth has died back and the plant's energy has returned underground. They are cleaned, trimmed, and dried, and the dried root is what moves through the trade: whole, in slices, or milled to powder.

Flora of China records the species, and the GBIF record fixes the binomial against global occurrence data. Those two references do the identity work. They do not tell you what is in a particular packet — more on that below.

The European garden angelica (*Angelica archangelica*) is a cousin, not a synonym. It is a larger, sweeter, more resinous plant with its own culinary and confectionery history, and its root is not the material described here. The English phrase "angelica root" can point at either, which is one reason the binomial belongs in any serious specification.

## One English word, two species

**Direct answer:** 当归 (*Angelica sinensis*) and 白芷 (*Angelica dahurica*) share a genus and an English name, and almost nothing else that matters. 当归 is the sweet, warm materia-medica root and sachet herb; 白芷 is the sharper aromatic root that appears in blended incense. A buyer who treats them as interchangeable buys the wrong material with the right word.

| | 当归 — dong quai | 白芷 — angelica root |
|---|---|---|
| Binomial | *Angelica sinensis* | *Angelica dahurica* |
| Root character | Fleshy, sweet, warm, earthy | Firmer, sharper, more resinous-aromatic |
| Dominant impression | Sweet root with a herbal edge | Bitter-sweet, pungent, slightly camphoraceous |
| Traditional standing | Materia-medica root; 女人要药 | Aromatic and materia-medica root with a strong smell |
| Incense role | Sachet herb; quiet depth note | Blended-incense (合香) aromatic, often a leading note |
| Typical failure in trade | Substituted with 白芷 or European angelica | Sold under the loose label "angelica root" |

The distinction matters because the two roots behave differently in a blend. 白芷 has the kind of loud, diffusive aroma that can carry a formula; 当归 has a rounded, low-sitting sweetness that supports. Swap one for the other and the formula does not simply change character — it changes structure, because the loud root takes over the opening while the quiet one is buried.

There is a second, subtler confusion inside the Chinese name system. 当归 is written with two characters that read as a phrase, not a description, so nothing in the name tells you what the plant looks like or where it grows. The English word "angelica," by contrast, is a genus-level label applied loosely across species on three continents. Neither name is a specification. Only the binomial is.

## What the name carries

**Direct answer:** 当归 reads as "ought to return," and the *Bencao Gangmu* preserves a story of a wife waiting for her husband's return as the explanation. The same text lists the older names 乾归, 山蕲, and 文无 — three aliases for one root, which is a sign of how long the material has been in the literature.

Most plant names describe something: a leaf shape, a habitat, a smell. 当归 does not. It reads as a small proverb, and the classical anecdote attached to it — a wife who waits, a husband who ought to come back — is the kind of story that gets told about a plant people already care about. Whether the story generated the name or the name attracted the story is not something a reader can settle from the text, and it does not matter for using the material. What matters is that the name is mnemonic, not botanical.

The alias cluster is more informative. 乾归, 山蕲, and 文无 are recorded alongside 当归 in the *Bencao Gangmu*'s 草之三 section, and a plant with several classical names is usually a plant that moved between regions and was renamed at each stop. Aliases accumulate around materials that are traded widely and used heavily. That is a historical signal, not a quality grade, but it explains why a modern buyer may meet the same root under three or four names in a single market.

Transliteration adds another layer. "Dong quai," "dang gui," "danggui," and the older "tang kuei" all render the same two characters. They are spelling variants, not different materials, and a specification that treats them as separate lines is creating confusion rather than precision.

## Why a medicine root sits in a scent archive

**Direct answer:** 当归 belongs here because it is a sachet herb with a real, if modest, aromatic role — not because it is burned. Its scent works by proximity in a cloth pouch, and its fame is medicinal rather than olfactory. Keeping those two facts separate is the honest way to describe the material.

An encyclopedia of incense has to draw a boundary somewhere, and this is one of the useful ones. 当归 is not a classical 香乘 material. It does not appear as a burned ingredient in the canonical formulas, and it is not a lead 香材 in any blending tradition. What it does have is a place in the 香囊 — the small cloth sachet of fragrant herbs worn on the body or hung in a room — where the root's warm sweetness is carried by air moving through cloth rather than by fire.

That role is real, and it is also limited. A sachet is a low-energy delivery system: no heat, no draught, just slow diffusion at room temperature. Materials that work in that setting are the ones with a persistent, heavy, easily released scent — roots, woods, resins, and a few dried flowers. 当归 fits because its aroma is dense and sweet rather than bright and fleeting.

The boundary matters for a second reason. Medicinal fame is a powerful marketing asset, and it is easy to let it slide into aromatic claims. This page does not do that. The traditional record is presented as tradition; the aromatic description is presented as smell. A reader who wants the medicine should read a materia-medica source. A reader who wants a warm root note in a sachet is in the right place.

## How a dried root releases scent at all

**Direct answer:** A dried root is a dense, low-porosity solid, so its aromatic compounds escape slowly and unevenly. Freshly cut or crushed surfaces release the most scent; intact surfaces release very little. That physical fact explains why a sachet needs agitation and why a whole root smells weaker than a sliced one.

The mechanism is straightforward once you picture the material. Aromatic molecules are stored in the root's tissue — in oil-bearing cells and ducts — and for the scent to reach your nose, those molecules have to travel out of the tissue, into the air, and to the receptor. In a dry root, that journey is slow.

Dried root → low moisture and compact tissue → volatiles diffuse slowly through the solid → only the outer layer contributes at rest → crushing or slicing exposes fresh tissue → a burst of aroma, then a faster decline.

That chain explains a set of everyday observations. A whole root kept in a drawer smells mild until you break it. A sliced root smells stronger in the packet because every cut face is a fresh surface. A powdered root smells strongest of all at first, and then fades fastest, because the same logic runs in reverse: more surface means more release now and less reserve later.

It also explains why root materials are usually described as base or middle notes rather than top notes. The top note is the fast, volatile fraction that arrives in seconds. Roots do not deliver that. They deliver a slow, steady presence that becomes noticeable over minutes and lingers in cloth.

## Building a sachet around a root

**Direct answer:** A sachet is a diffusion device, and its performance depends on three variables: how finely the root is divided, how tightly the cloth is woven, and how much air moves through the pouch. Get those three right and a small quantity of root will scent a drawer for a long time. Get them wrong and even good root seems inert.

Start with the material. Whole root releases scent slowly and needs to be broken or scored to wake up. Sliced root is the practical middle: enough cut surface to smell immediately, enough mass to last. Powder is the most intense at the start and the least durable, and it also sifts through loose cloth, which makes it a poor choice for a sachet unless the pouch is lined.

Then the cloth. A tight weave — silk, fine cotton, closely woven linen — slows the exchange of air between the pouch and the room. That sounds like a disadvantage, and in the first week it is: the scent arrives more slowly. Over months it becomes an advantage, because the aromatic reservoir drains more slowly. A loose, open weave gives a strong initial impression and a short life.

Then the fill. A sachet packed tight has little internal air movement, so the scent stays near the surface. A sachet filled loosely has more headspace and more exchange, so it perfumes a space faster and exhausts itself sooner. The practical compromise is a pouch that holds its shape without being compressed.

The daily-use chain runs like this: cloth and root sit in a humid room → both take up moisture → water competes with aromatic molecules for space in the fibre and on the root surface → the scent flattens → airing the pouch in dry shade and gently crushing the contents restores it for a while.

That last step is the one people skip. A sachet is not a candle; it does not need to be lit, but it does need to be disturbed. A squeeze once a week, a rest in dry air once a month, and the pouch keeps working far longer than one left untouched in a closed drawer.

## Grading a root you cannot lab-test

**Direct answer:** Without a laboratory, grade 当归 on four things: identity, root condition, aroma on fracture, and absence of off-notes. The order matters. A clean, well-dried root of the wrong species is still the wrong material, and a correctly identified root that smells of sulfur or damp is not a bargain.

| Check | Sound material | What failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Binomial stated as *Angelica sinensis*; supplier can name the species | "Angelica root" with no species; a sharp, pungent smell closer to 白芷 |
| Root body | Firm, dense, heavy for its size; bark intact | Light, spongy, or hollow; bark rubbed off; much broken scrap |
| Cut surface | Even slices, creamy to tan inside, concentric rings visible | Uneven thickness, dark or discoloured interior, dusty surface |
| Aroma | Sweet, warm, rooty on a fresh break; builds slowly | Flat and dusty, or a prickly sulfurous edge, or a musty basement note |
| Moisture | Dry to the touch, no clumping in the bag | Soft patches, condensation inside the packet, mould bloom |
| Foreign matter | Clean root; minimal soil and stem | Grit at the bottom of the bag, fibrous stem pieces, insect frass |

The aroma check deserves its own paragraph, because it is the only test most buyers can run. Break a piece rather than sniffing the outside. The exterior of a dried root has lost most of its volatile fraction; the interior is where the reservoir sits. A fresh break should give a sweet, warm, slightly spicy smell that grows over ten or twenty seconds rather than hitting all at once. A smell that arrives fast and sharp is more characteristic of 白芷 than of 当归 — which is exactly the substitution to catch.

Sulfur is the other off-note worth knowing. Some lots in the herb trade are fumigated, and the residue reads as a prickly, match-like sharpness that sits on top of the sweet root. It is not a scent you want in a sachet. A musty, cellar-like note is worse: it usually means the root was stored damp, and mould is a discard signal rather than a grading deduction.

## Forms, and what each one is for

**Direct answer:** 当归 moves as whole root, sliced root, and powder, and the right form follows the job. Whole root keeps longest and suits display and long storage; slices suit sachets and decanting; powder suits blending and immediate use. None of the three is a higher grade than the others.

| Form | Strength | Weakness | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole root | Slowest aroma loss; keeps identity visible | Needs breaking or scoring to release scent | Long storage, display, sachets refreshed by crushing |
| Sliced root | Good balance of surface and reserve; easy to portion | More surface means faster fade than whole | Sachets, small blends, sample evaluation |
| Powder | Immediate, intense aroma; easy to blend evenly | Fades fastest; sifts through cloth; hides identity | Blending into a paste or lined pouch; short-term use |

The trade-off is the same one that governs every aromatic material: surface area buys intensity and spends shelf life. A whole root is a savings account; a powder is cash in hand. The mistake is to buy powder for a sachet that will hang for a year, or to buy whole root for a blend that needs to be homogeneous by tomorrow.

There is a quality-signal argument for whole and sliced forms as well. A whole root can be inspected for species-typical shape, bark, and interior colour. A powder cannot — it is the form in which substitutions hide most easily. A buyer who cares about identity should buy the most intact form they can work with and do the milling themselves, or buy from a supplier whose specification names the species and the part.

## Storage, and the slow fade

**Direct answer:** 当归 keeps best whole, sealed, dark, and dry. The enemies are heat, light, oxygen, and moisture cycling, and the last one does the most quiet damage. A root stored well will hold its sweet note for a long time; a root stored in a warm kitchen cabinet will go flat without ever looking wrong.

The fade mechanism is worth spelling out: aromatic compounds at the root surface evaporate → the surface layer loses its charge → more compounds migrate outward from the interior → the cycle repeats → the root slowly equalises with its container → the scent flattens even though the root still looks sound.

Anything that speeds up the first step shortens the material's life. Heat does it. Light does it. A loose bag in a warm room does it. So does repeated opening of the container, because each opening exchanges saturated air for dry room air and pulls more volatiles out of the root.

The moisture cycle is the subtler problem. A root that picks up water in a humid week and loses it in a dry one is being physically worked, and each cycle can crack the tissue and expose new surface. That is why a sealed container with a stable, dry interior beats a breathable bag in a variable room. If the root does pick up moisture, dry it gently and thoroughly before resealing — a sealed jar around a damp root is a mould chamber.

Insect pressure is real for sweet roots, and it is the reason long storage deserves a periodic check rather than a one-time decision. Frass, webbing, and small exit holes mean the lot is finished. Freezing is sometimes used to interrupt an infestation, but a root taken from a freezer and opened warm will condense water on its surface, so any cold treatment needs a sealed, brought-to-room-temperature handover.

## Buying: a triage sequence

**Direct answer:** Buy 当归 by specification, not by photograph. Name the species, name the part, name the form, then judge the lot on break-aroma and condition. A seller who cannot name the binomial has not specified the material, whatever the label says.

The sequence below is the order that catches the most mistakes per unit of effort:

| Step | Question | Why it comes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Species | Is the specification *Angelica sinensis*? | Every later check is worthless if the plant is wrong |
| 2. Part | Is it the root — not leaf, stem, or a different root entirely? | "Angelica" alone does not name a plant part |
| 3. Form | Whole, sliced, or powder? | The form sets shelf life and how much identity you can verify |
| 4. Condition | Firm, dry, clean, free of mould and grit? | Condition decides whether the lot is usable at all |
| 5. Aroma | Sweet and warm on a fresh break, building slowly? | The only sensory test that distinguishes the species in hand |
| 6. Price logic | Is it priced as a sachet root rather than as a famous medicine? | Fame inflates price without improving the aroma |

Step six is the one buyers skip, and it is where money is lost. 当归 has a large medicinal reputation, and reputation is easy to charge for. The aromatic material is a root with a quiet, sweet smell; it is not a rare resin, and nothing about its scent justifies a luxury price. A lot priced on fame is being sold to a story, not to a nose.

Step one is the one buyers cannot fully verify from a packet, which is why the paper matters. A specification sheet that names the binomial and the plant part is doing real work. A supplier who answers "angelica root, from Gansu" has told you a province and an English genus word, and neither settles the species question.

In practice, the most useful thing a buyer can do is build a small reference set: one lot of confirmed 当归, one of confirmed 白芷, kept separately and smelled side by side until the difference is instant. The two are not subtle once you know them — 当归 is rounder and sweeter, 白芷 is sharper and more piercing — but the difference is hard to learn from descriptions alone.

## The traditional record, held at arm's length

**Direct answer:** Chinese materia medica gives 当归 a long and prominent record, and this page reports that record as tradition rather than as clinical fact. The documented, testable facts are botanical identity, plant family, and aromatic behaviour. The medicinal reputation is historical language and is labelled as such.

Three layers are worth separating, because they are routinely blended into one another:

| Layer | What it contains | How this page treats it |
|---|---|---|
| Historical record | The *Bencao Gangmu* 草之三 entry; the names 乾归, 山蕲, 文无; the 女人要药 epithet from the *Bencao Jingshu* tradition | Reported as documented tradition |
| Traditional belief | The root's reputation as a women's herb and its use in compound preparations | Labelled traditional belief; no efficacy claim |
| Botanical fact | *Angelica sinensis*, Apiaceae, perennial, cultivated in Gansu, Sichuan, Yunnan | Stated as established |

The epithet 女人要药 is the phrase that travels furthest, and it is also the phrase most often detached from its source. In the classical literature it is an evaluative statement within a medical framework — a claim about where the root sits in a system of correspondences — not a finding from a trial. A page that repeats it without that framing turns a historical judgement into a modern promise, which is exactly the sort of drift this archive exists to prevent.

There is a fair question underneath the caution: if the medicinal record is traditional, why mention it at all? Because the record is the reason the material is traded, named, and confused. A buyer who does not know that 当归 is famous as a medicine will not understand why it is expensive, why it is substituted, or why the aromatic description keeps getting inflated. The tradition is context. It is not evidence of efficacy, and it is not a selling point for a sachet.

## Where it sits beside other roots

**Direct answer:** 当归 is one of several aromatic roots and rhizomes in the Chinese material vocabulary, and it is the quietest of the group. 白芷 is sharper, 川芎 is more pungent and penetrating, atractylodes is drier and more resinous, calamus is sweeter and woodier. Position in that set explains why 当归 works as a supporting note rather than a lead.

| Material | Plant part | Family | Aromatic character | Typical role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 当归 *Angelica sinensis* | Root | Apiaceae | Warm, sweet, rooty, mildly spicy | Sachet depth note |
| 白芷 *Angelica dahurica* | Root | Apiaceae | Sharp, pungent, bitter-sweet | Leading 合香 aromatic |
| 川芎 *Ligusticum chuanxiong* | Rhizome | Apiaceae | Pungent, penetrating, slightly camphoraceous | Accent in blends |
| Atractylodes (苍术/白术) | Rhizome | Asteraceae | Dry, resinous, bitter-woody | Burned aromatic in tradition |
| Calamus (菖蒲) | Rhizome | Acoraceae | Sweet, woody, slightly spicy | Blends and sachets |

Two things stand out in that table. The first is that 当归 is the only material listed whose primary use is a sachet rather than a burn. The second is that three of the five are umbellifers, which is why the family keeps producing identification problems: the roots look alike dried, and the names overlap in translation.

A useful way to hold the group together is by energy. 白芷 and 川芎 hit the nose fast and fade; atractylodes and calamus sit in the middle; 当归 arrives last and stays longest. In a formula, that ordering maps onto the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) vocabulary: the fast, loud materials take the chief or minister slots, and the slow, quiet ones take assistant or guide work. Treat that mapping as editorial judgment rather than a rule from the classics — the classical system was built for formulas with many more components than a modern sachet, and applying it to a two-material pouch is an analogy, not a doctrine.

## Sachet or coal: the format decides

**Direct answer:** A root in a pouch releases scent by evaporation at room temperature; the same root on a burning coal is decomposed by heat and contributes smoke. The two are different physical events, so a material that pleases in a sachet can disappoint when burned, and the reverse also happens. Format is a decision about mechanism, not about status.

The chain on a coal runs roughly like this: heat reaches the root → tissue pyrolyses before the aromatic oil can distil cleanly → sugars and fibres scorch → sharp, tarry, or acrid notes form → the fine sweet fraction is burned away at the front → what reaches the nose is mostly smoke chemistry rather than root chemistry.

A sachet runs a gentler chain: room air passes through cloth → the warmest, most volatile molecules leave the root surface → they diffuse into the air inside the pouch → that saturated air seeps out through the weave → the room receives a thin, continuous stream of the root's lighter notes.

That is why sachet materials are chosen for persistence and burn materials for structure. A root that smells sweet and rounded in a pouch may reveal a bitter, scorched edge on a coal, because the heat is now generating compounds the pouch never produced. A resin that smells flat and heavy in a pouch may open into something complex on a coal, because heat is doing the work that proximity could not.

The middle path is a warm plate — an electric heater that lifts the material to a modest temperature without ignition. It sits between the two mechanisms: more energy than a sachet, no combustion. For a root like 当归, that is the only heated format worth trying, and even there the gain is small. The honest summary is that this material belongs in cloth, and forcing it into a burner asks it to do a job its physics does not support.

## Reading a supplier's specification, line by line

**Direct answer:** A useful specification answers five questions in writing: species, plant part, origin, form, and processing. Each missing answer shifts risk onto the buyer. The words to distrust are the ones that sound specific but are not — a province is not a species, and "natural" is not a process.

| Field | What a usable line says | What a vague line says | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Species | *Angelica sinensis*, named in full | "Angelica root" or "dong quai type" | The genus word covers several roots with different chemistry |
| Part | Root, with any trimming described | "Root/whole herb" or no part at all | Leaf and stem carry a different, greener scent |
| Origin | Province or district of cultivation | "China" | Growing region shapes root size and aroma, and it is the first thing trade buyers compare |
| Form | Whole, sliced to a stated thickness, or milled | "Cut" or "processed" | Form sets shelf life and how much identity remains checkable |
| Processing | Dried; any fumigation or treatment declared | "Natural" or "traditional method" | Undeclared fumigation leaves a sharp residue that ruins a sachet |
| Lot | A lot or batch reference that ties the packet to the paperwork | No reference | Without it, a complaint has nothing to attach to |

A specification is not a certificate, and a well-written sheet does not prove the contents match it. What it does do is make the claim falsifiable. If the sheet says *Angelica sinensis* root from Gansu in whole form and the packet arrives as pale powder with a piercing smell, the buyer has something concrete to reject against. If the sheet says "angelica root, natural," there is nothing to test and nothing to argue.

For a buyer working without a laboratory, the specification plus the break-aroma test is the whole toolkit. That is a thin toolkit, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. What it can do is catch the common failures: the wrong species, the wrong part, a fumigated lot, and a root that has been sitting long enough to go flat.

## A working vocabulary for the scent

**Direct answer:** Descriptions of 当归 tend to pile up adjectives without saying what they point at. A small, consistent vocabulary is more useful: sweet, balsamic, earthy, rooty, herbaceous, and spicy, each tied to a distinct impression rather than used as a synonym for "nice."

| Term | What it describes | How it shows up in 当归 |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet | A sugared, rounded impression without acidity | The first thing most people notice; fuller than in 白芷 |
| Balsamic | A soft, resin-like warmth with a faint vanilla edge | Present in the background, more noticeable in older root |
| Earthy | The smell of dry soil or a cellar floor | Strong in whole root, weaker in powder that has aired |
| Rooty | The specific raw smell of a freshly broken root | Clearest on a fresh break, which is why the break test matters |
| Herbaceous | A green, leafy note | Light; it keeps the sweetness from turning syrupy |
| Spicy | A warm prickle at the back of the nose | Mild here, and much sharper in 白芷 |

Two cautions about this vocabulary. The first is that words describe impressions, not molecules, and two people can use "balsamic" for different sensations. The second is that the same root smells different depending on form and temperature: cold root is quieter, a warm room lifts the sweet notes, and powder releases everything at once.

The practical use of the vocabulary is comparison. Smell a confirmed 当归, then a confirmed 白芷, and assign each term a position on a scale from absent to dominant. The exercise takes ten minutes and does more for a buyer's judgement than any number of adjective lists, because it converts a vague impression into a repeatable observation.

## A build sequence for a sachet

**Direct answer:** A sachet is assembled in six steps, and each one has a failure mode. The sequence matters more than the recipe, because a good root in a poor pouch performs worse than an ordinary root in a well-made one.

1. **Pick the pouch for the audience.** A pouch worn against the body needs a tight weave and a smooth seam; a pouch hung in a wardrobe can be looser. Tight weaves release slowly and last; loose weaves release fast and fade.
2. **Decide the form before you buy.** Sliced root is the default. Whole root needs breaking, and powder needs a lining to stop it sifting through the seam.
3. **Break the root coarsely.** A mortar or the back of a spoon is enough. The aim is fractured pieces with fresh faces, not dust — dust releases everything in the first week.
4. **Fill with headroom.** A pouch should hold its shape, not feel like a stone. Compressed contents have little internal air movement, so the scent stays locked at the surface.
5. **Label the pouch.** Species, form, and the date it was filled. In a drawer of similar pouches, the label is the only thing that preserves identity.
6. **Rest and refresh.** Air the pouch in dry shade once a month, crush the contents gently, and reseal. Disturbance is what keeps a passive device working.

Step five is the one most often skipped, and it is the one that matters most over time. A sachet without a date becomes an object of guesswork within a season; a sachet with a date tells you when to replace the filling rather than waiting for the smell to fail.

One misconception is worth correcting here. More root does not mean a stronger sachet. A pouch stuffed full has less air space, less exchange through the cloth, and a harder, denser mass that releases less readily than a loosely filled one. The scent comes from the surface, and the surface is what a full pouch reduces.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** 当归 used in a sachet carries the ordinary risks of a dried botanical: dust, mould if stored damp, and possible skin sensitivity in a small number of people. Burning any incense adds smoke and particulate matter, which is a separate and larger concern. Treat this as reference material, not clinical guidance.

Practical points:

- **Do not burn 当归 as a standalone incense.** It is not a burn material in the tradition, and a dense root does not smoulder well; the result is more likely to be acrid smoke than scent.
- **Keep sachets out of reach of children and pets.** A cloth pouch of dried root is a chewable object, and the contents are not food.
- **Watch for mould.** A musty smell, a bloom on the root, or condensation inside the pouch means discard the contents and wash or replace the pouch.
- **Ventilate any space where incense is burned.** Combustion produces smoke and fine particles regardless of the material, and concentration is the variable that matters most.
- **Patch-test a new sachet against bare skin.** A small number of people react to plant dust; a sachet worn against the skin is a different exposure from one hung in a room.

If you are pregnant, nursing, taking medication, or managing a health condition, treat any botanical with the caution you would apply to a new food or supplement, and ask a clinician rather than an incense page. Nothing here is a recommendation for internal use, and nothing here is clinical advice.

See the site's [safety guide](/safety/) for the general rules on smoke, ventilation, and combustion.

## FAQ

**What is dong quai (当归)?** A Chinese umbellifer grown for its thick, aromatic root, which is dried and used whole, sliced, or milled. The plant is *Angelica sinensis*, a perennial of the family Apiaceae cultivated mainly in Gansu, Sichuan, and Yunnan. The *Bencao Gangmu* files it in 草之三 under three older names — 乾归, 山蕲, 文无 — and the materia-medica tradition calls it 女人要药.

**Is dong quai the same as angelica root (白芷)?** Not the same plant. The two sit in one genus but not in one species: 当归 is *Angelica sinensis*, while 白芷 is *Angelica dahurica*. The first is a sweet, fleshy root used in sachets and materia medica; the second is sharper and more pungent, and it is the one that appears in blended incense. English collapses both under a single label, and that label is where the confusion starts.

**Is dong quai the same as European garden angelica?** Different species, not a synonym. *Angelica archangelica* is a larger, sweeter, more resinous plant with its own culinary history. It is a relative of 当归, and it should not be substituted without saying so on the specification.

**What does dong quai smell like?** Warm, sweet, and herbal, with a rooty, faintly spicy undertone and a dry rather than resinous feel. The scent is quiet at rest and builds when the root is broken or crushed. Compared with 白芷 it is rounder and less piercing.

**Can dong quai be burned as incense?** Not sensibly. It is a sachet herb, not a classical 香乘 material, and a dense dried root does not smoulder well. Burning it tends to produce acrid smoke rather than a clean scent. Its aromatic role is proximity, not combustion.

**How do I choose between whole, sliced, and powdered root?** Match the form to the job and the storage time. Whole root keeps longest and lets you inspect identity; slices are the practical sachet form; powder gives the strongest immediate scent and the shortest shelf life, and it hides substitutions most easily.

**Does the 女人要药 epithet mean the root is a medicine?** It means the classical literature classifies it that way within a traditional framework. That is a historical claim, not a modern clinical finding, and this page makes no medical claim for the material. The 女人要药 phrase is reported here as traditional belief.

**Why is dong quai expensive if its scent is modest?** Because price follows reputation, and the root's reputation is medicinal. A buyer paying a premium for 当归 as an aromatic material is paying for fame rather than for scent. Judge the lot on break-aroma and condition, and price it as a sachet root.

## Key takeaways

- 当归 is *Angelica sinensis*, a perennial umbellifer cultivated in Gansu, Sichuan, and Yunnan; the dried root is the material.
- Its classical identity is documented — *Bencao Gangmu* 草之三, names 乾归/山蕲/文无, the 女人要药 epithet — and that record is tradition, not clinical evidence.
- Its aromatic life is spent in a cloth pouch rather than on a coal, and the classical 香乘 formulas do not count it among their burned materials.
- 白芷 (*Angelica dahurica*) supplies the *Angelica* note in 合香; sharing a genus has not made the two roots interchangeable.
- A dried root releases scent slowly, so sachet performance depends on division, cloth weave, and air movement as much as on the root itself.
- Grade on identity first, then condition and break-aroma; sulfur, mustiness, and mould are discard signals.
- Whole root keeps longest, slices are the practical middle, powder is the most intense and the shortest-lived.

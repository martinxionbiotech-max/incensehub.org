**Direct answer:** The spice called 八角 (bājiǎo) is the dried fruit of *Illicium verum*. The tree is evergreen and is grown across southern China and northern Vietnam. The fruit is shaped like an eight-pointed star, smells of licorice and warmth, and belongs to the kitchen far more than to the classical incense bench. Those two facts — a powerful sweet spice and a latecomer to the incense formula — explain most of how it is bought, dosed, and confused.

## Key facts

**Direct answer:** A cultivated tree fruit, an anethole-rich sweet spice, a culinary cornerstone, and a material with one genuinely dangerous look-alike. Its place in the classical canon is absent.

## The star is a fruit

**Direct answer:** The eight points are not petals. Each point is a follicle — a small seed pod — arranged around a centre, and the whole cluster is the fruit. The shape is a botanical structure, not a decorative accident.

A star anise looks engineered. Eight woody arms radiate from a central hub, each arm splitting along one side to release a glossy brown seed. The symmetry is regular enough that the fruit is used as a visual motif in its own right, printed on packaging and carved into kitchen tools. The regularity is also a useful trade signal: a whole, unbroken star is easier to grade by eye than a bag of fragments.

The fruit is picked when mature and dried whole. Drying preserves the shape and, more importantly, the aromatic oil held in the pericarp — the woody wall of each follicle. The Chinese name is a counting name: 八角 means "eight corners," a description of the symmetry rather than a botanical term. A star with fewer points is not a different species; it is an irregular fruit. A whole star dries slowly and evenly; a broken star dries faster and loses more of its volatile fraction. That is why whole and broken fruit are not the same product, even when they come from the same tree.

The seeds inside carry their own aroma, but the pericarp does most of the work in the kitchen and in a blend. A star that has lost its seeds still smells; a star that has lost its oil does not. The distinction matters when buying: a bag of handsome but hollow stars can be visually impressive and aromatically dead.

## One molecule, three plants

**Direct answer:** Star anise, fennel, and anise smell alike because they all carry anethole. They are unrelated plants that converged on the same molecule, and the shared 茴 name in Chinese records that convergence.

Anethole is the reason a licorice note appears in three different botanical families. It is a small aromatic compound with a pronounced sweet, anise-like character, and it dominates the oil of all three materials. A nose that has learned anethole can recognise it instantly; a nose that has not may describe it as "black licorice," "fennel," or "that sweet smell in cough syrup."

The plants around the molecule are different. Star anise is a tree fruit in Schisandraceae. Fennel is a carrot-family herb grown for its seed-like fruit. Anise is another carrot-family herb, smaller and Mediterranean. Their shared chemistry is real; their shared ancestry is not.

| Material | Species and family | Part | Aroma beside anethole | Classical incense role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Star anise (八角) | *Illicium verum*, Schisandraceae | Star-shaped tree fruit | Warmer, spicier, slightly woody | None recorded; modern crossover |
| Fennel (小茴香) | *Foeniculum vulgare*, Apiaceae | Seed-like fruit | Greener, softer, more herbal | A supporting note in some 合香 |
| Anise (茴芹) | *Pimpinella anisum*, Apiaceae | Seed-like fruit | Cleaner, sharper, more one-dimensional | None as a classical 香材 |

The differences are small in isolation and large in a blend. Fennel's green edge makes it read like a herb; star anise's warmth makes it read like a spice; anise's cleanliness makes it read like a flavouring. Put star anise where fennel belongs and the formula gains a food note it did not ask for. Put fennel where star anise belongs and the formula loses the warmth that made the idea work.

## Why the old manuals are silent

**Direct answer:** The Song and Ming fragrance manuals do not list star anise among incense materials. That silence is a fact about the canon, not a ban on the spice, and it does not prove that no household ever burned it.

The classical Chinese incense manuals are curated documents. They record what a tradition selected, tested, and valued — the materials that belonged to a recognised practice. Star anise was a southern trade fruit and a kitchen spice. Its route into the fragrance world came later, through the culinary prestige of five-spice and the modern habit of blending sweet spices into incense.

That is an editorial reading of the evidence, not a documented history of every workshop. A manual's silence can mean several things: the material was unknown to the compiler, it was considered outside the tradition, it was too common to mention, or it simply did not occur to anyone to burn it. The honest statement is narrower: star anise has no recorded classical incense role, and its modern use is a crossover.

The distinction matters for buyers. A blend marketed as "traditionally classical" that leans on star anise is using the word "traditional" loosely. A blend marketed as "modern sweet-spice" and built around star anise is doing exactly what it says. The problem is not the spice; it is the borrowed pedigree.

## The toxic twin

**Direct answer:** Two fruits answer to the name. One is the edible spice, *Illicium verum*. The other is *Illicium anisatum*, the Japanese species, which carries toxic compounds and has no place in food or in a burner. The fruits look close enough that appearance alone cannot separate them.

Of all the facts about this spice, the species question is the one with real consequences, and it should be stated without softening. *Illicium anisatum* — also known as *I. religiosum* — contains toxic compounds, among them anisatin, and its fruit has appeared in adulteration cases. It is not a milder version of the spice. It is another species with another safety profile.

The difficult part is that appearance alone is unreliable. The two fruits are similar in size, colour, and star shape, and a broken or processed lot removes much of the evidence a visual check would use. A buyer who sorts by eye is not running a species test. That is why the species must be pinned on the paperwork and, where doubt remains, by a competent botanical identification rather than by a confident glance.

| Question | *Illicium verum* | *Illicium anisatum* |
|---|---|---|
| Common name | Chinese star anise, 八角 | Japanese star anise |
| Status | Edible culinary spice | Toxic; not for food or fragrance |
| Fruit | Eight-pointed, aromatic | Similar star shape; toxic chemistry |
| Smell | Sweet, licorice, warm | Similar aromatic family; not a safety guide |
| Adulteration risk | The target of substitution | The substituted material |
| What to do | Confirm species and source | Reject; do not eat or burn |

The practical rule is simple and absolute: if the species is not confirmed, do not use the material where it will be eaten or inhaled. A cheap lot with no species documentation is not a bargain. It is an unidentified botanical.

## What it does in a blend

**Direct answer:** Star anise adds a warm, food-like sweetness at the top and heart of a blend. It is potent, it reads as culinary, and it can overwhelm a formula in amounts that look modest on the scale.

The first thing a formulator notices is lift. Anethole is volatile and intense; a small amount reaches the nose quickly and announces itself. That makes star anise useful for opening a blend that would otherwise start flat. It also makes it easy to overdo. The difference between "a warm sweetness in the background" and "this smells like braised pork" is often a few tenths of a percent.

The second thing is the food association. Star anise is inseparable from Chinese braising and five-spice, and the nose knows it. In a modern gourmand or spice-forward composition, that association is an asset. In a classical wood-and-resin composition, it is a foreign accent. Neither result is wrong; the question is whether the formula wants the association.

The third thing is interaction. Star anise sits naturally beside cinnamon and clove, and the three together can produce a mulled-wine warmth that overwhelms lighter materials. It also has a way of flattening floral notes: the anethole sweetness covers the top of a rose or osmanthus and makes the blend read simpler than it is. The fix is usually to reduce the spice, not to add more flowers.

**Chain:** fruit warmed → anethole volatilizes → sweet licorice top → warmth spreads through the heart → a food-like base note settles → lighter florals are masked if the dose is too high.

## Forms, and what they hide

**Direct answer:** Whole stars are the easiest form to inspect and the slowest to lose aroma. Broken pieces and powder are more convenient and more perishable. Oil is a different product with a different strength.

| Form | What it is good for | What it hides | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole stars | Visual grading, culinary use, long storage | Hollow or seedless fruit | Looking good and smelling old |
| Broken stars | Bulk blending, easier packing | The proportion of husk to seed | Uneven aroma; dust |
| Powder | Spice mixes, incense blending | Species and freshness | Caking, rapid aroma loss |
| Distilled oil | Flavour and fragrance work | Dilution and origin | Confusing oil strength with fruit strength |

A whole star and a spoon of powder do not smell the same in the jar. The whole fruit releases slowly, so the jar smells restrained; the powder releases at once, so the jar smells strong. That difference disappears when both are heated, and it reverses in storage, where the powder fades first. A whole star is not automatically better than a broken one. It is simply easier to see. The whole form lets a buyer check the colour, the integrity of the points, and the presence of seeds. The broken form removes that evidence and replaces it with convenience. Powder removes almost all of it and adds a freshness clock.

Oil is a separate economy. Steam distillation concentrates the volatile fraction, and the resulting product is far stronger than the fruit. A formulator who treats oil as a drop-in replacement for powder will overshoot the dose by a wide margin. The two are related; they are not interchangeable.

## Quality: what you can see and smell

**Direct answer:** Good star anise is whole, heavy for its size, intensely aromatic, and free of debris. The decisive test is the smell, and the decisive safety check is the species — not the appearance.

Start with the nose. Crush a point between the fingers and smell it. A fresh star releases a strong, sweet, licorice-like aroma with a warm spicy edge. A stale star smells dusty, woody, or faintly of cardboard. The difference is obvious once you have a reference.

Then look at the fruit. The points should be intact and the colour should be a warm reddish-brown, not faded grey. A light star with thin points may be mostly husk; a heavy star with plump follicles carries more oil. Broken pieces and loose seeds in the bag are a sign of handling damage, not necessarily a defect in the material, but they shorten its useful life.

Then check for the things that should not be there: grit, mould, insect damage, and other plant material. Star anise is dried in the open in some supply chains, so a visual check is basic hygiene.

| Marker | Good sign | Warning sign | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aroma when crushed | Strong sweet licorice | Dusty, flat, cardboard | Species identity |
| Fruit integrity | Whole, plump points | Broken, hollow, faded | That the lot is fresh |
| Weight for size | Heavy, dense | Light, papery | That the oil content is high |
| Debris | Clean | Grit, mould, insects | That storage has been dry |
| Paperwork | Species and origin named | "Star anise" only | That the bag matches the paper |

The last row is the important one. Star anise is a case where the visual check and the safety check diverge: a lot can look perfect and be the wrong species. The paperwork and, where needed, a proper identification are the only reliable answers.

## From tree to drying floor

**Direct answer:** The fruit is picked mature, dried whole, sorted, and either sold as stars or ground and distilled. The process is short, and most of the quality is decided before the fruit leaves the tree.

Harvest is selective. The fruit is picked when it has reached its mature colour and size, then spread to dry. Sun drying is traditional; controlled drying is used where humidity or volume demands it. The aim is to bring the water content down without cooking the volatile oil. A slow, even dry keeps the colour and the aroma; a hot, fast dry can fade both.

After drying, the fruit is sorted. Whole, well-formed stars go one way; broken pieces and loose seeds go another. The sorting is partly cosmetic and partly functional: whole stars store better and present better, while broken material is cheaper and harder to keep fresh.

Grinding turns the dried fruit into powder for spice blends and incense. Distillation yields an oil rich in anethole, which is sold into flavour and fragrance work. The same fruit is a feedstock for shikimic acid, which goes into pharmaceutical manufacturing. One material, three lives: kitchen, perfume, and factory.

**Chain:** mature fruit → picked → dried whole → water falls → volatile oil retained → sorted by integrity → ground or distilled → aroma concentrated or dispersed.

The step that most affects a buyer is the dry. Fruit dried too fast can case-harden, leaving a moist centre that moulds in storage. Fruit dried too slowly in humid conditions can develop off-notes before it is packed. Neither fault is visible from the outside of a whole star.

## Five-spice is not a fragrance formula

**Direct answer:** Five-spice is a culinary balance built around warmth, sweetness, and savoury food. An incense blend is a scent structure built around volatility and dry-down. The same materials can appear in both, but the logic is different.

The usual five-spice cupboard set — star anise, cassia or cinnamon, clove, fennel, and Sichuan pepper — is designed for meat, fat, and broth. The spices are cooked into food, where heat, water, and fat extract and transform them. The balance is tuned to taste, not to the order in which aromas evaporate.

An incense blend is smelled rather than eaten, and it is heated rather than boiled. The materials release in sequence, and the formula has to work at the top, the heart, and the base. A five-spice ratio that tastes balanced in a braise can smell harsh or muddy in a stick, because the clove and star anise arrive together and the fennel has nowhere to go.

That does not make culinary ratios useless. It makes them a starting point rather than a recipe. A formulator who wants a five-spice character in incense has to rebalance for the burner: less clove, a gentler star anise, and a base that can carry the warmth without turning the whole stick into a kitchen.

## Sourcing and storage

**Direct answer:** Star anise is cultivated, so supply is stable and the conservation question is minor. The sourcing question is species, and the storage question is moisture and volatility.

The tree is grown rather than wild-collected, chiefly in southern China and northern Vietnam. Plantations and village plantings supply the market, and the fruit is harvested on a seasonal cycle. That makes star anise one of the easier aromatics to source in volume — the difficulty is not scarcity but verification.

Species verification is the first job. Because *I. verum* and *I. anisatum* are so similar, a buyer should insist on a species name and a known origin, and should treat an unlabelled bulk lot with suspicion. A supplier who cannot name the species is not a supplier of a food-grade spice; they are a supplier of an unidentified fruit.

Storage is the second job. Whole stars keep well in a sealed container away from light and heat. Powder loses aroma quickly and should be bought in quantities that will be used. Moisture is the enemy of both: a damp star moulds, and a damp powder cakes. The [incense storage guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) covers the general principles.

## A fruit that travels

**Direct answer:** The fruit comes from a tree that is planted rather than gathered, and it dries, stores, and ships well when kept whole. The whole star protects the seed and slows the loss of oil, which is why the trade prefers it intact.

The tree is grown rather than gathered. Orchards and village plantings in southern China and northern Vietnam supply the market, and the fruit is picked on a seasonal cycle when it reaches its mature colour. Picking is selective: immature fruit is left to fill out, and overripe fruit is left too long at its peril. The window is narrow enough that harvest is a skilled job rather than a mechanical pass.

Drying follows quickly. The fruit is spread in thin layers so that air can move around each star, and it is turned during the dry so that moisture leaves evenly. A slow dry keeps the colour and the volatile oil; a fast, hot dry can fade both. The difference is not visible on the day of packing. It shows up months later, when one lot still smells sweet and another smells like old wood.

Sorting comes next. Whole stars are separated from broken pieces and loose seeds, and the whole fruit is graded by size and appearance. The broken material is not waste; it goes into powder and into industrial extraction. But it is a different product with a different storage life, and a buyer who pays whole-fruit prices for a bag of fragments has been sold the wrong grade.

**Chain:** fruit picked → spread to dry → water leaves slowly → volatile oil retained → whole stars sorted → packed → shipped → aroma checked at destination.

The travel stage is kinder to whole fruit than to powder. A whole star is a small package with a woody shell; it resists compression, sheds dust, and keeps its oil away from the air. A powder is all surface, and every month in a warehouse costs it aroma. That is why the trade moves whole fruit long distances and grinds close to the point of use.

## The kitchen and the burner

**Direct answer:** In cooking, star anise is extracted by water, fat, and heat. In incense, it is released by dry heat into air. The same spice behaves differently because the extraction is different.

A braise is a wet, fatty, slow environment. Water carries flavour, fat dissolves aromatic compounds, and hours of gentle heat transform the spice into something rounder and deeper than the raw fruit. The star anise in a braise is not the star anise in the jar. It has been cooked into a system, and its sharp anethole edge has been softened by fat and time.

An incense stick is a dry system. The spice is heated in air, and there is no water or fat to round it. Anethole volatilizes early and intensely, and the food association arrives with it. The result can be pleasant — a warm sweetness at the top of a blend — or it can read as a kitchen note in a context that wanted something else.

| Question | Kitchen | Incense |
|---|---|---|
| Extraction medium | Water, fat, steam | Dry heat, air |
| Time | Minutes to hours | Seconds to minutes |
| Effect on anethole | Rounded by fat and water | Sharp, volatile, immediate |
| Role of the spice | Build a savoury, warming base | Add a sweet-spice accent |
| Failure mode | Bitterness from over-reduction | Cloying or food-like dominance |

What translates between the two is the idea of warmth. What does not translate is the roundness that fat and water provide. A formulator who wants the braise effect has to build it from other materials — a resin for body, a wood for depth — because star anise alone will give the top note without the cushion.

## Dose, or the tyranny of anethole

**Direct answer:** Star anise is potent, and potency is not the same as quality. A small amount adds warmth; a larger amount flattens the blend and makes everything smell like five-spice.

Anethole is a loud molecule. It reaches the nose quickly, it persists, and it masks quieter materials. In a blend with delicate florals, a dose that seems modest on the scale can cover the flowers entirely. The result is not "more spice"; it is "less of everything else."

Three scenarios are worth separating. In a sweet-spice blend built around cinnamon and clove, star anise is at home; the three share a warm register, and the star adds a licorice edge that the others lack. In a floral blend, it is a guest at best; a trace can add warmth, but the dose has to be small enough that the flowers still speak. In a wood-and-resin blend, it is a foreign accent; it can brighten the opening, but it can also make a classical composition smell like a modern experiment.

**Chain:** dose rises → anethole dominates the opening → quieter top notes are masked → the blend flattens into a single warm note → the cook recognizes braise, not perfume.

The practical rule is to add star anise last and in steps. Taste the blend before and after; if the change is "warmer" the dose is working, and if the change is "everything tastes like star anise" it has gone too far. The boundary is not a number. It is the point where the other materials stop contributing.

## Reading a label

**Direct answer:** A useful star anise label names the species, the origin, the form, and the crop or lot. A label that says only "star anise" has answered the easiest question and skipped the important ones.

Species is first because of the toxic twin. *Illicium verum* is the edible species; *I. anisatum* is not. A label that does not name the species is not a food-grade specification, and a buyer should treat it accordingly.

Origin is second. Star anise comes from southern China and northern Vietnam, and regional differences in climate and drying practice affect the fruit. A named region is a traceability handle; a country is a start; "imported" is nothing.

Form is third. Whole, broken, powder, and oil are different products with different storage lives and different uses. A label that does not distinguish them is asking the buyer to guess.

| Label field | Why it matters | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Species | Separates the edible fruit from the toxic look-alike | That the bag matches the paper |
| Origin | Narrows the supply chain and the drying practice | That the lot is fresh |
| Form | Predicts storage life and behaviour | That the form suits your process |
| Crop or lot | Lets you compare one delivery with the next | That the next lot will match |
| Grade | A seller's sorting language | That the grade is standardized |

A grade name is the least useful field and the most heavily marketed. "Premium," "AAA," and "selected" are in-house words. They may reflect a real sorting standard, or they may reflect a good photographer. The species, origin, and form are the fields that change what the material does.

## What "warm" means

**Direct answer:** Warm is a sensory direction, not a temperature. Star anise, cinnamon, and clove are all warm, and they are warm in different ways.

Cinnamon is warm and woody, with a sweetness that sits close to bark. Clove is warm and sharp, with a numbing edge that can dominate a blend. Star anise is warm and sweet, with a licorice centre and a slightly cooling afternote. Put the three together and you get the familiar mulled-spice chord; separate them and the differences are obvious.

The word "warm" is useful because it describes a direction that most noses recognize without training. It is not useful as a specification, because it covers materials with very different chemistry and very different behaviour in heat. A formulator who asks for "something warm" may receive cinnamon, clove, star anise, ginger, or a resin, and each will take the blend somewhere different.

The practical move is to pair the direction with a texture. "Warm and dry" points toward cinnamon bark; "warm and sharp" toward clove; "warm and sweet" toward star anise. The added word narrows the field without pretending to be a grade.

## A spice with an industrial life

**Direct answer:** Star anise is not only a kitchen spice. It is distilled for oil and extracted for shikimic acid, and those industrial uses shape the supply that reaches the incense trade.

Steam distillation turns the fruit into star anise oil, an anethole-rich concentrate sold into flavour and fragrance work. The oil is far stronger than the fruit, and it is sold into a different market with different specifications. A perfume house buying oil is not buying the same thing as a cook buying whole stars, even though both trace to the same tree.

The same fruit is a raw material for shikimic acid, which is extracted for pharmaceutical synthesis. That demand creates a competing channel for the raw material and can affect what is available to food and fragrance buyers. It also means that "star anise" as a commodity is not one market; it is several markets overlapping on the same fruit.

For the incense buyer, the practical implication is that quality signals from the culinary market do not automatically transfer. A fruit selected for oil content may not be the best-looking star; a fruit selected for appearance may not be the most aromatic. The question is always "selected for what?"

## Star anise in modern fragrance

**Direct answer:** Modern perfumery uses star anise as a sweet-spice accent, often beside vanilla, tonka, or cinnamon. It reads as warm and edible, which is an asset in some compositions and a limitation in others.

The gourmand turn in late-twentieth-century perfumery made edible notes respectable. Star anise fitted the mood: it was sweet, warm, and familiar from the kitchen, and it could be abstracted into a perfume without smelling literally like a braise. A trace of anethole beside vanilla creates a soft, spicy sweetness that reads as comfort rather than food.

The limitation is the same as the asset. Anethole is unmistakable. In a composition that wants to be abstract, the spice can pull the whole thing toward the kitchen. Perfumers manage this by pairing it with materials that change its context: a dry wood, a bitter citrus peel, a resin that adds depth without adding sweetness. The spice is still there, but it is no longer the headline.

There is also a technical reason to use the oil rather than the fruit. Perfumery works in alcohol and oils, where a distilled extract disperses cleanly and a powdered fruit does not. The oil gives control; the powder gives texture and a different, more rustic effect. The choice depends on the medium, not on which form is "better."

For an incense maker, the lesson is that star anise is a colour, not a foundation. It tints a blend warm and sweet; it does not build the structure. A formula that leans on it for structure will smell like a spice rack, no matter how carefully the other materials are chosen.

## The five-spice problem

**Direct answer:** Five-spice is a family of recipes, not a fixed formula. Using it as a shortcut for incense fails because the balance is built for cooking, not for burning.

Walk into five kitchens and you will find five five-spice mixes. The constants are warmth and aroma: star anise, cassia or cinnamon, clove, fennel, and Sichuan pepper are the usual cast, but the proportions shift with region, family, and the dish. Some mixes lean sweet, some lean peppery, some add ginger or liquorice. There is no single canonical ratio, and a claim to one is a marketing story.

The blend works in cooking because it is cooked. Fat carries the aromatics, water rounds the edges, and long heat integrates the spices into a single savoury warmth. The cook tastes and adjusts; the mixture is a starting point, not a precision instrument.

Incense does not cook. The materials are heated dry, and they release in sequence rather than integrating into a sauce. A five-spice ratio that tastes balanced in a braise can smell harsh in a stick, because the clove arrives sharp and the star anise arrives sweet and the two do not have fat to soften them. The formulator has to rebuild the balance for the burner.

That is why "five-spice incense" is usually a concept rather than a recipe. The idea can work — warmth, sweetness, a peppery edge — but only if the materials are re-dosed for dry heat. Copying a kitchen ratio is the most common way to get a stick that smells like a pantry.

## A buyer's question list

**Direct answer:** Ten questions separate a real supplier from a label. The answers do not need to be polished; they need to be specific.

1. **Which species is this?** A good answer names *Illicium verum*. A weak answer says "star anise."
2. **Where was it grown?** A good answer names a province or district. A weak answer says "China."
3. **When was it harvested?** A good answer gives a season or a crop year. A weak answer says "fresh."
4. **How was it dried?** A good answer describes sun or controlled drying. A weak answer says "naturally."
5. **Is it whole fruit or broken?** A good answer separates the grades. A weak answer says "mixed."
6. **Has it been tested for species?** A good answer describes the check. A weak answer says "trust me."
7. **How has it been stored?** A good answer describes cool, dry, sealed storage. A weak answer says "in the warehouse."
8. **What is the lot number?** A good answer has one. A weak answer changes the subject.
9. **Can I see a sample?** A good answer sends one. A weak answer sends a photo.
10. **Will the next lot match?** A good answer talks about consistency. A weak answer promises perfection.

The list is deliberately plain. The point is not to interrogate a supplier but to hear whether the answers are specific. A supplier with a real chain can answer most of these without effort. A supplier with a story will answer in adjectives.

## How the fruit fails

**Direct answer:** Star anise fails in four ways: it loses oil, it takes on moisture, it grows mould, or it is damaged by insects. Each failure has a different smell, and none of them can be reversed.

Loss of oil is the slow failure. The fruit still looks like a star, but the colour fades from warm reddish-brown to grey, and the smell thins from sweet licorice to dry wood. A faded star is not poisonous; it is simply tired. In a blend it contributes texture and little else, which is worse than contributing nothing because it takes up space that a live material could use.

Moisture is the fast failure. Dried fruit is hygroscopic, and a damp warehouse or a badly sealed container lets water in. The first sign is a loss of crispness: the star bends instead of snapping. The second sign is a musty smell, and by then the aromatic profile is already compromised. A star that has gone soft should not be ground and blended in the hope that the smell will come back.

Mould is the visible stage of the moisture failure. It appears as a grey or green bloom in the crevices between the follicles, sometimes with a dusting on the seeds. Mouldy spice should be discarded, not washed and dried; the visible growth is the end of a process that has already changed the material.

Insect damage is the fourth. Small holes in the pericarp and fine dust in the bag are the signs. The insects are after the seed, and their presence means the lot has been stored warm and open. A sieved lot may look clean, but the damage remains.

| Failure | What you see | What you smell | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oil loss | Faded colour, light weight | Dry wood, faint sweetness | Use for texture only, or discard |
| Moisture | Soft stars that bend | Damp, flat, slightly sour | Dry thoroughly or discard |
| Mould | Grey or green bloom | Musty, cellar-like | Discard |
| Insects | Holes, dust, webbing | Dusty, faintly animal | Discard the lot |

The pattern is that all four failures are storage failures. Star anise leaves the tree in good condition; what happens afterward decides whether it arrives alive. That is why a supplier's warehouse is part of the specification, even though it never appears on a label.

## Three warm spices, compared

**Direct answer:** Star anise, cinnamon, and clove are the usual warm trio, and they are not interchangeable. Cinnamon is woody and soft, clove is sharp and numbing, star anise is sweet and licorice-like.

Cinnamon brings a bark sweetness that sits close to the material. It is warm without being sharp, and it can carry a blend for a long time without demanding attention. In incense it reads as a soft, dry warmth; in cooking it reads as the background note in a braise or a bake.

Clove is the opposite. It is potent, sharp, and slightly numbing, with a eugenol centre that can dominate a blend at a dose that looks tiny. It is useful for lift and for a medicinal edge, and dangerous for the same reasons. A clove-heavy blend smells like a dentist's waiting room, not a spice market.

Star anise sits between them. It is sweeter than clove and more aromatic than cinnamon, with a licorice note that neither of the others has. It is also the most food-associated of the three: cinnamon and clove appear in perfumery as abstractions, while star anise tends to bring the kitchen with it.

| Spice | Centre | Character | Best role | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cinnamon | Cinnamaldehyde | Woody, soft, sweet | Long warm base | Dusty if stale |
| Clove | Eugenol | Sharp, numbing, potent | Lift and edge | Overwhelms at low dose |
| Star anise | Anethole | Sweet, licorice, warm | Sweet-spice accent | Reads as food |

The comparison is useful because it shows that "warm" is a family, not a single note. A formulator who reaches for one of the three without considering the others is choosing a texture as well as a temperature. The right choice depends on what the blend needs: softness, edge, or sweetness.

## Sourcing and storage, in practice

**Direct answer:** Buy whole fruit from a named origin, keep it dry and sealed, and grind only what you will use. The routine is simple because the risks are concentrated in moisture and species.

Whole stars keep for a long time in a sealed container away from light and heat. The woody shell protects the oil, and the shape resists crushing. Check the container periodically for condensation and smell; a musty note means moisture has entered, and the lot should be discarded rather than blended.

Powder is a different proposition. It loses aroma within months, and it cakes if the humidity rises. Buy small quantities, keep them sealed, and use them before the smell fades. A powder that has lost its sweetness will not recover in a blend.

Species verification is the one step that cannot be skipped. Keep the supplier's species declaration with the lot, and if the material is going into a product that will be eaten or burned, ask for a proper identification where there is any doubt. The cost of a test is small compared with the cost of a toxic substitution.

## The fruit up close

**Direct answer:** A star anise is a cluster of follicles, not a single seed pod. The woody walls hold the oil, the seeds hold some aroma, and the proportion of wall to seed changes how the fruit behaves.

Each point of the star is a follicle: a dry, woody case that splits along one edge when the fruit is mature. Inside sits a single glossy seed. The follicles are arranged around a short central axis, and the whole cluster is what the tree produces as a fruit. The arrangement is regular enough that a damaged star is easy to spot: a missing point leaves a clean scar, while a broken point leaves a jagged edge.

The pericarp — the wall of the follicle — is where most of the aromatic oil sits. It is thin, hard, and reddish-brown when sound, fading to grey when old. A star with thick, plump walls carries more oil than a star with thin, papery walls, which is why weight for size is a crude but useful quality signal.

The seed contributes a different kind of aroma: nuttier, oilier, less sweet. In culinary use the seed is part of the package, and a star with its seeds intact smells fuller than a star that has lost them. In distillation, the pericarp does most of the work, and the seeds are a minor contributor.

This anatomy explains a trade habit. Whole stars are graded by size and integrity because the wall is the valuable part and the shape is the easiest way to check it. Broken pieces are sold cheaper because the wall is exposed and the oil is leaving. Powder is cheaper still because it is all wall and no protection.

## In the mouth and in the air

**Direct answer:** Star anise tastes sweeter and rounder than it smells, with a cooling afternote that the nose alone does not predict. The difference between taste and smell is part of why it works in food and puzzles formulators in incense.

Put a fragment on the tongue and the first impression is sweetness, followed by a licorice warmth and a faint numbness at the edges. The cooling afternote is real, and it comes from the same family of aromatic compounds that give the spice its character. It is a mouth sensation, not a smell, and it does not transfer to a burner.

In the air, star anise is drier and more one-dimensional. The sweetness is there, but the roundness of taste is missing, and the cooling edge is faint. What remains is a warm licorice note that can read as sharp if the dose is high. That is why a cook who tastes a sauce and a formulator who smells a stick can disagree about the same spice: they are experiencing different parts of it.

The difference matters for pairing. In food, star anise sits well with fat, soy, sugar, and slow heat. In incense, it sits well with dry woods, resins, and other warm spices, and less well with delicate florals. The pairing logic is not transferable because the medium is not transferable.

A practical test for a formulator is to smell the spice at three dilutions: full strength, a trace on a strip, and a trace in a simple blend. Full strength tells you what the material is; the strip tells you what it does at low dose; the blend tells you how it behaves with other materials. The three answers are often different.

## Provenance claims, and what they mean

**Direct answer:** Star anise is cultivated, so "wild" claims are a warning sign rather than a virtue. Origin claims are useful when they name a region, and organic claims need documentation rather than a label.

The tree is grown in orchards and village plantings. There is no significant wild harvest of *Illicium verum* for the spice trade, and a seller who markets "wild star anise" is either using the word loosely or describing something other than the cultivated fruit. That does not make the material bad; it makes the claim unreliable.

Origin is more useful. Guangxi and Yunnan in southern China, and northern Vietnam, are the classic producing regions, and a named district is a better handle than a country. Regional climate affects drying conditions, and drying conditions affect the final aroma. A buyer who knows the region has a way to compare one lot with another.

Organic claims require a certification chain. A label that says "organic" without a certifier and a certificate number is a marketing word, not a verification. The same is true of "hand-selected" and "premium": they may describe a real sorting step, but they do not describe a standard.

The practical rule is to prefer claims that can be checked. Species can be checked. Origin can be traced. Form can be seen. Grade names and virtue words cannot, which is why they carry so little information for the space they take on a label.


One more storage habit is worth keeping: never store star anise beside a strongly scented material. The fruit takes up foreign aromas through its woody wall, and a bag kept next to soap, coffee, or a resin will smell of the neighbour before it smells of itself. A sealed jar in a dark cupboard is enough; the spice does not need refrigeration and does not benefit from it.
## Buying: a short checklist

**Direct answer:** Confirm the species, buy whole where possible, smell before committing, and keep the lot dry. The checklist is short because the risks are concentrated.

1. **Species.** Ask for *Illicium verum* by name. "Star anise" alone is not an answer.
2. **Origin.** Ask for a region — Guangxi, Yunnan, or a named district — rather than a country.
3. **Form.** Whole stars for inspection and storage; powder only if you will use it quickly.
4. **Aroma.** Crush a point. The smell should be strong, sweet, and clean, not dusty.
5. **Integrity.** Look for whole points, plump follicles, and an absence of debris.
6. **Storage.** Confirm the lot has been kept dry, and plan to keep it that way.

The order matters. Species first, because a species error is a safety error. Form and aroma next, because they decide whether the material is worth buying. Storage last, because it is the easiest problem to prevent and the hardest to reverse.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The fruit is food, yet a stick made with it still sends smoke and airborne grit into the room when it burns. Keep air moving, set the burner on a stable surface, and never use fruit whose species has not been confirmed.

The species warning is the one that separates star anise from most other aromatics on this site. *Illicium anisatum* is toxic, and the similarity between the two fruits means that appearance is not a safeguard. If a lot has no species documentation, do not burn it and do not eat it. That is not caution for its own sake; it is the minimum response to a known adulteration risk.

For confirmed *I. verum*, the ordinary incense precautions apply. Smoke is a combustion product, not a fragrance, and a closed room is the wrong place for it. Keep the burner away from children and pets, and do not inhale concentrated smoke. Nothing here is medical advice, and the [safety guide](/safety/) sets out the general rules for smoke, heat, and open flame.

## Summary

The spice sold as 八角 is the dried fruit of *Illicium verum*, a tree cultivated in southern China and northern Vietnam. Its sweet, licorice-like warmth comes from anethole, the same molecule that drives fennel and anise, two unrelated plants that share its 茴 name. It is a defining kitchen spice and a materia-medica aromatic in the traditional record, yet the classical Chinese incense manuals do not list it; its place in modern blends is a crossover. The safety-critical fact is the existence of *Illicium anisatum*, a toxic look-alike that appearance cannot reliably exclude. Buy by species, buy whole where possible, smell before committing, and keep the lot dry.

## FAQ

**Can star anise go into an incense stick?** Yes, as a ground spice in a small dose. It brings a warm sweetness that pairs well with cinnamon and clove, and it can flatten delicate florals if used too freely. Test it at a low dose in a simple blend before committing a batch, and confirm the species first. The spice is a colour, not a foundation.

**Is star anise a classical Chinese incense ingredient?** No. The Song and Ming fragrance manuals do not list it. Its presence in modern blends comes from its standing as a kitchen sweet spice, not from a classical 香材 role. A blend that uses it can be good, but it should not call itself traditional on that basis.

**What does star anise smell like?** Sweet, licorice-like, warm, and spicy, with anethole at the centre. It reads as a food spice rather than a floral or woody material, and in a blend it adds a warm sweetness that can dominate if overused. The closest familiar smells are anise, fennel, and black licorice.

**How do Chinese and Japanese star anise differ?** The difference is safety, not flavour. *Illicium verum* is the edible fruit. *Illicium anisatum*, also called *I. religiosum*, is a toxic species that must not be eaten or burned. Appearance is a poor guide because the fruits are so similar, so the species should come from documentation or a proper botanical identification.

**Why is star anise in five-spice?** It is one of the defining warm spices of the Chinese five-spice tradition, along with cassia or cinnamon, clove, fennel, and Sichuan pepper. Its sweetness and warmth suit braised meat and rich sauces. That culinary role — not an incense tradition — is why it is familiar and why it occasionally crosses into modern fragrance blends.

**Are anise and fennel related to star anise?** No. Star anise is a tree fruit in Schisandraceae; anise (*Pimpinella anisum*) belongs to the carrot family, as does fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare*). The three share anethole, which is why they smell alike and why the Chinese names share the 茴 element, but they are different plants with different textures and different roles.

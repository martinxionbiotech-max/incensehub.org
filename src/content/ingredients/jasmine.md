**Direct answer:** 茉莉 is the white blossom of *Jasminum sambac*, a southern Chinese crop grown for tea scenting. A second species, 素馨 (*J. grandiflorum*), supplies the heavier perfumery material. Incense uses a rebuilt floral note rather than the blossom itself, because the flower's volatiles do not survive combustion.

## One name, several flowers

**Direct answer:** "Jasmine" is a common name attached to plants in at least three families, and Chinese keeps two true jasmines distinct under 茉莉 and 素馨. The label alone does not tell you which flower, which scent, or which use you are buying.

The naming problem is worse than it looks. True jasmines are *Jasminum* species in Oleaceae. Several unrelated ornamentals are sold as "jasmine" because they are white and fragrant, and one of them, night-blooming jasmine, is in the nightshade family. A buyer who orders "jasmine" by common name can receive a plant that shares a word and nothing else.

| Label | Plant | Family | Smell | Use | Why it gets confused |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 茉莉 | *Jasminum sambac* | Oleaceae | Light, tea-like sweetness | Tea scenting; floral accord | Resembles 素馨 |
| 素馨 | *Jasminum grandiflorum* | Oleaceae | Heavy, indolic, waxy | Perfume absolute | Sold as plain "jasmine" |
| 含笑 | *Michelia figo* | Magnoliaceae | Ripe banana and sweet flower | Garden plant | Small white bloom |
| Night-blooming jasmine | *Cestrum nocturnum* | Solanaceae | Sweet and heavy after dark | Ornamental | The English name |
| Star jasmine | *Trachelospermum jasminoides* | Apocynaceae | Creamy and mild | Climber | The English name |

Two rows in that table are true jasmines. The other three are name collisions, and the collisions matter commercially because a buyer may price a product by the word rather than the species. A tea buyer needs 茉莉; a perfumery buyer usually needs 素馨 or a specific absolute; a garden buyer may want any of the ornamentals. These are separate markets that happen to share a label.

There is a further layer: 茉莉 is generally understood as a loanword rather than a descriptive coinage, while 素馨 reads as a native descriptive name. The etymology is a reminder that the tea flower traveled along trade and religious routes before it became a Chinese garden staple. The plant was cultivated long before the modern tea industry, and the name settled into the language while the botanical identity remained a single species.

## Buying fresh flowers

**Direct answer:** Fresh jasmine is bought by bud condition and smell, not by appearance alone. The best flowers are swollen, closed, unbruised, and fragrant in the evening; open, wet, or fermented blossoms have already lost the part that matters.

Start with the smell at the right hour. Jasmine is a night flower, and a sample taken in the middle of a hot afternoon can seem muted. Smell again in the evening, when the volatile output rises. A fresh batch should smell sweet and floral with a green edge; a sour, cooked, or fermented note is a warning that the flowers have been sitting warm and deep.

Then look at the surface. Bruised petals turn brown and release a heavier, duller smell. Wet flowers are a risk: moisture on the petals encourages microbial growth, and a sealed bag traps the condensation. A picker who delivers damp flowers has created a problem that the tea maker cannot fully undo.

sealed warm bag → condensation on petals → microbial growth → sour and cooked notes → the tea absorbs them → the batch is spoiled

Handling at the receiving end is simple and easy to get wrong. Keep the flowers shallow and cool, do not compress them, and use them the same day. A deep pile generates heat in the center even in a cool room. A plastic bag tied shut holds the moisture in. The flower is still alive and still respiring; it needs air and a little space.

Drying is a different product, not a way to keep fresh flowers. A dried jasmine blossom loses most of its volatile top and takes on a hay-like, soft floral character. That can be pleasant in a sachet or a blend, but it is not the material that scents tea. A buyer who expects dried buds to behave like fresh flowers will be disappointed by the result.

## The scent, described without romance

**Direct answer:** Jasmine is a dense white floral: sweet and heavy, with an animalic undertone. *J. sambac* reads fresher and tea-like; *J. grandiflorum* reads richer and more indolic.

The first impression is sweetness, but sweetness alone would be a candy, not a flower. What makes jasmine recognizable is the combination of a clean white-floral top with a heavier, almost narcotic undertone. Perfumers call that undertone indolic, after indole, a molecule that smells fecal or mothball-like when concentrated and floral when diluted. The same material can read beautiful or dirty depending on dose, which is why jasmine is both a classic perfume note and a difficult one to handle.

Concentration changes perception. A jasmine absolute sniffed from the bottle can seem heavy, syrupy, even slightly animalic. A trace of the same absolute on a blotter, after the solvent has gone, reads clean and luminous. This is not a contradiction; it is the nose responding to intensity. A formulator who judges jasmine only at full strength will overdose it.

*J. sambac* and *J. grandiflorum* sit at different points on that spectrum. Sambac is greener, fresher, and more tea-like, with a sweetness that reads almost like a flower floating in hot water. Grandiflorum is broader and heavier, with a waxy, fruity, indolic depth that survives dilution and gives perfume its "body." A blend built on sambac will feel airy; one built on grandiflorum will feel upholstered.

Commercial "jasmine" notes are usually neither. They are accords built from a small set of aroma materials that reproduce the recognizable floral idea at a fraction of the cost. That is not automatically dishonest: an accord can be consistent, stable, and clearly labeled. The problem begins when an accord is presented as a natural absolute.

## Where jasmine sits among the white florals

**Direct answer:** Jasmine is the dense, indolic white floral. Rose is rounder, osmanthus fruitier, orange blossom cleaner, and lily of the valley cooler and greener.

| Floral material | Botanical source | Scent centre | Weight | Usual role | Contrast with jasmine |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jasmine | *Jasminum sambac*, *J. grandiflorum* | Sweet, indolic, tea-like or narcotic | Heavy | Heart | The reference point |
| Rose | *Rosa* species | Honeyed, rosy, slightly spicy | Medium to heavy | Heart, base | Rounder and less animalic |
| Osmanthus | *Osmanthus fragrans* | Apricot, honey, soft floral | Medium | Heart | Fruitier and lighter |
| Gardenia | *Gardenia jasminoides* | Creamy, lactonic, green | Heavy | Heart | Creamier, less spicy |
| Tuberose | Tuberose | Creamy, narcotic, rubbery | Very heavy | Heart | More powerful and more demanding |
| Orange blossom | *Citrus aurantium* | Fresh, soapy, citrus-floral | Medium | Top to heart | Brighter and cleaner |
| Lily of the valley | *Convallaria majalis* | Green, cool, fresh | Light | Top to heart | Cooler and greener; usually an accord |

The table is a map of weight, not a ranking. Jasmine's distinguishing feature is the indolic undertone: the same quality that makes it smell rich in dilution makes it smell heavy or dirty when concentrated. Rose has a honeyed depth but not that animalic edge. Orange blossom has a clean, soapy brightness that reads almost the opposite of jasmine's warmth. Tuberose is the closest in intensity, and it is even less forgiving of a heavy hand.

For a formulator, the map suggests pairings. Jasmine's weight needs a bright top — citrus, green, or a light herbal note — or the composition opens flat and sweet. It needs a base with some dryness — sandalwood, a balsamic resin, a woody amber — or the dry-down turns syrupy. Rose and osmanthus sit close enough to blend without a seam; tuberose and gardenia can overwhelm it if used at similar strength. These are craft judgments rather than rules, and the only reliable test is the blend on a blotter over time.

## Why the flowers are picked at night

**Direct answer:** Jasmine buds open and release their strongest scent at night, and the harvest is timed to catch them just before opening. Picking at the wrong hour, or letting the flowers heat up after picking, costs more aroma than any later processing step can recover.

The mechanism is a plant's pollination strategy. Night-opening flowers advertise themselves to nocturnal insects, and their volatile output peaks when the pollinators are active. For the grower, that means the field is worked after dark, and the pickers select buds that are swollen but still closed.

bud about to open → volatile emission rises at night → flower picked before opening → cool night air slows respiration → volatiles stay in the tissue until layering

The timing has a second purpose. An open flower has already released much of its scent into the air. A bud that opens during or after picking releases it into the sack instead of into the tea. Picking just before opening keeps the volatile payload inside the flower until it is placed next to the leaf that will absorb it.

Heat is the enemy of that plan. A sack of flowers left in a warm room, or piled too deep, begins to respire faster, and the tissue can heat up. Once that happens, the smell turns from fresh floral to a sour, cooked, slightly fermented note. Tea layered with such flowers absorbs the off-note along with whatever jasmine character remains.

warm, deep pile → respiration and microbial activity → temperature rises → floral volatiles degrade → sour and cooked notes form → the batch is compromised

This is why jasmine work is scheduled at night and why the flowers are moved quickly. The harvest is not romantic; it is a race against the flower's own metabolism. The same logic applies to a small incense maker who buys fresh flowers: the material is perishable, and a refrigerator is not a neutral storage space, because condensation and bruising do their own damage.

## Scenting tea: how 窨制 actually works

**Direct answer:** 窨制 (yìnzhì) is a repeated layering process in which fresh jasmine flowers release volatiles into tea leaves, which adsorb them. The spent flowers are removed after each pass, and the cycle is repeated to build depth.

The process is often described as "scenting," which sounds passive. It is not. Tea leaf is a dry, porous, hygroscopic material with a large internal surface, and it takes up aromatic molecules from the air around it. The flower is a living source that keeps releasing those molecules for a period after picking. Put the two together in a controlled pile, and the leaf gradually loads with jasmine aroma.

fresh flowers layered with tea → volatiles migrate into the leaf → the leaf and air approach equilibrium → spent flowers removed → a fresh charge of flowers added → the gradient is renewed → aroma loads deeper

That chain explains why one pass is not enough. Adsorption slows as the leaf approaches equilibrium with the surrounding atmosphere, so the first charge does most of its work quickly and then stalls. Removing the spent flowers and adding fresh ones resets the gradient. Each pass drives more aroma into the leaf, up to the point where the leaf is saturated or the flowers stop releasing.

The details of the craft sit in the control of moisture and heat. Fresh flowers are wet, and a wet pile can overheat; the leaf can absorb water along with aroma, and a damp tea is a tea that will spoil. The work is therefore a balance: enough humidity and warmth for the flowers to keep releasing, not so much that the leaf or the pile turns. A maker who treats 窨制 as "add flowers and wait" will produce a flat or sour tea.

Removing the spent flowers is not cosmetic. A dead flower is a decaying flower, and decay brings sour, vegetal, and fermented notes. The traditional practice of removing them after each pass protects the tea from exactly that. Some finished teas include a few dried blossoms for appearance, and those blossoms contribute little to the smell; the aroma was laid down by earlier charges that are no longer in the box.

The number of passes is the usual grade signal. More passes mean more flower material, more labor, and more opportunities for failure, so a heavily scented tea costs more to make. That does not mean the pass count alone defines quality: the base tea, the freshness of the flowers, and the drying and storage after scenting all matter. A high pass count on a poor base tea is an expensive way to make a mediocre product.

## Reading a jasmine tea grade

**Direct answer:** A jasmine tea grade is a story about inputs and process, not a single number. The useful questions are which base tea, how fresh the flowers, how many scenting passes, and how the finished tea was dried and stored.

| Marker | What it usually indicates | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Several scenting passes | More flower charges, deeper loading | Was the leaf good enough to carry the extra aroma? |
| "Fresh flower" claim | Blossoms processed soon after harvest | Does the cup taste clean, or dull and cooked? |
| A named leaf grade | The base tea is specified | Is the leaf intact, or mostly broken pieces? |
| Petals left in the tea | Usually decorative | Does the aroma come from the leaf, or only the petals? |
| "No flavoring" | Flower-scented only | Is the note integrated, or sharp on top? |
| Loud smell, low price | Suspicious pairing | How does the dry leaf smell, and how does it brew? |

The table is a set of questions, not a verdict. A tea with visible flowers can be excellent, and a tea with no flowers can be ordinary; the presence of petals tells you about presentation, not about the scenting. The reliable test is to smell the dry leaf, then brew it and smell the liquor. Natural jasmine scenting tends to smell integrated: the floral note and the tea note move together. Added flavoring often sits on top, sharp and separate.

For a buyer comparing two teas, the useful discipline is to brew both at the same strength and compare the empty cup as well as the liquor. A well-scented tea leaves a floral residue in the cup after the liquid is gone. A flavored tea often leaves sweetness without the floral echo.

## Brewing jasmine tea

**Direct answer:** Treat jasmine tea as a green tea with a floral top note: water off the boil, a short first infusion, and a vessel you can smell from. Boiling water and long steeps flatten the flower and push the leaf's astringency forward.

The leaf is the base, so the brewing rules that apply to green tea apply here. Water that is too hot extracts bitterness and tannin faster than it releases the floral note; water that is too cool leaves the leaf under-extracted and the cup thin. The practical signal is the smell: if the steam smells sharp or cooked, the water is too hot.

A small vessel is better than a large one for the same reason a wine glass is better than a mug. The aroma collects in the headspace, and each pour gives you a fresh read on the flower. A gaiwan or a small teapot works; a wide mug dissipates the smell before it reaches the nose.

The empty cup is part of the test. A well-scented tea leaves a floral trace after the liquor is gone; a flavored tea often leaves sweetness without the floral echo. That residue is a more honest signal than the first sip, because it lingers after the volatile top has gone.

Iced jasmine tea is a different method, not a colder version of the same one. Stewing the leaf and then chilling it produces a flat, bitter drink; a cold infusion or a quick chill after a short, strong brew preserves more of the floral note. The flower's volatiles are sensitive to heat, and a long hot steep gives them more time to escape.

Storage belongs here because it changes the cup. Jasmine tea picks up moisture and odors, and a bag left open beside spices will taste of the shelf rather than the flower. A sealed tin, a cool cupboard, and a short turnaround are the whole method.

## Why green tea takes jasmine so well

**Direct answer:** Green tea is a quiet base, so the floral note registers. A fully oxidized black tea brings its own malty and fruity aroma, which competes with the flower.

Tea leaf is not an inert sponge. It has its own volatile profile, and that profile changes with oxidation, firing, and age. Green tea is processed to stop oxidation early, which leaves a fresher, lighter, more vegetal aroma. That relative quiet is an advantage in scenting: the flower's note sits on a base that does not argue with it.

Black tea is a different canvas. Full oxidation produces malty, sweet, fruity, and sometimes smoky notes, and those notes occupy the same aromatic space that jasmine wants to fill. A jasmine black tea can be excellent, but it is a blend of two strong voices rather than a flower on a quiet ground. The jasmine has to be dosed higher, and the result is a different drink.

leaf structure → adsorption of floral volatiles → scent held in the leaf matrix → firing removes moisture and sets the aroma → storage either preserves or drains it

Leaf grade matters for the same reason. Young, tender leaves have a large surface relative to their mass and take up aroma readily. Coarse, late-picked leaves are thicker and less responsive, and they bring their own harsher notes. A high flower-to-leaf ratio cannot rescue a coarse base; it can only cover it for a while.

This is also why jasmine tea is usually made from green tea rather than from white or oolong tea. White tea is delicate but scarce and expensive; oolong carries its own roast and fruit, which complicates the floral line. Green tea offers the most predictable canvas at a workable cost, and the craft developed around that fit.

## From flower to concrete and absolute

**Direct answer:** Jasmine is normally traded as concrete and absolute, not as a steam-distilled essential oil. The flowers are solvent-extracted into a waxy concrete, then washed with alcohol to separate the aromatic absolute from the wax.

The sequence explains the cost:

fresh flowers → solvent extraction → concrete (wax plus volatiles) → alcohol washing → wax removed → solvent evaporation → absolute

Each step loses material. The concrete contains the flower's waxes along with its aroma, and the absolute is the alcohol-soluble fraction that remains after the wax is stripped. What is left is intensely aromatic but no longer smells exactly like a flower on the bush; it smells like a concentrated, slightly transformed version of it.

The harvest side is just as demanding. The flowers are picked by hand at night, before opening, and they begin to deteriorate soon after picking. A producer cannot stockpile them for a convenient processing run. The equipment has to be close to the field, and the flowers have to move through extraction while they are still fresh. That combination of hand labor, nocturnal timing, and perishable input is why genuine jasmine absolute is expensive and why cheap "jasmine absolute" deserves a question.

A retail confusion sits on top of this. "Jasmine oil" in a shopping listing often means a fragrance oil diluted in a carrier, not a botanical extract. True jasmine oil from steam distillation is a specialty item, and most of the jasmine material in perfumery is absolute. A buyer who wants the botanical should look for the word "absolute" or "concrete" and a species name, not the generic word "oil."

## Handling the absolute

**Direct answer:** Jasmine absolute is a thick, intensely aromatic material that needs gentle warming and a solvent to work with. It is not a drop-in liquid perfume, and treating it like one wastes material and distorts the smell.

The physical behavior surprises first-time users. Concrete is waxy at room temperature, and absolute can range from a thick liquid to a semi-solid depending on the batch and the weather. A cold bottle may need to sit in a warm room for a while before it can be poured. Direct heat is a bad idea: it drives off the volatile top and can scorch the material, leaving a cooked note that no later step removes.

Absolute dissolves in alcohol, not in water. A formulator usually makes a dilution in ethanol or another suitable solvent, then doses that dilution into the blend. The dilution is not a compromise; it is a handling necessity, because the pure material is too concentrated to measure accurately by drop. A good dilution also makes the material easier to smell without nose fatigue.

Staining and discoloration are practical concerns. Jasmine absolute is dark and can mark paper, fabric, and light-colored wax. A finished product may darken over time, especially if it is exposed to light and air. That does not necessarily mean the smell has failed, but it does mean a maker should test the material in the intended base rather than assume the absolute will behave like a clear synthetic.

Storage follows the same logic as the flower: cool, dark, sealed, and not opened more than necessary. A bottle that is half full holds air, and air plus time oxidizes the aromatic fraction. Decanting into a smaller container, or displacing the headspace with an inert gas if the scale justifies it, slows the decline. For a small maker, the simpler discipline is to buy a quantity that will be used within a reasonable period.

## Jasmine in incense: the accord question

**Direct answer:** Raw jasmine flowers make poor incense. Burning the blossom destroys the delicate volatiles and produces smoke and acrid plant notes, so the floral impression is usually rebuilt as an accord over a wood or resin base.

The chemistry is unforgiving. A flower's scent is carried by molecules that are meant to evaporate at ambient temperature. A burning coal is hundreds of degrees hotter, and the flower's cellulose and sugars burn alongside the aroma. The delicate floral molecules are pyrolyzed or swept away in smoke before they can be perceived as jasmine.

flower on a hot coal → cellulose and sugars burn → smoke and acrid notes dominate → floral volatiles decompose → the air smells of burning plant, not of jasmine

That is why jasmine incense is a construction rather than a single material. A maker builds a base of wood or resin, then adds a floral accord at a low dose. The base carries the burn and the dry-down; the accord supplies the recognizable floral idea. A well-built accord can be stable, pleasant, and consistent from batch to batch.

Natural absolute or rebuilt accord is a formulation choice, not a moral one. Absolute brings harvest variation and a high price; an accord brings consistency and a lower cost. Each can be well made. The failure is a label that claims one while delivering the other: "natural jasmine" on a synthetic accord misrepresents the material, while "jasmine fragrance" tells the truth.

For a collector, the practical question is what the incense is trying to do. A floral stick built on sandalwood and a jasmine accord is a modern composition with a floral accent. A stick that claims to be "pure jasmine flower" is making a claim that combustion chemistry does not support. The first can be excellent; the second is a category error.

Dose matters as much in incense as in perfume. Jasmine reads sweet and heavy, and a small amount lifts a blend; too much turns it soapy, cloying, or laundry-like. The material has a low ceiling before it stops smelling like a flower and starts smelling like a product. A maker who treats "more jasmine" as "more luxurious" usually produces a stick that is hard to burn twice.

## Building a jasmine accord

**Direct answer:** A jasmine accord has to do four jobs at once: supply a sweet-floral body, add a green lift, suggest the indolic depth of the natural flower, and leave a smooth dry-down. Miss one and the note reads as soap, candy, or solvent.

The skeleton is easier to understand than a formula. The sweet-floral body usually comes from benzyl acetate and related esters, the family of materials that gives jasmine its familiar perfumery signature. A green top — a small amount of a grassy or leafy material — keeps the sweetness from collapsing into candy. A trace of indole supplies the animalic depth that makes jasmine recognizable rather than generic. A fruity nuance, often from a lactone or a soft ester, rounds the edges.

sweet esters → floral body → green materials → lift and freshness → trace indole → animalic depth → fixative base → slower release

The balance is where the craft sits. Too much indole and the accord smells dirty or mothball-like; too little and it smells like a clean shampoo. Too much green and it turns harsh; too little and it is a sweet blob. The same set of materials can produce a credible jasmine, a laundry fragrance, or a cheap air freshener depending on proportion and context.

This is why a maker builds an accord rather than simply buying absolute and dosing it. Absolute is variable, expensive, and delicate in a finished product. An accord can be standardized, tested against a reference, and adjusted for the base. The trade-off is that an accord is an interpretation: it captures the idea of jasmine, not the whole flower. A good one is honest about that.

No generic recipe travels well. A ratio that works in an alcohol-based fragrance may fail in a candle, where heat changes which facets dominate, or in an incense base, where combustion adds its own notes. The accord has to be tested in the medium where it will live. A bench formula is a starting point, not a finished product.

## 茉莉 in the Chinese record

**Direct answer:** 香乘 lists 茉莉 and 素馨 as separate flowers, and the tea craft is traditionally dated to the Song. The "oldest scented-flower tea" line is a traditional claim, not a dated record.

The useful fact is the pairing of names. 香乘 names 素馨 and 茉莉 separately, including in 心字香 and the ten-flower blend 逗情香. A recipe that says 素馨 wants *J. grandiflorum*; one that says 茉莉 wants *J. sambac*. Two materials, not two spellings.

The tea history is usually dated to the Song dynasty (960–1279), but the method matters more than the date: fresh blossoms, repeated layering, spent flowers removed. The "oldest" claim is repeated far more often than it is documented, and this page treats it as traditional.

The distinction that matters is between documented use and demonstrated efficacy: the first is history, the second is clinical evidence, and jasmine has only the first. Materia medica references are traditional belief, not clinical evidence.

## Adulteration and misidentification

**Direct answer:** Two risks dominate: a label that omits the species, and a price gap that invites substitution. "Jasmine" alone can mean several plants, and absolute is costly enough that diluted or synthetic versions circulate.

| Label wording | What it may be | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| "Jasmine absolute" | True extract, or an extended or diluted one | Species, batch record, side-by-side smell |
| "Jasmine oil" | Often fragrance oil plus carrier | Ingredient list; is a botanical extract named? |
| "All-natural incense" | Natural absolute or a natural-style accord | Full disclosure, not the front panel |
| "Sambac" | *J. sambac*, the tea species | Origin and form also stated? |
| Highly scented "jasmine tea" | May be flavored | Dry leaf, liquor, and leaf grade |

The naming risk is the easier one to manage. Ask for the species, and expect one of two answers: *J. sambac* or *J. grandiflorum*. A seller who cannot choose is selling a category. The substitution risk is harder because a good accord can smell convincing, and a diluted absolute can smell thin rather than fake. The useful test is comparison: smell the sample beside a known reference, at the same dilution, on the same kind of blotter. Without a reference, "smells like jasmine" is a low bar.

There is a specific trap with "jasmine oil." In aromatherapy retail, the phrase often describes a blend of fragrance materials and a carrier oil, not a steam-distilled botanical. That product can be pleasant and perfectly legitimate as a fragrance oil, but it is not the same material as jasmine absolute, and it should not be priced as one. Read the ingredient list before the marketing copy.

For tea, adulteration usually takes the form of flavoring rather than flower substitution. A tea can be scented with a jasmine flavor rather than fresh flowers, and the result can be strong and one-dimensional. The tell is in the cup: natural scenting tends to fade gradually and leave a floral echo, while added flavoring can sit sharply on top and disappear abruptly.

## A maker's quality checklist

**Direct answer:** A jasmine batch is controlled at four points: incoming flowers, the scenting run, the retained sample, and the finished package. Each point produces a record that lets a maker compare one lot with the next.

The incoming check is sensory and physical. Look at bud stage, bruising, and foreign matter; smell the flowers in the evening, when the volatile output is highest; and take the temperature of the sack if it feels warm. A warm, deep sack of blossoms is already deteriorating, and no later step fully rescues it.

The scenting record should capture what was actually done: flower charge, leaf charge, number of passes, time in contact, and the room conditions. These are the variables that explain why two batches with the same recipe smell different. A record that says only "scented" is a story, not a specification.

A retention sample is the cheapest insurance in the process. Seal a small portion of each finished lot, label it with the date and the inputs, and keep it for comparison. When a customer reports a change months later, the sample answers the first question: did the tea change, or did the memory?

A blind sensory panel is more useful than a single expert. Pour the reference and the candidate side by side, code the cups, and score floral intensity, integration, astringency, and off-notes. The point is not a grade but a direction: is this lot closer to the reference than the last one? A panel of two or three trained noses is enough for a small operation.

Packaging is the last control point and the easiest to neglect. Jasmine aroma migrates, and so do the smells around it. A barrier film, a tight seal, and a dark shelf slow the loss; a folded bag in a warm room accelerates it. The package should also carry the species, the form, and the lot date, because those three fields make every later conversation possible.

What fails most often is not the flower. It is the record: a batch that cannot be traced, a sample that was not kept, a label that says "jasmine" without a species. The checklist is boring, and that is the point. Boring records are what let a maker defend a good batch and correct a bad one.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Smoke and concentrates are the two hazards. Ventilate the room when burning incense, and treat absolute as an industrial fragrance material rather than a food or medicine.

Incense smoke is a combustion product, and the floral note does not make it benign. Burn in a ventilated space, keep the burner away from flammable material, and do not leave it unattended. Anyone with a respiratory condition should be cautious around any incense smoke.

Absolute and concrete are concentrates; treat them as industrial fragrance materials, not as something to swallow or dab neat. Some people are sensitive to fragrance materials, so a patch test is sensible before using a new absolute in a product worn on the body. Keep bottles out of reach of children and pets.

Jasmine tea is a separate case because it is an infusion of tea leaves. It contains caffeine unless the base tea is decaffeinated, and the flower does not change that. Anyone limiting caffeine should treat jasmine tea as tea, not as a caffeine-free floral infusion.

## Summary

Jasmine is one of the few flowers whose reputation rests on a process rather than a plant alone. 窨制 — repeated layering of fresh blossoms against tea — is what made it a Chinese institution, and it explains both the price of good jasmine tea and the difficulty of faking it.

The botany is simpler: 茉莉 is *Jasminum sambac*, 素馨 is *J. grandiflorum*, and 香乘 treats them as separate materials. In incense, heat destroys the delicate volatiles, so the note is rebuilt rather than burned. A buyer's checklist is short: species, form, and an honest label.

## FAQ

**Is jasmine the same as jasmine tea?** No — one is the blossom, the other is leaf that has taken on its aroma. Fresh flowers are layered against tea and then taken out; the leaf keeps the scent. The finished product may contain no petals at all.

**Is jasmine a wood or a flower?** A flower. The shrub is woody, but the trade sells blossoms, not timber. The tea flower and the perfumery flower are different species; neither is sold as wood.

**Is jasmine tea caffeine-free?** No. The base is *Camellia sinensis* leaf, which naturally contains caffeine; the blossom only adds scent. A decaffeinated base is the only way to make the drink caffeine-free.

**Why does jasmine incense smell different from a fresh flower?** Heat destroys the molecules that carry the living floral note. On a coal, the blossom's plant tissue burns and throws smoke, while the delicate volatiles break down before they can be read as jasmine. Makers get around this by building the note from aroma materials over a wood or resin base. The result can resemble jasmine, but it is an interpretation, not the flower.

**How do 茉莉 and 素馨 differ?** They are two species with different weights. 茉莉 (*J. sambac*) is the fresher, tea-like flower; 素馨 (*J. grandiflorum*) is heavier and more indolic. 香乘 lists them separately, and each has its own page here.

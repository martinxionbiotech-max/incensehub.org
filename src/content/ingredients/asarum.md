**Direct answer:** Asarum (细辛, xìxīn) is the dried root and rhizome of *Asarum sieboldii*, a low woodland herb in the birthwort family (Aristolochiaceae) that grows in shade across China, Korea, and Japan. The name means "thin and pungent," and the root lives up to it: slender, deep purple, and intensely acrid, with a scent closer to medicine than to perfume. It is a classical materia-medica root, and no incense or sachet role is documented for it.

## A root named after its own bite

**Direct answer:** The name is a description, not a poem. 细 is "fine" or "slender"; 辛 is "acrid" or "pungent." Put together, they promise a wiry root with a hot bite — and the old literature treats that promise as a specification.

Plant names in Chinese usually record a place, a season, or a likeness. This one records an experience. The first syllable describes the root — wiry, not tuberous — and the second describes what it does: 辛, the acrid, dispersing, nose-clearing quality counted among the five flavors. The label is a promise about the material before anyone opens the sack.

The word is also a trap. English "pungent" leans toward flavor, while 辛 gathers a whole family of feelings: pepper's sting, camphor's cool burn, ginger's sharpness, the lift of a crushed leaf. 细辛 sits inside that family. In the hand it smells hot, rooty, faintly camphoraceous, with a peppery edge that catches high in the nose.

Part of what 辛 names is not smell at all. The trigeminal nerve reports sting, coolness, and irritation alongside aroma, and those signals are what make a material read as medicinal rather than fragrant. That is why 细辛 belongs on the medicine shelf and not in the incense box. A camphor note can be composed with; a nasal sting cannot.

草之二 states the same thing through taste: "華州真細辛，根細而味極辛" — Huazhou's genuine root, fine in form and fierce on the tongue. The standard is sensory, and its two halves sit inside the two characters.

## What plant you are actually holding

**Direct answer:** What reaches the market is underground tissue: rootstock, dried and cleaned, from a small herb that lives on shaded forest floors in East Asia. No leaf, bark, or resin is involved. The point is worth stating plainly because the trade name is often explained through comparisons with ginger, ginseng, or a resin, and none of those describes the physical object in the sack.

Nothing about the plant is arborescent. It is an understory herb, at home in leaf litter and deep shade, too small to yield timber or bark. What is harvested is underground: the rootstock, lifted, cleaned, and dried. That single fact explains the scale of the trade and the look of the product — small pieces, variable sizes, no bales of uniform material.

Start with the family, because the family is the first safety fact. *Asarum* is a genus in the Aristolochiaceae, and that family is where aristolochic acids are found — the reason birthwort relatives attract caution in modern writing. No assay of this species is offered here, and no claim is made either way about its content. The point is that the Latin family name should register as a warning, not as decoration.

The genus also has a Chinese center of diversity, and the trade name 细辛 is applied across more than one species. Flora of China records *Asarum sieboldii* and notes the closely related *Asarum heterotropoides*, which is also gathered as 细辛 in northern China. Both are small woodland herbs with aromatic roots; both can appear in a bag labelled 细辛. The botanical difference is real, and the commercial difference is often invisible.

> **A sourcing note:** a supplier who says "细辛" has named a trade category, not a species. The useful question is which *Asarum* is in the lot and where it was collected, because the common sources are different plants with different ranges.

## Two species under one trade name

**Direct answer:** 细辛 in commerce can mean *Asarum sieboldii* or the northern *Asarum heterotropoides*, and the label rarely says which. The two are close relatives with similar roots, so the name survives while the species information disappears between the collector and the buyer.

Botany keeps the two plants apart; commerce puts them together. A digger works one district, a dealer buys from several diggers, and the sack that leaves the warehouse bears a single label. That is how a category becomes a bucket. Nobody has to intend a fraud for this to happen; it is what a use-based name does when the plants look alike.

The chain is easy to state:

collectors work different ranges → roots of two species arrive at the same dealer → sorting is done by appearance, not by taxonomy → the visual differences overlap → the name becomes the only common denominator → species information is lost at the point of sale

This is the same structural problem that shows up across the materia medica, and the classical literature responds to it with descriptions rather than with labels. The *Bencao Gangmu* does not say "buy 细辛"; it says what the true root looks like. That is a recognition standard designed for a market where names cannot be trusted on their own.

For a buyer, the practical consequence is that the species question has to be asked explicitly. "Is this *Asarum sieboldii*?" is a better question than "Is this real 细辛?", because the second can be answered with a nod while the first requires a source. A supplier who can name the species and the region is giving information; a supplier who repeats the trade name is giving reassurance.

There is an editorial point here that applies well beyond this page. A trade name is a search key and a category, not a specification. The older literature understood that, which is why its quality sections are so concrete about form and color. The modern buyer inherits the name and loses the description, and then wonders why the material varies.

## A name that has always needed a referee

**Direct answer:** 细辛 has a classical look-alike, 杜衡 (*Asarum forbesii*), whose curved, brittle, yellow-white roots were sold in its place for centuries. The *Bencao Gangmu* preserves a warning old enough to be a proverb: 杜衡亂細辛，自古已然矣.

The comparison comes from Shen Kuo's *Mengxi Bitan*, preserved as a quotation in the *Bencao Gangmu*. His genuine root is "極細而直，柔韌，深紫色，味極辛": wiry, straight, supple, purple-black, fiercely acrid. His double is the opposite — curved, brittle rather than pliant, and pale. Two descriptions, one table, no taxonomy required.

| Feature | 细辛 (*Asarum sieboldii*) | 杜衡 (*Asarum forbesii*) |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Slender, straight, pliant | Curved, brittle |
| Color | Deep purple | Yellow-white |
| Pungency | Extreme, acrid | Aromatic but milder |
| Classical status | The materia-medica root | The documented substitute |

Read the table as a set of questions, not a verdict. A single curved root in a bundle does not condemn the lot; a uniform bundle of straight, deep-purple roots does not prove the species. What the classical description gives a buyer is a vocabulary for asking, and a reason to distrust a lot that looks nothing like the description.

The persistence of the substitution is the more interesting fact. 杜衡 was not a rare fraud invented by a modern middleman. It was a known look-alike that growers, diggers, and dealers had to sort out long before anyone wrote a quality standard. That tells you something about the economics of wild-collected roots: when two plants grow in similar ground and one is more valuable, the cheaper one will be in the bag unless someone checks. The classical warning is a response to a market problem, not a botanical curiosity.

There is a second layer to the confusion. 杜衡 is itself an *Asarum*, and the two plants are close enough that a non-specialist can mistake one for the other. The difference is not a matter of gross anatomy; it is a matter of proportions, color, and the intensity of the root's bite. That is exactly the kind of difference that survives in a text but disappears in a photograph.

## Wild ginger is not a ginger

**Direct answer:** The English name "wild ginger" is a sensory nickname, not a botanical relationship. *Asarum* sits in the birthwort family; true ginger is *Zingiber officinale*, a different family with a different chemistry. The shared name describes a similar heat in the root, and it causes a predictable set of confusions.

English common names are often borrowed from the sensation a plant produces. A root that bites like ginger gets called wild ginger, just as a leaf that smells of lemon gets called lemon balm. The habit is old and not unreasonable — before taxonomy, a name had to be memorable — but it becomes a problem when the nickname is treated as a category.

The botanical facts are not close. *Asarum sieboldii* is a low woodland herb in the Aristolochiaceae. True ginger is a rhizomatous tropical herb in the Zingiberaceae. The two are not in the same family, let alone the same genus, and their root chemistry is different. A formulation that treats one as a substitute for the other is not making a small substitution; it is changing the material.

The naming confusion has three practical forms:

- **When the English name means 细辛.** A translated page says "wild ginger" and stops there; the reader then hunts the phrase and lands on a nursery listing, a culinary ginger, or a different *Asarum*.
- **When it means 杜衡.** The same phrase gets attached to the classical double, which hides the substitution inside a translation.
- **When it means true ginger.** A cook expects *Zingiber officinale* and meets an acrid root from another family entirely.

The chain of confusion is predictable:

one English nickname → several botanical referents → search results merge them → a buyer cannot tell which plant a page means → the trade name becomes the only anchor → species information is lost again

That is the same failure that the Chinese trade name produces, translated into a second language. The lesson is not that common names are useless. It is that a common name has to be paired with a botanical one before it can carry a sourcing decision.

For this page, the precise formulation is simple: 细辛 is *Asarum sieboldii*, a birthwort-family woodland herb. "Wild ginger" is a nickname to recognize and set aside. If a supplier uses the English name, ask for the Latin one. A supplier who cannot give it is not necessarily selling the wrong root, but the buyer has no way to check.

## The boundary: aromatic, but not incense

**Direct answer:** 细辛 is not documented as a classical incense or sachet ingredient. Its pungency is medicinal, its classical standing is materia medica, and nothing in the record consulted here assigns it a role in the burning-fragrance canon. The negative finding is the entry's most important claim.

This is a page about an aromatic material that does not belong in an incense bowl, and the distinction needs to be stated without hedging. We did not find 细辛 in the incense manuals or in the sachet recipes consulted for this encyclopedia. It appears in the materia-medica record, with a name that describes its bite, a set of quality markers, and a dosing caution. That is a different kind of standing.

The temptation for a content writer is to close the gap. An aromatic root, a long history, a strong smell — surely it was used in sachets somewhere. The honest answer is that "surely" is not a source. Aromatic plants are common; incense materials are a narrower set, defined by how they behave when heated, how they blend, and whether the tradition assigned them a role. 细辛 fails that test on the record, and the failure is informative.

The mechanism is not mysterious:

a root whose aroma is dominated by acrid, camphoraceous volatiles → the material is burned → the volatile fraction flashes off with the smoke → what remains is char and acrid particulate → the room reads as medicine burning, not as incense

A second chain explains why the same material can still be aromatic in the hand:

dried root → tissue broken or crushed → volatile compounds enter the air at room temperature → the nose receives a sharp, spicy cloud → the impression is strong but short-lived → the material reads as a pungent drug rather than a perfume

Neither chain is a laboratory result. They are readings of how the material behaves, offered to explain a documentary absence rather than to replace it. The documentary point stands on its own: no classical incense or sachet use is claimed here, because none was found.

That absence has a practical consequence for formulators. A material that is not in the canon has no traditional dose, no traditional partner list, and no traditional role in a 君臣佐使 (chief-minister-assistant-guide) frame. Using it anyway would be an experiment, not a reconstruction. An encyclopedia should say so plainly, and let the reader decide whether the experiment is worth running.

## The sachet question, examined

**Direct answer:** A sachet is not a small incense burner. It works by slow evaporation at room temperature, not by combustion, and it asks a material to release a changing scent over hours or days. 细辛's record does not assign it that role, and its pungency is a poor fit for it.

A sachet is a slow device. Dried plant tissue sits behind cloth, and whatever evaporates at room temperature drifts into the room. No flame, no soot, no heat beyond the ambient. The scent is whatever the material offers without help, which is why sachet recipes favour materials that release gradually and keep developing.

Fire is another process altogether. Heat strips the volatile fraction in seconds, and combustion contributes soot, carbon, and new reaction products that were never part of the cold scent. A root that fills a room in the hand may give thin, acrid smoke on a coal; a quiet material may open up under heat. One test does not predict the other.

For 细辛, both routes fail on the record. The classical literature places it in materia medica, describes its quality as a matter of form and pungency, and attaches a dosing caution. None of that is a sachet brief. The absence is not a prohibition; it is a statement about what the sources support. A reader who wants to experiment is free to do so, but the page will not dress the experiment as tradition.

Lists propagate. A sachet recipe appears on one site, gets copied to a dozen others, and within a year the material looks traditional because it is everywhere. Repetition is not evidence. The test is to trace a claim back to a text, and to notice when the trail ends at another list. That habit is dull, and it is the only defence against a category error that has been copied into respectability.

There is a useful test for any "aromatic root" claim on the internet. Ask which classical text assigns the material a fragrance role, and what that role is. If the answer is a general statement about aromatic plants, the claim is a category error. Aromatic is a property; incense is a use.

## What makes a root aromatic

**Direct answer:** A root is a storage organ, and its aromatic fraction is part of what it stores. That fact explains most of the differences between a root material and a resin, a wood, or a flower: the scent is locked inside tissue, released by cutting or grinding, and changed by drying.

Plants make aromatic compounds for reasons that have nothing to do with perfume: defense, signalling, and storage. In a root, those compounds sit in living tissue rather than in a surface duct or a resin pocket. The practical consequence is that the material has to be mechanically opened before it gives up its smell. A whole root is a sealed package; a crushed root is an open one.

Root structure matters here. A root has an outer layer, a cortex, and a central vascular core, and the aromatic tissue is not distributed evenly through those zones. The outer and middle tissues usually carry more of the volatile fraction than the woody core, which is one reason a thin, pliant root can smell stronger than a thick, woody one of the same weight. The classical preference for slender roots is a preference for the part that is rich in aromatic tissue and quick to dry.

Drying changes the balance. Water leaves, the tissue shrinks, and the remaining aromatic compounds become a larger share of the mass. Some volatiles leave with the water; others are held more tightly as the tissue collapses. The result is not a simple concentration but a shift in proportion, which is why a dried root can smell both stronger and flatter than the fresh one.

Grinding goes further. It opens cells, increases surface area, and releases the volatile fraction into the air. A freshly ground root smells vivid for a short time and then fades, because the compounds that made it vivid have left. Whole roots keep their aroma longer because most of the tissue is still sealed.

This is the general mechanism behind a very specific judgment:

root tissue intact → volatiles stay inside the cells → the cold smell is modest → cutting or crushing opens the tissue → the volatile fraction escapes quickly → the first impression is strong and the tail is short

A resin behaves differently. It is already an exudate, a hardened mass of compounds that were secreted onto the surface, and its aroma is available without any mechanical opening. A wood behaves differently again: the aromatic fraction sits in the heartwood, and the material releases it slowly over a long burn. A root sits between the two: sealed like a wood, concentrated like a resin, and dependent on preparation in a way that neither is.

For a buyer, the practical translation is simple. A root that has been ground is a different material from a root that is whole, even when both come from the same plant. The ground form gives an immediate impression and a short life; the whole form gives a slower impression and a longer one. Neither is better in the abstract. They are different tools, and the choice depends on whether the material is being evaluated or stored.

There is a boundary worth stating. None of this makes a root an incense material. Aromatic tissue is a property of the plant; a place in the burning-fragrance canon is a fact about a tradition. The mechanism explains how a root behaves when heated, not whether it belongs in the blend. Confusing the two is the most common category error in writing about aromatic plants.

## From forest floor to a dry root

**Direct answer:** The processing chain is short — dig, clean, dry — and most of the quality is decided before the root reaches the drying rack. Shade-grown habitat, the age of the plant, and the care taken in lifting the root all show up in the dried material.

The classical material is a dug root, not a tapped resin or a distilled oil. That means the harvest is destructive: the plant is lifted, the root and rhizome are separated, and the aromatic tissue is dried. There is no coppicing, no tapping, and no second harvest from the same plant. The supply is a function of how many plants can be found and how much labor goes into finding them.

Drying is the step that turns a fresh root into a trade good, and it is also the step that sharpens the impression:

fresh root lifted from the soil → water leaves the tissue during drying → the aromatic fraction is concentrated in the remaining dry matter → the dried root reads hotter per gram than the fresh root → a small piece carries more punch than its size suggests

The chain is a reading of the material, not a measurement, and it has a limit. Volatiles also leave during drying, so the relationship is not a simple concentration. A slow, shaded dry preserves more of the top note; a hot, fast dry pushes the root toward a flatter, dustier smell. That is why two lots of the same species can smell different even when both are genuine.

Cleaning matters for a different reason. A woodland root arrives with soil, leaf litter, and fragments of other roots. The classical quality description is a description of a cleaned root — straight, pliant, deep purple — and none of those features can be judged on a dirty bundle. A supplier who cannot show a cleaned, sorted lot is asking the buyer to grade the soil.

Storage follows the same logic as any aromatic root. The material is dried, so moisture is the enemy; the aroma is volatile, so heat and air are the enemies. A sealed container in a cool, dark place is the ordinary answer. The mistake to avoid is storing it beside mild materials that will take up the smell, because a pungent root does not stay in its own jar in any practical sense.

## Why the lot varies

**Direct answer:** A wild-collected root arrives in mixed sizes and ages, and the mixture is the normal condition of the trade. Variation is not a defect in itself; an unsorted lot is. The classical quality description works as a sorting standard precisely because the raw supply is uneven.

Fields can be harvested at a single age and graded by sieve. A forest herb cannot. What a digger brings back depends on what the season offered, so the sack contains roots of different vintages and diameters, and the moisture in them varies with the patch they came from. Drying then exaggerates the spread instead of evening it out.

The chain runs through the lot:

mixed plant ages → mixed root diameters → uneven moisture at harvest → uneven drying across the batch → uneven pungency from one handful to the next → a lot that reads strong in one place and flat in another

That is why the classical text spends its words on form and color rather than on a single grade. A description that lets a sorter separate the roots is more useful than a label that declares the whole sack excellent.

For a buyer, the practical response is to ask how the lot was sorted and to look at the range inside it, not only at the best piece on top. A handful that contains one beautiful root and a dozen woody fragments is a different product from a handful of uniform roots, even when both carry the same name and the same weight.

## How to read a lot

**Direct answer:** Judge the root on four axes: form, color, pungency, and species. The first three come from the classical description; the fourth comes from the label and the supplier. None of them is a laboratory test, and together they are specific enough to separate a genuine lot from the documented substitute.

The classical criteria are unusually concrete for a root:

1. **Form** — slender, straight, and pliant, not curved or brittle.
2. **Color** — deep purple, not yellow-white.
3. **Pungency** — extreme and acrid, with a camphoraceous edge.
4. **Species** — *Asarum sieboldii*, or the northern *A. heterotropoides*, not *Asarum forbesii*.

Those four checks are sensory and morphological. They measure no compound and certify no safety. What they catch is the failure the old literature actually documents: a stand-in sold under the right name.

| Signal | What it suggests | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Straight, slender, pliant roots | the classical form description is met | species identity on its own |
| Deep purple color | the expected root type | that the lot is uniform |
| Yellow-white, curved, brittle roots | possible 杜衡 | deliberate adulteration |
| Extreme acrid pungency | aromatic strength | safety or species |
| Flat, dusty smell | old stock or a hot dry | that the root is fake |
| Uniform, clean, sorted pieces | careful handling | that the label is accurate |

The table is a screening aid, not a verdict. A lot can pass every visual check and still be the wrong plant; a rough-looking lot can be genuine. The checks narrow the question, and the label answers the rest — or fails to.

There is an economic dimension to the grading problem. Wild-collected roots are sold by weight, and the visual differences between species are small. A dealer who buys from many small collectors has to sort what arrives; a dealer who buys from one collector may not. That is why the classical warning has survived: it describes a structural problem in the trade, not a one-off fraud.

A final check is worth keeping separate from the others. Smell the root in the hand, then crush a small piece and smell again. The difference between the two impressions tells you how much of the aroma is locked in the tissue and how much has already escaped. A root that smells the same crushed and whole has little left to give.

## A taste standard in a scent world

**Direct answer:** The classical quality language for 细辛 is a taste language — "味極辛" — not a perfume language. That is not a translation problem; it tells a reader what kind of material this is and which sense the tradition used to judge it.

"Thin root and extremely pungent taste" is a compact standard. It tells a sorter to look at the root and then to bite or chew a piece. The judgment is immediate, repeatable, and does not require a laboratory. It also assumes a context in which the material was handled by people who used it as medicine, not by perfumers evaluating a top note.

A modern reader cannot simply copy the method. Chewing an unidentified root is a bad idea, and this page does not recommend it. What can be copied is the logic: identify the part, observe its form, and test the intensity of the material in a way that is proportionate to the risk. For a scent evaluation, that means crushing a small piece and smelling it rather than tasting it.

The difference between taste and smell matters for interpretation. A taste standard records the material's action on the tongue and mouth — the burn, the numbing, the salivation. A smell standard records what reaches the nose. The two senses overlap, but they are not the same channel, and a root can be fierce on the tongue and modest in the air.

That gap explains why 细辛 reads as medicinal to a perfumer. The material's most obvious quality is a trigeminal bite, not a rounded aroma. A perfumer can use a sharp note, but a note that primarily stings is difficult to place in a composition. It does not blend; it interrupts.

The chain runs like this:

high pungency in the root → a strong trigeminal response on contact → the nose reads the material as a stimulus rather than a scent → the note dominates any blend it enters → the material behaves as a corrective or a medicine, not as a perfume component

This is a reading of how the material behaves in use, not a chemical assay. The historical record supplies the observation; the interpretation is editorial.

The lesson for anyone reading old drug entries is that a taste standard is not a botched perfume review. It is a different instrument for a different job. Measuring 细辛 against perfumery criteria is like judging a chisel by how well it spreads butter.

For this encyclopedia, the standard is narrower. The page records the classical criteria, translates them, and marks the boundary. It does not turn "味極辛" into a fragrance grade, and it does not pretend that a taste description can tell a modern buyer how a lot will smell when burned.

## The manuscript behind the entry

**Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* is a compilation, not a single author's field notes. Li Shizhen assembled it in the Ming dynasty from earlier materia-medica texts, and the entry for 细辛 carries quotations older than the book itself. Reading it as a compiled record explains both its precision and its gaps.

A compilation works by accumulation. An editor gathers what earlier texts say, arranges it under a heading, and adds commentary where the sources disagree. The result is a layered document: a name gloss from one source, a quality description from another, a warning quoted from a third. The 细辛 entry has exactly that texture. The aliases, the "thin root and extremely pungent taste" formula, and the 杜衡 warning do not all come from the same hand or the same century.

Read as layers, the entry becomes more trustworthy, not less. The compiler does not claim to have seen the plant; he preserves what earlier authorities wrote. When the text calls the confusion ancient, it quotes an older witness — which is exactly why the warning carries weight. A modern buyer inherits a failure mode that predates the Ming book.

The structure of the book matters too. The *Bencao Gangmu* is organized by category, and 细辛 sits in the grass section, 草之二. That placement is a classification decision, not a fragrance judgment. It tells a reader where the material belonged in the medical system, and it says nothing about whether the root was burned, worn, or used as a drug. A page that treats the placement as evidence of incense use has misread the genre.

There is a further limit. A compilation preserves what earlier writers chose to record. It does not preserve the everyday practice of people who never wrote anything down. The absence of 细辛 from the incense manuals is therefore a statement about the written record, not a claim about every household in every century. This page says "not documented" rather than "never used" for that reason, and the distinction is deliberate.

The method also explains why the entry is so concerned with recognition. In a trade where names travelled faster than plants, a text that described the root's form and color was doing the work of a quality standard. The compiler was not writing for a laboratory; he was writing for readers who had to judge a sack of roots by eye and by bite.

Read this way, the classical entry is less a verdict than a toolkit:

a name and its aliases → a sensory description → a look-alike warning → a dosing caution → a category placement

Each element answers a different question. Together they give a reader a way to ask, not a way to be certain. That is the most a historical text can do, and it is more than most modern product pages attempt.

## What the old standard leaves out

**Direct answer:** The classical markers describe a root's form, color, and bite. They do not describe moisture, storage history, mold, contamination, or the difference between a fresh lot and one that has sat in a warehouse for years. A modern buyer needs both sets of checks.

A sensory standard is a remarkable instrument. It can be applied without a laboratory, it travels in a sentence, and it catches the failure mode that mattered most in its own time: the substitution of a stand-in. What it cannot do is measure anything. It gives a verdict on appearance and intensity, not on the condition of the tissue.

Moisture is the first gap. A dried root that has taken up water is heavier, softer, and more vulnerable to mold, and the classical markers say nothing about it. The check is simple — squeeze, listen for a snap, look for dark patches — but it has to be done, because a clean-looking root can be wet inside.

Storage history is the second. A root kept in a damp room, or beside a strongly scented material, will carry the consequences in its smell. The old description assumes a clean, recently dried lot; it does not describe what happens after a year in a warm warehouse. A buyer who evaluates only the shape is evaluating the part of the history that survived.

Contamination is the third, and the most important. Soil, dust, mold, and residues are not visible in a hand specimen, and no amount of sensory skill resolves them. A supplier's documentation, a clean facility, and a willingness to answer questions do more than a beautiful root.

The gap is not a flaw in the old text. It is a difference in scope. The classical author was writing a recognition guide for a trade in which the material moved quickly and the buyer handled it in person. A modern buyer may be ordering from a photograph, through a chain of intermediaries, with weeks of storage in between. The old markers still work for what they were built to do; they simply do not cover the new questions.

Documentation is the modern substitute, and it has its own limits. A certificate can confirm that a lot was tested; it cannot tell you whether the sample tested was the sample shipped. A supplier audit can confirm that a facility is clean; it cannot confirm that a particular sack is clean. Paperwork narrows the risk. It does not remove it.

A single root is not a sample. A sack has a top, a middle, and a bottom, and the pieces on top are usually the ones the seller wants seen. Draw small handfuls from several depths, mix them, and judge the mixture. If the lot is small enough to see all of it, say so; if it is not, do not pretend that one handsome root represents a hundred kilos. This is not laboratory sampling theory, and it does not need to be. It is the difference between inspecting a product and admiring a display.

Grading also has a cost. A buyer who demands the finest roots pays for sorting labor; a buyer who demands a low price accepts a wider range. Neither position is wrong, and asking for both is. The old standard describes the top of the range, not the average of the sack. Read as a description of the best material, it is a useful benchmark. Read as a promise about every piece, it is a recipe for disappointment.

The buyer's own eye is the final gap. Uniform, dark, clean pieces look professional, and sorting can manufacture that impression. The old markers assumed a buyer with the material in hand; today the first view is often a selected photograph. Ask what the frame leaves out — the base of the sack, the dust, the broken pieces, the roots that never reached the top layer. A supplier who shows that view is worth more than one who stages it.

Two lists, then, and a lot has to survive both. The old one asks about form, color, pungency, and species. The new one asks about moisture, storage, contamination, and paperwork. Material that clears both is worth buying. Material that clears only the first is a handsome root with an unknown past.

## The classical record, read carefully

**Direct answer:** 细辛 appears in the *Bencao Gangmu*, 草之二, as a materia-medica root with a name gloss, a set of quality markers, and a dosing caution. The record is historical. It is not a modern clinical dossier, and this page does not translate it into one.

The *Bencao Gangmu* entry is compact. It records the aliases 小辛 and 少辛, explains the name through the quality of the root, and preserves the 杜衡 warning from Shen Kuo. The interesting part for a modern reader is how much of the entry is about identification. The classical author is not primarily selling the material; he is telling you how to recognize it and how not to be cheated.

The dosing caution is the other half of the record. The sources note that 细辛 is potent and was used in small amounts. In the materia-medica frame, that is a statement about the material's strength and the care required in handling it. It is not a dose recommendation for a modern reader, and it is not a formulation instruction for an incense maker. It is a historical observation about how the root was treated by people who used it.

| Claim | Confidence | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Species | Firm | *A. sieboldii* in Flora of China and GBIF |
| Name | Firm | 细 + 辛; old synonyms in the text |
| Impostor | Firm | 杜衡 described and warned against |
| Medical use | Traditional | the old drug literature, read as history |

The table is the honest summary of what this entry knows. Two rows are established or documented, one is a historical claim, one is an absence, and one is a boundary. A reader who wants a fragrance material can stop at the fourth row and look elsewhere; a reader interested in the history of Chinese materia medica can read the middle rows as history.

A historical text settles only historical questions. It can say how the root was described, how the name was built, and that caution surrounded its use. It cannot certify the sack in front of you, and it cannot tell a laboratory what the tissue contains. Keeping those questions apart is what makes the page usable as both history and reference.

## Safety: family context, handling, and smoke

**Direct answer:** 细辛 belongs to a plant family with a documented safety reputation, and its classical record carries a dosing caution. It is presented here for identification, not for use as a medicine or a burning fragrance; the page makes no medical claim and gives no dose.

The family context deserves to be stated plainly. The Aristolochiaceae is the family in which aristolochic acids occur, and that is the reason the family name carries weight in safety discussions. This page does not assay *Asarum sieboldii* for those compounds, does not claim that the species is free of them, and does not claim that it contains them. The honest position is that the family is a caution flag and the species-level question is beyond the scope of an identification entry.

The classical caution points in the same direction from a different angle. A material described as potent and used in small amounts is a material that was handled with care. That is not the same as a modern toxicological assessment, and it should not be presented as one. It is a historical signal that the root was not treated as a casual ingredient.

Smoke is its own problem. Burning anything fine enough produces particles and gases, and an acrid root adds an unpleasant edge to both. Use ventilation, a clean burner, and a safe distance; the [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case. This page does not recommend putting 细辛 on a coal at all.

The conclusion is a limit, not a scare label. Aromatic, old, and interesting are three true things about 细辛, and none of them makes it a fragrance ingredient, a medicine, or a safe experiment. The page reports the record and goes no further.

## What a buyer should ask

**Direct answer:** A buyer should ask for the species, the region, the plant part, and the form, and should expect the answers to be specific. "细辛" alone is a trade name, and a trade name is not an identity.

The questions below are the ones that change what arrives in the bag:

| Decision | What turns on it | Weak reply | Strong reply |
|---|---|---|---|
| Species? | drug root versus its double | "ordinary 细辛" | Latin binomial and district |
| Origin? | range and habitat shape the lot | "China" | province or collecting area |
| Parts? | old material is rootstock | "the roots" | rootstock, cleaned, dried |
| Sorted? | old markers assume a clean lot | "natural" | sorting and foreign-matter note |
| Market? | drug trade and fragrance trade differ | "both" | stated intended use |

The last question is the one that matters most for this entry. A supplier who sells 细辛 as a fragrance material is making a claim the classical record does not support. A supplier who sells it as a materia-medica root is at least naming the right category. The buyer's job is to notice which claim is being made.

Trade names deserve one more warning. A label that has lasted for centuries is a good search term and a bad specification. 细辛 contains a species question, and the old literature itself separates the genuine root from its double. Asking which plant is in the bag follows the older method rather than rejecting it.

The same discipline applies to photographs. A clean image can show color, shape, and uniformity, which are real signals. It cannot show pliancy, density, or the intensity of the bite, and those are the checks the classical description actually relies on. A listing built only on a photograph is a listing built on half the evidence.

## Editorial view: the discipline of the negative

**Direct answer:** 细辛 is the clearest case in this encyclopedia of a material whose value to a reader lies in what it is not. It is aromatic, classical, and well documented — and it is not an incense material. Saying that plainly is more useful than inventing a fragrance role the record does not support.

Three judgments follow, and they are editorial.

- **An absence can be the finding.** Ingredient pages usually explain a use. This one explains a limit, because the limit is what a formulator or buyer most needs to know. A fragrant root outside the burning canon is a reminder that "natural aromatic" and "incense material" describe different things, and that the gap is documented rather than invented.
- **Identification is the real subject.** The *Bencao Gangmu* entry spends its energy on how to recognize the root and how to avoid the substitute. That is a quality standard in the oldest sense: a description specific enough to be checked. A modern page can do worse than follow the method — name the plant, describe the markers, name the look-alike, and admit what cannot be checked from a photograph.
- **Safety context belongs in the identity, not the footer.** A birthwort-family root with a classical dosing caution is not a neutral botanical. Putting the family context at the end of the page, after the reader has already formed an impression, would be a rhetorical choice rather than an editorial one. The honest structure puts it early and repeats it where it matters.

Put plainly: this is a drug-literature root, not a perfumery material, and the page is stronger for saying so. The transferable rule is to establish which tradition a plant belongs to before writing its product copy, building a formula, or placing an order.

## Summary

Asarum (细辛) is a rootstock in the Aristolochiaceae, the birthwort family, and the old drug literature knows it as a small, wiry, purple-black root with a ferocious bite. The name says as much: fine and acrid. Its identity is pinned to *Asarum sieboldii*, with *A. heterotropoides* as a northern source and 杜衡 (*A. forbesii*) as the documented stand-in. Nothing in the sources consulted gives it a place in the incense or sachet canon, and the entry does not invent one.

## FAQ

**What is asarum (细辛)?** The dried root and rhizome of *Asarum sieboldii*, a low woodland herb of the birthwort family native to China, Korea, and Japan. The name means "thin and pungent," and the root is slender, deep purple, and intensely acrid. It is a classical materia-medica root, not a documented incense material.

**What does 细辛 mean?** 细 is "slender" and 辛 is "pungent" or "acrid." The two characters describe the root's form and its heat. The old text applies the same language to the genuine article — a fine root with a fierce taste — and records the synonyms 小辛 and 少辛.

**Is 细辛 the same as 杜衡?** No. *A. sieboldii* is wiry, supple, purple-black, and fierce; *A. forbesii* is pale, curved, brittle, and milder. The old text preserves the warning "杜衡亂細辛，自古已然矣" — the substitution is ancient, not a modern market trick.

**Is 细辛 used in incense or sachets?** Nothing consulted for this entry assigns either role. The texts treat it as a drug, its heat works on the mouth and sinuses, and no passage describes it on a coal or in a sachet. The claim is therefore left unmade. Fragrance in the plant does not imply fragrance in the burner.
**Can you identify 细辛 from a photo or a smell sample?** Not reliably. A photograph can show color and form but not pliancy, weight, or the intensity of the bite; a smell sample can be strong and still come from the wrong species. The classical checks are sensory, and they work best on a clean, physical lot with a species name attached.

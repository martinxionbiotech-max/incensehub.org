> **Direct answer:** Champaca in this entry means 白兰花 (báilánhuā), the white, heavily scented blossom of *Michelia alba* — now usually placed in *Magnolia* as *Magnolia × alba* — a magnolia-family evergreen cultivated from Vietnam and Thailand through the southern Chinese provinces. The blossom is sweet and heady with a fruity edge; it is worn on the body, layered into tea, sewn into sachets, and extracted for perfume work. In incense it behaves as an accent, never as a structural base.

> **Direct answer:** A cultivated white magnolia flower with a short fresh life, a strong sweet scent and a naming problem larger than any adulteration risk.

## A name that misleads in two directions

> **Direct answer:** The 兰 in 白兰花 does not mean orchid, and the English word "champaca" usually points at a different species than the one this page describes.

Start with the character. 兰 (lán) is used across Chinese plant names as an honorific for fragrance — 兰花 for orchids proper, 玉兰 for the yulan magnolia, 木兰 for the magnolia genus, 白兰花 for this flower. It signals that a plant smells good. It does not assign a botanical family. Anyone who reads 白兰花 as "white orchid" has followed the character and missed the plant: *Michelia alba* is a magnolia, a woody tree with fleshy petals and a fruity-floral chemistry nothing like an orchid's.

Then the English. "Champaca" entered perfumery as the name of *Magnolia champaca*, the golden-flowered species (黄兰, huánglán), whose absolute is a recognised material in white-floral accords. 白兰花 is *Michelia alba*, the white species. The two are close relatives in the same family — often discussed as parent and hybrid — and their names have been used loosely for a century. They are not the same flower, and an oil sold as "champaca absolute" is not automatically an oil of 白兰花.

That single fact reorganises how this page has to be read. Everything about the flower's cultural life in southern China belongs to 白兰花. Much of the perfumery language that surrounds the word "champaca" belongs to the golden species. A buyer who does not separate the two is specifying a mood, not a material.

## Four flowers in the white-magnolia cluster

> **Direct answer:** 白兰花, 黄兰, 玉兰 and 含笑花 are four distinct magnolia-family flowers that get confused in trade and in garden writing.

| Chinese name | Botanical name | What it is | Scent character | Where the confusion bites |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 白兰花, 白兰 | *Michelia alba* (*Magnolia × alba*) | White-flowered evergreen, cultivated | Sweet, heady, fruity white-floral | The subject of this page; often mislabelled "white orchid" |
| 黄兰 | *Magnolia champaca* (*Michelia champaca*) | Golden-flowered relative | Deep, sweet, slightly tea-like | The usual plant behind English "champaca" products |
| 玉兰 | *Magnolia denudata* | Deciduous spring magnolia | Pale, lemony, lighter | Sold as 白玉兰 in some markets, which muddles labels |
| 含笑花 | *Michelia figo* (*Magnolia figo*) | Small evergreen shrub | Banana-and-pear sweetness | Called a "white magnolia" in garden writing |

The table is also a warning about Chinese retail names. 白玉兰 is used in some southern markets as a loose synonym for 白兰花, but in botanical Chinese it properly names *Magnolia denudata*, the spring-flowering deciduous magnolia. Two different trees, one character apart. On a label, 白玉兰 should be treated as ambiguous until the binomial appears.

What follows from this is a discipline rather than a fact: for any purchase above sample size, ask for the genus and species in Latin. Common names in this cluster have never been stable, and no amount of local expertise substitutes for the binomial.

## What the flower actually is

> **Direct answer:** A creamy-white, multi-petalled blossom on an evergreen tree, borne through the warm season and picked by hand while it is still firm.

*Michelia alba* is an evergreen tree of moderate size, grown in southern China, Vietnam, Thailand and across the tropical belt for exactly one product: its flowers. The bloom is white to cream, with long, narrow, fleshy petals arranged around a central receptacle, and it opens progressively rather than all at once, which is why a tree can be picked repeatedly over a season instead of cropped once.

The × in *Magnolia × alba* is not decoration. It marks the taxon as a hybrid, which fits its history: the tree is known from cultivation, not from a wild population, and is generally treated as a cross involving *Magnolia champaca*. Botanists have moved it between *Michelia* and *Magnolia* as the limits of the magnolia family have been redrawn, which is why both names still appear on labels and in nurseries. Neither name is wrong; the synonymy is simply unsettled in common use.

The practical consequence for a buyer is small but real. A supplier quoting *Michelia alba* and a supplier quoting *Magnolia × alba* are describing the same tree. A supplier quoting only 白玉兰 may be describing a different one. See the [yulan magnolia entry](/ingredients/yulan-magnolia/) for the deciduous namesake, and [michelia-figo](/ingredients/michelia-figo/) for the banana-scented shrub that completes the cluster.

## Why the scent behaves the way it does

> **Direct answer:** The fragrance is concentrated in thin, watery petals that give it up fast, so the fresh blossom is the aromatic peak and every processing step is a subtraction.

A petal is a short-lived organ. It is thin, poorly protected against water loss, and loaded with volatile compounds whose job is to attract pollinators during a brief window. Once the flower is picked, that window closes quickly:

> hand-picked blossom → respiration continues, water leaves the thin petals → the edges brown and the tissue collapses → the light, fruity top notes break down first → what remains is a heavier, sweeter, slightly stewed floral

This is why the flower is a same-day material in the fresh trade and why drying changes its character rather than merely preserving it. The dried flower is still pleasant — deeper, softer, closer to a dried-fruit floral — but it is no longer the scent that made the tree worth planting. Anyone who has smelled 白兰花 on a warm evening and then smelled the dried petals in a warehouse has met the difference.

The comparison with its neighbours is instructive. Gardenia reads creamy and lactonic; jasmine reads sharp and animalic-indolic at the top; ylang-ylang reads rubbery-sweet and heavy. 白兰花 sits closer to a fruit than to any of them — sweet, rounded, with a lift that reads almost like ripe pear. That is the note a formulator is buying, and it is the note most easily lost.

| Floral | Core impression | Weight | Behaviour when dried |
|---|---|---|---|
| 白兰花 (*M. alba*) | Sweet, fruity, rounded | Medium-heavy | Mutes quickly; deepens and loses lift |
| 黄兰 (*M. champaca*) | Deeper, tea-like, slightly spicy | Heavy | Holds better; absolute is the standard form |
| Gardenia | Creamy, lactonic, green-edged | Heavy | Darkens and turns cheesy |
| Jasmine | Sharp, indolic, bright | Heavy | Survives drying better than most |
| Ylang-ylang | Rubber-sweet, banana-like | Very heavy | Oil is the only practical form |

## From tree to market

> **Direct answer:** The supply chain is horticultural and perishable — hand-picked flowers moved fast — which is why this is a regional fresh-flower culture and an export trade in dried petals and extracts.

Every aromatic material has a supply shape, and the shape explains the culture around it. Resins are traded in lumps that keep for years. Heartwoods are traded in kilograms that keep for decades. A champaca flower keeps for hours.

Three consequences follow:

1. **Harvest is by hand and by eye.** Petals bruise, and a bruised petal browns within the day. Pickers take blossoms at the right stage — open enough to be scented, firm enough to survive handling.
2. **The fresh market is local.** Flowers are sold in small bunches in southern Chinese cities in season, worn on the person or hung in a room, because there is no way to move them far while they are still themselves.
3. **The exportable forms are the processed ones.** Dried petals, powder and extract travel; fresh blossoms do not. A foreign buyer is therefore always buying a transformed material, and should judge it as such.

That is the honest reading of the flower's regional fame. Southern China's attachment to 白兰花 is not a marketing story layered onto an export commodity; it is what a perishable, intensely scented flower produces in a warm city — a daily, local, personal fragrance. The cultural weight and the physical perishability are the same fact seen from two sides.

## Why it is a summer flower

> **Direct answer:** Heat drives the volatiles out of the petals, so the flower is loudest exactly when it is least able to travel.

Fragrance release is a physical process, and temperature governs it. A warm petal gives off more molecules per hour than a cool one, which is why a single stem can perfume a room on a hot evening and barely register in an air-conditioned one. Humidity works in the same direction: moist air holds aromatic molecules longer, so a warm, humid street carries the scent further than a dry, cool one.

That has three practical consequences, and all of them shape the trade:

- **The season is short and local.** Blossoms arrive in the hot months, in quantity, and must be moved the same day. The trade is built around proximity, not around storage.
- **Warm storage is destructive.** A sack of dried petals left in a hot warehouse loses its top notes faster than the same sack kept cool and dark. Heat does the same damage to dried petals that it does to the fresh flower, only more slowly.
- **Cold mutes the material.** A chilled blossom smells of very little until it warms in the hand. Anyone judging a sample straight out of a cold room is judging the temperature, not the flower.

The sensory corollary is worth stating for anyone who has only met the flower as a dried powder: 白兰花 is one of the materials whose reputation is inseparable from its climate. The scent people remember is a hot-evening scent, and no dried form fully reproduces it.

## Propagated by hand, not by seed

> **Direct answer:** The tree is a cultivated hybrid that is propagated vegetatively, which is why named forms are clones and why the flower crop is uniform within a line.

*Magnolia × alba* is known from cultivation rather than from a wild population, and it is generally treated as a hybrid involving *Magnolia champaca*. Hybrids of this kind often set little viable seed, and the tree is accordingly raised by vegetative means — grafting onto a rootstock, or air-layering a selected branch. The consequence is that a named line is a clone: every tree in it carries the same flowering behaviour, the same petal shape and the same scent profile.

For a buyer, that has a specific meaning. Variation between suppliers is not random genetic drift within 白兰花; it is variation between different clonal lines, different rootstocks, different soils and, above all, different harvest and drying practice. Two lots that both test as *M. alba* can smell noticeably different without either being misidentified.

For a grower, the same fact explains the economics. A tree that must be propagated by hand is slower and more expensive to establish than one grown from seed, which is why the flower trade sits with established horticultural regions rather than spreading wherever the climate would allow. The plant is easy to love and not especially easy to multiply.

## Scenting tea with 白兰花

> **Direct answer:** Blossoms are layered against finished tea leaf so that volatiles migrate into the leaf, then taken out before storage; the flower is a reagent, not an ingredient.

Tea scenting belongs to the classical aromatic technique of southern China, and 白兰花 sits in the same practice family as jasmine and osmanthus. The mechanism is straightforward physical chemistry rather than mystique:

> dry tea leaf + fresh blossoms → the leaf adsorbs volatiles from the flower → repeated layering builds intensity → the spent flowers are removed → the leaf holds the note, the flower does not ship with it

The removal step is not cosmetic. A fresh flower left in contact with tea adds moisture, and moisture in stored leaf invites mould and staleness. Scenting is therefore a timed operation: enough contact to transfer aroma, not enough to spoil the leaf. That is why the technique is a craft with a season and a vocabulary rather than a recipe anyone can run from a datasheet.

For a reader trying to understand the flower's standing, the tea connection explains something the incense connection cannot. 白兰花 is famous in southern China because it perfumed a daily drink and a daily pocket, not because it perfumed a temple. Its prestige is domestic and sensory, and that is a different kind of prestige from the classical 香材 (incense-material) canon, which is textual, ritual and much older.

## Putting champaca into a burnable blend

> **Direct answer:** Dried champaca petals are a low-resin floral accent; the extract is a concentrated top-and-heart note; neither supplies the structure a stick needs.

A petal is cellulose, sugar, water and a small volatile load. It contains no resin duct and no gum, so when it burns it chars and collapses rather than sustaining an ember. The chain is predictable:

> dried petal powder → little resin or gum → the char path depends entirely on the base → the volatile top releases early in the burn → the second half of the stick is the base's character, not the flower's

This is why a "champaca" incense can smell wonderful in the box and thin in the room. The flower did its work before the first third was finished. It is also why the material is usually dosed as a modifier — a few percent to soften a citrus or to round a woody heart — rather than as a headline ingredient.

The extract behaves on a different curve. A champaca absolute or oil carries far more odour per gram, and it sits higher in the burn because it is more volatile than any wood. Loaded onto a finished stick, it gives a strong opening and fades; blended into the fragrance charge with a fixative, it lasts longer. Either way, the oil is a top-to-heart material, and the base still has to be built from something else.

There is a third, less discussed option: using the flower as a *theme* rather than a material. A stick can be built on a white-floral accord — a small amount of real extract plus supporting aromatics — and named for champaca without containing much dried petal at all. That is legitimate practice as long as the label does not imply otherwise. It becomes a problem only when a buyer assumes the name on the box describes the contents of the stick. The general screening logic for that assumption sits in the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

## What it pairs with

> **Direct answer:** 白兰花 blends best with citrus tops, other white florals and soft woods; it fights with anything sharply green, smoky or medicinal.

A fruit-leaning white floral is a sociable material. It sits naturally beside the citrus family, where the shared fruit facet makes the transition invisible, and beside sandalwood, where the creamy wood gives the flower something to sit on. It is less comfortable next to heavy smoke, camphoraceous herbs or resins with a strong terpenic edge, which flatten the fruit note and leave an odd sweetness behind.

| Partner | What it does for champaca | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Bergamot, sweet orange | Reinforces the fruity lift | Citrus tops fade first; the flower can outlive them |
| Jasmine | Builds a full white-floral heart | Jasmine's indolic edge can overpower the softer fruit |
| Gardenia | Adds creaminess and weight | Two heavy florals can turn cloying fast |
| Sandalwood | Gives the flower a base to rest on | The wood can read dry against a watery floral |
| Vetiver | Adds a dry, earthy counterweight | Too much turns the blend bitter |
| Tea absolutes | Echoes the flower's own tea-scenting history | Can muddy the fruit note if overdone |
| Heavy smoke or birch tar | — | Flattens the top and leaves a harsh sweetness |

The table is a starting point, not a rule. The useful discipline is to test the pair at the ratio you intend to use, because a white floral that behaves well at a trace can dominate at ten times that dose. Champaca is not a material that improves by being made louder.

## Two champacas, two trades

> **Direct answer:** The golden species supplies most of the perfumery absolute; the white species supplies the fresh-flower culture of southern China.

It helps to hold the two markets apart, because they answer different questions. *Magnolia champaca*, 黄兰, is the species that built the perfumery material called champaca: a deep, sweet, slightly tea-like flower whose absolute is dark, rich and long-lasting, used in white-floral accords where a heavier, more resinous sweetness is wanted. *Michelia alba*, 白兰花, is the species that built a daily-life culture: a lighter, fruitier white flower, sold in bunches, worn, and used to scent tea.

A formulator who wants the perfumery effect and a reader who wants the southern-Chinese flower are therefore not talking about the same raw material, even when both say "champaca". Neither is superior. The golden species is the better extractive; the white species is the better fresh flower, and its dried petals are a gentler, more delicate incense accent.

The practical question to put to a supplier is short: which binomial, which plant part, and which extraction route. Those three answers separate the two trades more reliably than any description of the scent.

## Working with the extract

> **Direct answer:** Absolute is dark, thick and powerful; it needs dilution before evaluation and a fixative before it will hold in a blend.

Solvent extraction of delicate petals yields an absolute rather than a steam-distilled oil, because steam heat would cook the fruit note out of the flower. The result is a dark, viscous material with a scent far denser than the fresh blossom — closer to dried fruit and honey than to the airy flower on the tree.

Three working notes follow:

1. **Dilute before you judge.** A neat absolute on a smelling strip is not the material as used. Evaluated at a working dilution, the fruit note reappears and the heaviness recedes.
2. **Give it a fixative.** The extract is volatile in the top and heart. Without a heavier material underneath, it lifts off the substrate and leaves the base bare.
3. **Expect colour migration.** Dark absolutes can stain paper, wax and pale powders. In a light-coloured product, that is a formulation constraint rather than a defect.

A buyer comparing two absolutes should compare them at the same dilution on the same substrate, and should ask for the species on the certificate. Those two habits remove most of the ambiguity that surrounds this material in the perfume trade.

## Fresh, dried, powdered, extracted

> **Direct answer:** Four forms, four different jobs; choosing the wrong one is a more common failure than choosing a poor lot.

| End use | Form to specify | What to state on the order | The failure to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wearing the flower | Fresh blossoms, opened but firm | Harvest day, bunch size, no bruising | Flowers picked too open, browning within hours |
| Scenting tea | Fresh blossoms, clean and dry | Food-contact handling, no spray residue | Wet flowers, or flowers left in the leaf too long |
| Sachets and display | Dried whole blossoms | Drying method, colour retention | Brittle, shattered petals; faded colour |
| Incense sticks and cones | Dried petal powder, stated mesh | Sieve size, moisture, stem fraction | Powder too fine to burn evenly, or too coarse to blend |
| Perfumery | Absolute or essential oil | Species, extraction method, lot analysis | "Champaca" oil that is actually the golden species |
| Candles, diffusers, soaps | Oil or compounded fragrance | Carrier compatibility, IFRA-style documentation | Top note flashing off in the carrier |

The pattern in that table is that the flower's value migrates with the form. Fresh blossoms are a local, seasonal, personal pleasure. Dried petals are a shelf-stable aromatic. Powder is a manufacturing input. Extract is a perfumery material with its own market and its own documentation. A supplier who is excellent in one of those lines is not automatically competent in another, which is why the form should be settled before the price is discussed.

For powder specifically, two variables decide how the material behaves in production: particle size and moisture. Finer powder distributes more evenly but can choke the burn; coarser powder keeps the char path open but releases aroma unevenly. The right target depends on the base, the binder and the equipment, so a mesh figure from another factory is a starting point and not an answer. The [incense powder and resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/) overview covers how floral powders behave in a burnable mix, and [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) covers where the grinding sits in the process.

## Storage and shelf life, form by form

> **Direct answer:** Every form of this flower is on a clock, and the clock runs at a different speed for each one.

| Form | Best kept | Realistic holding | What goes wrong first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh blossoms | Cool, damp, unsealed, used the same day | Hours | Edge browning, then a stewed note |
| Dried whole petals | Airtight, dark, cool, no strong odours nearby | Months, if dried properly | Fading top note, then brittleness |
| Petal powder | Sealed, cool, used soon after grinding | Weeks to a few months | Loss of fruit note; clumping from moisture |
| Absolute or oil | Full dark bottle, minimal headspace, cool | Years, with slow drift | Oxidation; the top turns heavy and honeyed |

The reason for the spread is surface area. A whole petal exposes little tissue to air; powder exposes almost all of it. That is why a powder that smelled vivid on the day it was milled can be flat a season later while the whole petals from the same sack are still pleasant. Grinding is convenient for the blender and costly for the aroma.

The storage rule that follows is unglamorous and effective: buy powder in the quantity you will use, keep whole petals for stock, and treat the extract as the long-lived form. A producer who holds all three is not being fussy — each is doing a different job at a different timescale, and matching the form to the turnover is most of the discipline.

## What the label should say

> **Direct answer:** A usable champaca label carries six things, and the first of them is the binomial.

Trade labels in this material are usually short and often vague. A buyer who sets out the required fields in advance gets a comparable quotation instead of a poem:

- **Binomial.** *Michelia alba* or *Magnolia × alba* — not 白兰花 alone, and never 白玉兰 without the Latin.
- **Plant part.** Flower only, with a stated tolerance for stem, leaf and calyx.
- **Form and grade of division.** Whole dried petals, powder with a declared mesh, or extract with its extraction route.
- **Harvest window.** The season, so a buyer can judge freshness against the calendar rather than the sales copy.
- **Drying or extraction method.** Shade dried, sun dried or machine dried for petals; solvent extraction for absolute.
- **Lot identity.** A code that connects the sample to the shipment.

Two of those fields do most of the defensive work. The binomial removes the 黄兰 and 玉兰 problems in one line. The lot identity makes a sample meaningful, because a sample without a lot code is a promise about a past batch rather than evidence about a future one.

What a label cannot tell you is whether the material will work in your base. That question is answered by a trial, and a trial needs enough material to run more than once. Buying a large lot on the strength of a small, beautiful sample is the standard way this material disappoints.

## Judging a lot

> **Direct answer:** Species, condition, scent and cleanliness, in that order; a numeric grade does not exist for this material, and anyone quoting one is inventing a scale.

| Check | What you are looking for | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Michelia alba* named in Latin, not just 白兰花 | That the lot matches the sample |
| Colour | Cream-white, even, no brown edges | Age since picking |
| Condition | Whole petals, no shattering, no compression damage | Drying temperature |
| Scent | Sweet and fruity at arm's length, not musty | Absence of added fragrance |
| Cleanliness | No leaf, stem, insect or dust fraction | Residue status |
| Moisture | Dry to the touch, no clumping in the sack | Storage history before dispatch |
| Powder | Stated mesh, even distribution, free-running | Behaviour in your specific base |
| Extract | Species, method, lot documentation | Suitability for your carrier |

Two of these deserve emphasis. The species check comes first because every other observation is meaningless if you are holding 黄兰 or 玉兰. The scent check comes second because it is the only one that tests the thing you are buying — and it must be done at arm's length on a small quantity, not by burying your nose in a sample, which overwhelms the nose and flatters poor material.

What this material does not have is a grading literature. There is no recognised scale of grades, no auction system and no accepted vocabulary of "first flush" or "superior". Quality talk around 白兰花 is sensory and local. That is not a weakness; it simply means the buyer's own protocol replaces the certificate.

## Traditional standing and the evidence line

> **Direct answer:** The aromatic and cultural uses are documented; the medicinal language attached to the flower is traditional, and this page treats it as such.

Three uses are well documented in the flower's home region: the fresh blossom worn for scent, the flower used to scent tea, and the petals used in sachets and floral incense blends. All three are aromatic or cultural, and all three can be described without any health claim.

A fourth layer exists in the materia medica tradition, where 白兰花 appears in the language of 芳香化湿 — aromatics that "transform dampness". That is traditional belief expressed in a classical medical idiom. It is not clinical evidence, this page does not repeat it as fact, and no reader should treat a scented flower as a remedy. The distinction is not pedantry: the moment a cultural practice is restated as a therapeutic claim, an honest ingredient page becomes a misleading one.

What can be said with confidence is narrower and more interesting. The flower's hold on southern Chinese daily life comes from its behaviour — it is generous in the heat, it perfumes a room from a single stem, and it fades within a day. That is a fragrance story, and it needs no medical framing to be worth telling.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The risks are smoke from burning and intensity from handling; neither is a medical claim, and both are manageable.

Smoke is the first thing to think about with any burning aromatic, and champaca is no exception. Champaca does not change that arithmetic. Burn in a ventilated space, keep the source away from anything that can catch, do not let children or pets near a lit stick, and do not sit in a closed room with a smouldering cone for hours. The site's [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case.

Handling is the second point. Fresh 白兰花 is intensely scented, and a large bunch in a small closed room can be overwhelming before it is dangerous. Dried petals can carry dust and fine plant fragments, so people with sensitive airways should handle them in a ventilated space. As with any aromatic material, keep essential oil away from eyes and do not apply it neat to skin.

No part of this page is medical advice, and none of the traditional uses described above should be read as treatment.

## An editorial view

> **Direct answer:** Naming is the real risk in this material, freshness is not a universal virtue, and the regional fame of 白兰花 is not a quality grade.

**The naming trap is the whole risk.** This is not an expensive aromatic that attracts sophisticated faking. It is a flower with a muddled name in two languages at once — 兰 that is not an orchid, 白玉兰 that may be another tree, "champaca" that usually means the golden species. Almost every bad purchase in this material traces back to a name that was never pinned down. That is a documentation problem, and it is cheap to solve.

**Fresh is not automatically better.** For wearing and tea scenting, fresh is the point. For incense, fresh is a liability: the petals carry water, they brown, and they cannot be ground evenly. The dried and extracted forms are not degraded versions of the fresh flower — they are different materials with different jobs. A buyer who insists on "fresh" for a burnable product has misunderstood the form, not raised the standard.

**Regional fame does not grade a lot.** The flower's standing in southern China is real and old, and it says something about the scent and the culture. It says nothing about the sack in front of you. Those are separate questions, and mixing them is how a mediocre lot gets sold on a good story.

**Unless the species is stated, the price means little.** This is the one hard line in the file. A quotation for "champaca" without a binomial is a quotation for an idea. With the binomial, the same number becomes comparable across suppliers.

## FAQ

**What is champaca (白兰花)?** In this entry, the cream-white blossom of *Michelia alba*, a magnolia-family tree of southern Chinese and Southeast Asian gardens. The scent is sweet and heady with a soft fruit edge, and the flower's working life runs through tea scenting, sachets and modern floral incense.

**Is 白兰花 an orchid?** No. Here 兰 works as an honorific for anything fragrant, not as a statement of plant family. 白兰花 is a magnolia, and it should not be confused with 玉兰 (*Magnolia denudata*), the deciduous magnolia of early spring.

**Is champaca the same as 黄兰?** Not quite. In English perfumery, "champaca" most often refers to *Magnolia champaca*, the golden-flowered species (黄兰). 白兰花 is *Michelia alba*, the white species. Both are magnolia-family, and their extracts are related but not interchangeable, so the binomial on the label is what settles it.

**Why does my champaca incense smell weak?** Usually because the dried petal fraction is high and the base is doing the burning. Petal powder has little resin, so the floral note releases early and the rest of the stick is base and binder. A small addition of extract, or a lower flower fraction over a better base, changes the result more than buying a "stronger" batch.

**Does 白兰花 have medicinal uses?** Traditional materia-medica writing gives the flower aromatic uses, and that language is recorded here as traditional belief. It is not established clinical evidence, and this page makes no medical claim.

- [Magnolia × alba (syn. Michelia alba) on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/3987892) — the current placement of the white champaca in *Magnolia*.
- [Michelia alba on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/5635329) — the long-used name under which the tree is traded and described.

Both records support the same identification: one tree, two names, with the generic boundary between *Michelia* and *Magnolia* still unsettled in common usage. The cultural and aromatic uses described above are documented practice in southern China, not laboratory findings. The materia-medica layer is marked as traditional throughout.

## Summary

白兰花 (*Michelia alba*, syn. *Magnolia × alba*) is a cultivated white magnolia, and its blossom is among the scents that define summer in southern China: sweet, heady, faintly fruity, and at its best within hours of picking. It goes on the body, into tea, into sachets and into the extractor, and in perfumery the word "champaca" more often names its golden relative *Magnolia champaca*. In incense it is an accent with a short arc — a floral top over a base built from something else — and in trade its difficulty is nomenclature rather than adulteration. Pin the binomial, choose the form for the job, and the material is as straightforward as it is beautiful.

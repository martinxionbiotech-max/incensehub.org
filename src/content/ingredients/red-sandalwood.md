> **Direct answer:** 紫檀 (zǐtán) is the crimson core of *Pterocarpus santalinus*, an Indian legume. Three traits made it valuable — it is heavy enough to carve, red enough to dye with, and sweet enough to scent a room when warmed. Incense makers treat it as a supporting material rather than a lead note.

Most writing about 紫檀 collapses those three properties into a single story about "sandalwood," and the collapse produces most of the errors that follow. A carver, a dyer, and a formulator are not buying the same thing. They are buying hardness, colour, and aroma respectively, from a material that happens to supply all three in varying proportions. Once that is clear, the grading arguments, the substitution problem, and the strange history of the name all become easier to follow.

## Key facts

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Which plant? | *Pterocarpus santalinus*, a legume tree (Fabaceae) |
| Which part is used? | The dark inner wood; the pale outer wood is thrown away |
| Where does it grow? | Dry forest in southern India, chiefly the Eastern Ghats |
| Why is it red? | Santalin, a red extractive deposited in the inner wood |
| What does it smell like? | Softly woody and sweet, with a rosy-spicy edge on warming |
| Is trade restricted? | Yes — CITES Appendix II since 2019 |
| What else is it called? | 紫檀木, 紫檀香 (loosely), red sanders, red sanderswood |

The alias list is worth reading twice. "Red sanders" is the name used in the timber trade for the same species, and it appears in export documents, sawmill inventories, and customs paperwork. A buyer who knows the material only as "red sandalwood" may fail to recognise it under the name it actually travels with.

## Three trades, three buyers

> **Direct answer:** The same heartwood is graded differently by a carver, a dye or lacquer worker, and an incense maker. Each is buying a different property, so a batch that fails one buyer's test may be perfectly sound for another.

This is not a detail. Most confusion about "quality" in 紫檀 comes from mixing three specifications into one word.

| User | Buys for | Rejects on | Can tolerate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cabinetmaker or carver | Blocks that are sound, heavy, and large enough to yield a part | Splits, brittle patches, run-out grain, sapwood hidden inside | Weak smell; slight shade differences between boards |
| Dyer or lacquer worker | Wood rich enough in colour to give a strong bath | Thin, pale, young material; mixed species in the sack | Knots, figure, block size, lack of aroma |
| Incense formulator | Clean meal that smells sweet on heat and burns predictably | Grit, bark, fungal stain, stale or foreign odours, erratic mesh | Big knots, showy grain, mild banding in colour |

One trade's reject is another's raw material: the crooked log a cabinetmaker refuses may mill perfectly, and the shavings a joiner sweeps up are exactly what a dyer wants. So "grade" without a stated use says nothing. Ask what the grade is for before arguing about what it is worth.

The three markets also explain why the material's reputation is unstable. Furniture buyers drove one set of preferences — size, figure, darkness — and those preferences leaked into incense writing, where colour and density became shorthand for aromatic quality. The shorthand is useful but not exact, because the aromatic fraction and the pigment fraction are not the same thing.

## What the material is, physically

> **Direct answer:** It is heartwood — the dead inner core of the stem — and nothing else on the tree is traded. The pale outer band is waste.

A heartwood is the dead, inner wood of a mature stem. As a tree ages, the inner wood fills with extractives — compounds the living tree deposits into the already-conducted xylem — and those deposits change its colour, density, and resistance to decay. In *Pterocarpus santalinus* the extractive load is heavy and intensely red, and the resulting wood is hard enough to be worked like a fine furniture timber rather than a soft aromatic chip.

Downstream, everything follows from that density. Water penetrates slowly, polish sits high, shapes hold, and grinding takes patience. Combustion is sluggish for the same reason. As for the colour, it is carried through the tissue rather than painted on, so a genuine block stays red where it is cut while a stained imitation reveals pale fibre beneath a scratch.

## Colour and scent are separate properties

> **Direct answer:** The red pigment and the aromatic volatiles are two different fractions of the same wood. A deep red piece is not automatically more fragrant, and a fragrant piece is not automatically dark. Grading that treats colour as a proxy for scent is grading the wrong variable.

This is the single most useful distinction on the page, and it is the one most often collapsed in sales language. The pigment — santalin — is a relatively heavy, non-volatile extractive. It stays in the wood and in the powder. The aromatic impression comes from volatile compounds that escape when the wood is warmed. The two can be correlated in a good batch because both accumulate in old, well-developed heartwood, but the correlation is not a law.

Practical consequence: when a supplier says "very red, very fragrant," ask which property is being specified. If the answer is colour, the material may be excellent for colour work and ordinary for scent. If the answer is scent, the piece may be a lighter, younger heartwood that smells fine and looks brown. Neither is fraud. Treating one number as a proxy for the other is where buyers get hurt.

Mechanism, in the simplest form:

- **Old heartwood → more extractive deposition → deeper colour and higher density → slower, steadier burn.**
- **Young or sapwood-contaminated material → less deposition → paler colour, lower density → faster, harsher burn with more of the "wood smoke" note.**

That chain explains why the traditional grading instinct — heavy, dark, close-grained — is not mere aesthetics. It is a proxy for age and extractive load.

## Reading colour without a laboratory

> **Direct answer:** Judge colour on a fresh, cut surface in daylight, against a reference you trust. Photographs, polished faces, and indoor lighting all lie, and a red surface does not prove a red core.

Colour is the property most often discussed and least often measured. Five habits make the judgement more reliable.

First, expose fresh material. Wax, polish, and handling all sit on the surface, so a knife cut on a hidden face tells you more than any finished side. Second, take the piece to daylight. Indoor lamps shift brown toward red and red toward orange; a north-facing window is unflattering and therefore useful. Third, wet a small patch on the fresh cut and watch how the colour behaves — surface stain and through-colour look different when the wood is damp. Wipe it dry afterwards. Fourth, compare with a reference chip of known identity, because colour memory is unreliable across minutes, let alone across shipments. Fifth, remember that powder and solid are not comparable: fine particles scatter light, so the same wood reads duller as meal than as a block.

There is a naming nuance behind the colour question. 紫 is usually translated "purple," but in this context it covers a range from deep violet-red to near-black crimson. Genuine old heartwood can look almost black in a polished face and blood-red in a fresh cut. A bright, uniform orange-red is a different signal — it may indicate younger wood, or a surface colourant. That reading is editorial judgement, not a laboratory test, and it should be checked against the other signals rather than trusted alone.

The same caution applies to oxidation. A long-exposed face darkens and dulls; a freshly planed face is brighter. Comparing two pieces that have aged differently is comparing two surfaces, not two woods.

## Why two pieces of the same species differ

> **Direct answer:** Species identity fixes the range of possibilities, not the contents of a particular block. Age, position in the stem, growing conditions, drying history, and storage all move colour, density, and aroma within that range.

A buyer who expects consistency from a botanical name will be surprised by 紫檀. The name guarantees a species; it does not guarantee a specification. The variables that matter most are ordinary ones:

Age comes first. The inner wood darkens and gains extractives over decades, so an old stem carries a thick, heavy, deeply coloured core while a young one carries a thin pale centre. End grain shows the proportion at a glance.

Position in the stem matters almost as much. Wood from near the pith and wood from near the bark differ in weight and in the way the grain runs, and a plank sawn across the stem can contain both — which is why one board may look patchy without being defective.

Growth conditions leave their own record. Ring width and density reflect rainfall, soil, and competition; slow-grown stock tends to be heavier and more uniform, while fast-grown stock is not necessarily bad but works and burns differently.

Drying can ruin good wood. Checks, case-hardening, and discolouration are process faults rather than tree faults, and a split blank is often evidence of a bad kiln schedule rather than a bad log.

Storage finishes the job. Light, air, and handling alter the surface year by year, so two boards from the same stem can look like different materials after a decade in different rooms.

So describe the piece, not just the plant. "Heartwood only, air-dried, even crimson through the cut, no sapwood band" tells a supplier what to select; "premium 紫檀" tells them only what to write on the invoice.

## Reading end grain

> **Direct answer:** A clean end-grain face shows the anatomy that a side face hides: the band of pale sapwood, the density of pores, the pattern of rays, and the width of growth rings. For a dense hardwood, that face is the most informative surface in the sample.

Wood is a bundle of longitudinal cells. In hardwoods, some of those cells are wide vessels — the pores that carry water in the living tree — and their size and arrangement differ between species. A fresh end-grain cut, planed or scraped sharp, shows those pores as pinpricks or as lines. It also shows rays, the radial ribbons of tissue that appear as flecks on a quarter-sawn face.

For 紫檀, the useful readings are comparative rather than absolute. A dense, slow-grown heartwood shows closely packed tissue with fine pores and narrow rings. A faster-grown piece shows wider rings and a more open texture. Neither reading identifies the species on its own — that requires botanical work — but both tell you whether the piece is the kind of material the seller claims.

The sapwood band is the easiest thing to see and the most useful. On a genuine heartwood block, the pale outer band is clearly demarcated and can be trimmed away. On a block that has been stained to look uniform, the boundary may be blurred, or the pale wood may appear in the middle of a sawn face where a stain could not reach.

Grain direction matters for another reason. A block sawn with the grain running out of the side will tear when worked and may split under load. A carver reads the run-out before buying; an incense maker reads it before grinding, because a splintered block produces splinters in the meal.

## What "sweet" means in a wood

> **Direct answer:** Sweetness in incense writing is a metaphor borrowed from taste, not a sugar content. It describes a rounded, soft, slightly floral impression that lacks the sharp or bitter edges of smoke, and it is a perception rather than a measurable property.

The word does a lot of work in aromatic description, and it is worth pinning down. A "sweet" wood is not sugary. It is a wood whose volatile profile, when warmed, produces an impression that reads as soft and rounded rather than dry, acrid, or medicinal. The same word appears for sandalwood, for benzoin, for tonka, and for 紫檀 — four materials with nothing chemically in common.

This matters for judging 紫檀 because sweetness is the note most often claimed and least often demonstrated. A raw block rarely smells sweet at room temperature; the impression appears on warming. A seller who invites you to smell a cold, polished piece is showing you dust and finish, not the aromatic character. The test is a gentle heat on a clean, freshly exposed surface, away from the nose, with the first smoky moment discounted.

The metaphor also has a limit. 紫檀 is sweet in a low, woody register, not in the creamy, lactonic way of sandalwood. If the sweetness you detect is bright and almost floral, check whether the sample has been doctored or whether you are smelling a different material.

## Aroma: what 紫檀 actually contributes

> **Direct answer:** Warm it gently and you get soft wood sweetness with a rosy-spicy edge. Put flame to it and the note collapses into ordinary smoke.

The scent has three phases worth knowing. The first is a dry, woody warmth that appears before any visible smoke — the wood is warming, not combusting. The second is the sweet-rosy middle, which is where the material earns its place in a blend. The third, if the heat is too high, is ordinary wood smoke: acrid, tarry, and capable of burying everything else in the formula.

The lesson for a maker is that 紫檀 rewards indirect heat and punishes flame. On a charcoal disc or an electric heater, the note unfolds slowly and lasts. In a burning stick, the wood is part of the fuel bed, so its aromatic contribution is always mixed with combustion products. That does not make sticks wrong — it makes the dosage and the particle size matter more than the raw material's reputation.

**Mechanism chain — why the powder behaves differently from the chip:**

- Whole heartwood has a small surface-to-mass ratio → heat penetrates slowly → the aromatic fraction releases over a long period → the note is low and steady.
- Grinding raises the surface-to-mass ratio sharply → the same heat reaches far more material at once → release is faster and hotter → the top of the note becomes sharper and the tail shorter.
- Fine powder in a stick also changes the burn itself → tighter packing, less air between particles → the coal front creeps rather than races → the stick may go out unless the binder and the format are adjusted.

This is why a formula developed with chips cannot be transferred gram-for-gram to powder. The material is the same; the physics is not.

## The naming problem: 紫檀 as a category

> **Direct answer:** The word has always worked as a trade grade as much as a plant name — a label for dark, heavy, red-purple hardwood that more than one rosewood could satisfy.

Everything starts with one character. 檀 signals prestige in the old aromatic vocabulary; it never signalled kinship. *Santalum album* wears it, 紫檀 wears it, and 降真香 is discussed in the same breath, yet the three plants belong to different lineages. When English rendered 紫檀 as "red sandalwood," it carried the prestige across and left the botany behind, planting the idea that one is a tinted version of the other. Family, continent, anatomy, and chemistry all say otherwise.

Inside Chinese usage the looseness runs the other way. Historical texts used 紫檀 for dark, dense, red-purple hardwood generally, and several *Dalbergia* rosewoods — the material of fine Ming and Qing furniture — sit inside that usage. A classical reference to 紫檀 may therefore be pointing at a class of timbers rather than at *Pterocarpus santalinus* specifically. That is not a translation problem. It is a historical naming habit, and it means a label alone cannot settle identity.

For this encyclopedia, the entry is fixed: **紫檀 = *Pterocarpus santalinus***. That is a deliberate editorial decision, stated so the reader can calibrate every claim on the page. When a seller uses 紫檀, the word is a claim to be checked against species and origin, not a fact that arrives with the shipment. The wider discipline is set out in the [incense authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/), and the specific English-language trap is unpacked in [sandalwood vs red sandalwood](/blog/sandalwood-vs-red-sandalwood/).

## Where it grows, and why the supply is tight

> **Direct answer:** A narrow endemic of the Eastern Ghats, slow to build a usable core, and therefore easy to over-harvest — which is what led to the 2019 listing.

A narrow endemic with a valuable heartwood is a predictable conservation problem. The tree is not widespread; the part that is wanted is the part that takes longest to make; and the demand is not seasonal. Harvest pressure follows. The CITES listing means international trade is monitored through a permit system rather than prohibited outright — a distinction that matters, because "CITES-listed" is often repeated as if it meant "banned."

For a buyer, the listing changes the paperwork, not the chemistry. Legally traded material exists. What it does not do is arrive cheaply or anonymously. An offer of abundant, inexpensive 紫檀 heartwood is therefore a signal to slow down: either the material is not the species, or the chain of custody is not what it claims. Neither possibility is resolved by looking at the colour, because colour is the easiest property to fake.

## What an Appendix II listing actually does

> **Direct answer:** Appendix II does not ban trade. It requires that exports be authorised by a permit, issued by the exporting country's management authority, and that the shipment be accompanied by that documentation. The mechanism is a paper trail, and its weak point is the same as every paper trail: the paper can describe a shipment that the wood does not match.

The convention's logic is worth understanding, because it explains the questions a legitimate seller expects. Appendix II covers species that are not necessarily facing extinction today but whose trade must be controlled to keep it from becoming a problem. The control works through permits: an export permit is issued only when the authority is satisfied that the export is legal and that the trade is not detrimental to the species' survival. Some importing countries also require an import permit. The permit travels with the shipment.

For a buyer, three practical consequences follow:

- **A permit is shipment-specific.** It identifies a quantity and a consignment. A certificate that says the seller is "CITES registered" is not the same as a permit for the wood in the box.
- **The exporting authority, not the seller, issues it.** Ask to see the document, not a promise that one exists.
- **The listing is about the species, not the grade.** A legal shipment can contain poor material; an illegal one can contain excellent material. Legality and quality are separate axes, and both need checking.

The last point matters because buyers often use documentation as a proxy for quality, or quality as a proxy for legality. Neither inference holds. The permit answers "may this travel?" The cut face answers "what is it?" The trial burn answers "is it any good?"

## How the dye market changed, and what it left behind

> **Direct answer:** 紫檀 was a colour material before it was an incense material in Western trade. The rise of synthetic red dyes in the nineteenth century removed most of the industrial demand for natural red pigments, and the wood's commercial identity shifted toward timber, carving, and — for a smaller audience — aroma.

The history is a useful antidote to the assumption that this wood has always been valued for scent. For centuries, a red wood that yielded a strong, light-fast colour was valuable for reasons that had nothing to do with a censer. Textiles, lacquer, manuscript decoration, and later European dye houses all had uses for a stable red.

Synthetic chemistry changed that. From the mid-nineteenth century onward, coal-tar dyes offered consistent colour at a fraction of the cost of extracting it from wood. The craft uses survived in niches — restorers, traditional lacquer workers, small-scale textile dyers — while the bulk trade moved elsewhere. What remained was the timber trade and a specialised aromatic use.

This is why old descriptions of 紫檀 often emphasise colour and working qualities rather than scent. The writers were describing the material's main market. A modern reader who encounters those descriptions and assumes they are tasting notes is reading a dye specification as if it were a perfume review.

## Terms that appear on invoices

> **Direct answer:** Trade paperwork uses a small vocabulary of its own, and each term narrows what the seller is actually promising. Reading those terms correctly prevents a grade name from being mistaken for a specification.

| Term | What it normally means | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Billet | A short, squared section cut for a specific job | Species; the billet may be a different dense wood |
| Flitch | A slab sawn from one log, kept in sequence | Grade; the sequence may include sapwood at the edges |
| Heart only | Sapwood trimmed away | How much heart; whether the core is sound |
| Air-dried | Dried in open air rather than a kiln | Final moisture; the figure at the time of sale |
| Kiln-dried | Dried under controlled heat | Schedule; fast schedules can case-harden |
| Certificated stock | Material covered by a permit or certificate | Whether the certificate matches this consignment |
| Chain of custody | Documented path from harvest to sale | The quality of the documentation itself |

Two habits follow. The first is to ask for the noun behind the adjective: "air-dried" is a process, not a grade, and "heart only" is a claim about trimming, not about species. The second is to treat any term that cannot be checked against the physical piece as marketing until proven otherwise.

The vocabulary is not dishonest. It is simply compressed, and compression always hides something. A buyer who unpacks the terms gets a clearer picture than one who argues about the price of an undefined noun.

## The furniture vocabulary behind the name

> **Direct answer:** In the furniture trade the same wood travels as *zitan*, and the surrounding vocabulary — hongmu, huanghuali, rosewood — is a set of trade categories rather than botanical ones. Knowing the vocabulary prevents a label from being mistaken for a species.

"Zitan" is the older romanisation of 紫檀 and remains the standard term in writing on Chinese furniture. It sits inside a wider vocabulary that English-speaking collectors often treat as if it were taxonomy:

- **Hongmu (红木)** is an umbrella term for dense, dark, often reddish hardwoods used in Chinese furniture. It is a commercial and aesthetic category, not a family.
- **Huanghuali (黄花梨)** names a different prized wood, associated with *Dalbergia odorifera* and used for the finest Ming furniture. It is not 紫檀, and the two were used for different effects.
- **Rosewood** is an English trade word applied to many species across several genera. It tells you the wood is dense, dark, and open-grained; it does not tell you which tree.
- **Red sanders** is the Indian timber name for *Pterocarpus santalinus*. It belongs to the export and sawmill vocabulary rather than the collector's vocabulary.

The lesson is that every one of these words is a claim about a market, not a statement about a plant. A piece described as "zitan" may be genuine *Pterocarpus santalinus* or another dense rosewood that passed through the same category. A piece described as "rosewood" may have nothing to do with either. The botanical name is the only term in the list that cannot slide.

This vocabulary also explains why furniture history and incense history diverge. Collectors care about figure, patina, joinery, and provenance. Formulators care about powder behaviour and aroma. The same word, 紫檀, carries both conversations, and the two have never been required to agree.

## Where the scent sits among aroma families

> **Direct answer:** 紫檀 belongs to the woody-balsamic corner of the map: dry rather than resinous, sweet rather than floral, and low in projection. It is a base-register material, closer to cedar and sandalwood than to citrus, mint, or the animalic fixatives.

Perfumery sorts materials into families for convenience, and the sorting is useful even when it is imprecise. The map below places 紫檀 against materials a formulator is likely to have on the same shelf.

| Family | Typical materials | Relation to 紫檀 |
|---|---|---|
| Woody, dry | Cedar, cypress, vetiver | Close cousins; 紫檀 is sweeter and heavier |
| Woody, creamy | Sandalwood, guaiac | Adjacent; 紫檀 lacks the milky lactonic quality |
| Balsamic, resinous | Benzoin, styrax, frankincense | Complementary; resins supply lift and sweetness 紫檀 does not have |
| Spicy | Clove, cinnamon, cardamom | 紫檀 carries a faint spice edge that bridges to this family |
| Floral | Rose, jasmine, osmanthus | 紫檀 sits underneath rather than beside them |
| Citrus and green | Bergamot, petitgrain, mint | Poor partners at high dose; the contrast reads as discord |
| Animalic | Musk, ambergris, onycha | Historically paired; 紫檀 gives the animalic note a dry floor |

The practical reading is that 紫檀 is a *floor*, not a *ceiling*. It gives a blend a bottom, and it accepts almost anything above it. What it cannot do is supply lift, diffusion, or a bright opening — those have to come from resins, florals, or citrus, and the formulator who asks 紫檀 for them will keep adding material without getting the effect.

There is a cultural layer on top of the chemistry. In the classical register, the woody-balsamic families were the respectable core of a blend, and the bright, sharp materials were used sparingly as accents. That hierarchy is a matter of taste and convention, not a law of composition.

## Seals, beads, and small carvings

> **Direct answer:** Much of the historical 紫檀 that survives is small: seal knobs, beads, boxes, brush rests, and Buddhist implements. Those objects show what the wood does over time — how it wears, how it polishes, how it responds to handling — better than any fresh block can.

A seal is a small carved block used to stamp a name or a phrase. 紫檀 was favoured for seal knobs and for the seals themselves because it holds a fine edge and takes a high polish without a finish. A bead is simpler: a drilled sphere, turned and polished, strung into a mala or a rosary. The wood's density makes a bead feel cool and heavy in the hand, and its colour deepens with skin contact.

These objects are useful evidence for a modern buyer in three ways. First, they show the colour of aged, handled heartwood, which is often darker and browner than a fresh cut. Second, they show wear patterns: genuine dense wood polishes to a soft sheen rather than fuzzing at the edges. Third, they show how the material behaves at small scale, where a hidden crack or a patch of sapwood is impossible to disguise.

The market consequence is that small objects are where substitution is most common and least visible. A bead can be dyed, a seal can be stained, and a photograph can make either look convincing. The same discipline applies as for powder: check a fresh surface, check density, and check the paperwork rather than the patina.

Collectors value patina; formulators should not. A dark, glossy surface is a record of handling, not a guarantee of aroma. The two markets read the same object for different evidence, and neither reading transfers to the other.

## What a censer does to the wood

> **Direct answer:** A censer controls how much oxygen reaches the material and how fast heat arrives. Those two variables decide whether the wood smoulders, pyrolyses, or burns, and each produces a different smell from the same sample.

Warming and burning are different processes. Warming drives off volatile compounds without igniting the solid. Pyrolysis breaks the wood down with heat in a limited supply of oxygen, producing tars and aromatic fragments. Combustion consumes the material in a visible flame or a glowing ember and produces ash, water vapour, carbon dioxide, and the acrid compounds that read as smoke.

The hardware mediates between these states. An ash bed insulates and evens out the heat. A mica plate or a metal screen separates the material from the coal, so the wood warms rather than chars. A closed censer limits oxygen and encourages smouldering. An open burner with a strong draught pushes the coal front along and produces more complete combustion, which is cleaner but less aromatic.

For 紫檀 the practical consequence is that the same powder can read as sweet, as tarry, or as nothing at all, depending on the setup. A formulator who blames the material for a poor result may be blaming the burner. The variables to change, in order, are the gap between heat and wood, the amount of material, and the airflow — not the dosage of fragrance oil or the ratio of binder.

Residue tells you which regime you were in. A pale, powdery ash suggests fairly complete combustion. A black, sticky residue suggests pyrolysis and unburned tar. Neither is a health measurement; both are clues about how the censer is running.

## Working the wood

> **Direct answer:** 紫檀 is hard, dense, and abrasive to edged tools. It rewards sharp edges, light cuts, scraping rather than heavy sanding, and a wax finish rather than an oil finish — especially on pieces that will later be burned or ground.

Carvers and turners who work dense rosewoods know the drill. The material dulls high-speed steel quickly, burns if a dull edge is forced, and tears rather than slices when the grain reverses. The usual adaptations are ordinary craft practice:

- **Keep the edge sharp and the angle high.** Dense wood punishes a low bevel.
- **Take light passes.** A heavy cut heats the surface and glazes it.
- **Scrape before sanding.** A cabinet scraper removes tear-out that sandpaper would only polish.
- **Drill before shaping.** Pre-drilling avoids splits in small sections.
- **Finish with wax, not oil.** Oil penetrates and stays; wax sits on the surface and can be removed. For a piece destined for incense use, a finish that does not enter the wood is the safer choice.

The same properties that make the wood a pleasure to finish make it a nuisance to grind. Heat builds quickly, the powder clumps, and the red dust stains everything. A maker who has worked the material as a carver already knows half of what the incense side requires.

## Moisture, shrinkage, and the workshop

> **Direct answer:** Dense wood holds water stubbornly and moves as it dries. A block that feels dry on the surface may still be wet inside, and a powder that looks free-running may be carrying enough moisture to clump, mould, or burn badly.

Wood is hygroscopic: it exchanges water with the air until it reaches an equilibrium. In a humid room it takes water up and swells; in a dry one it gives water off and shrinks. Because shrinkage is uneven — tangential movement exceeds radial movement — drying sets up internal stress. That stress is what produces checks, cup, and case-hardening.

For a buyer, the practical tests are simple. Weigh a sample, leave it in the intended storage conditions for a period, and weigh it again. A stable weight suggests the piece has settled; a falling weight means it is still drying. A moisture meter gives a number, but the meter's calibration and the wood's density affect the reading, so the weight-change method is a useful cross-check.

For a formulator, moisture is a process variable rather than a material property. Powder that has absorbed water behaves differently in a press, sticks to the die, and burns unevenly. The remedy is not heat, which drives off volatile aromatics along with water, but controlled storage and small working batches.

The workshop consequences are mundane and worth stating. Dense stock dries slowly, so kiln schedules are gentler and longer than for softwoods. Sawn blanks need stickers and air movement. Finished pieces need a stable room, not a radiator. And a powder jar needs a lid, not a scoop left in it.

## From tree to working material

> **Direct answer:** Cleaning comes first: the pale outer band is cut away and discarded. What remains is sawn into blocks for carving, milled into meal for incense, or soaked and boiled to draw out the red colouring matter.

| Form | How it is made | What it is for | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole heartwood | Heartwood squared or left in sections | Carving, furniture, seals, beads | Species, density, absence of sapwood |
| Cut blanks | Sawn to dimension | Implements, handles, small carvings | Checking, cracks, grain run-out |
| Chips and shavings | Offcuts and trimmings | Warming, pigment extraction | Dust, bark, foreign wood |
| Powder | Milled heartwood, screened | Incense blending, colouring | Particle size, colour consistency, contamination |
| Extract / pigment | Shavings soaked or boiled | Dye, lacquer colour | Strength, solvent, additives |

The powder route is the one that matters most for incense, and it is the least standardised. "紫檀 powder" can mean anything from a fine flour that passes a hundred-mesh screen to a coarse sawdust with visible splinters. The two behave differently in a formula, colour differently, and burn differently. A specification that names only the wood and not the particle size has specified almost nothing.

There is also a workshop reality the retail language hides: this wood is hard. It dulls tooling, generates heat during grinding, and produces a powder that clumps in humid air. Milling it in small batches and screening immediately is standard practice for a reason. A supplier who can state the mesh range and the milling date is telling you something about process discipline, not just about the wood.

## Grading: what each signal can and cannot prove

> **Direct answer:** Colour, density, grain, and smell are screening signals, not proof of species. Each one can be faked or imitated, and each one fails in a specific way. The only conclusive check is botanical or documentary identification of *Pterocarpus santalinus*.

| Signal | What it suggests | How it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Deep, even crimson | Mature heartwood, good extractive load | Dyed or stained substitute; the colour sits on the surface |
| High density | Old, slow-grown heartwood | A different dense hardwood; density alone is not species-specific |
| Fine, close grain | Slow growth, good working quality | Species with similar grain; visual similarity is common in the rosewood group |
| Sweet-rosy smell on warming | Aromatic fraction present | Scented or oiled material; smell can be added |
| Documented origin | Legal, traceable supply | Paperwork that describes a shipment, not the wood in it |

Read the table as a sequence of filters, not a scorecard. Colour is the cheapest test and the easiest to defeat. Density is harder to fake but not species-specific. Documents are only as good as the chain behind them. A serious buyer uses several signals together and accepts that the combination still leaves residual uncertainty — which is why the sourcing relationship, not the single inspection, carries the real weight.

There is a useful negative test as well. Genuine heartwood is red through the piece. Scratch a hidden face and look at the shaving: if the red is a skin over pale wood, the material has been coloured. That test costs nothing and catches the most common deception in the category.

## Substitution: what gets sold in its place

> **Direct answer:** The substitutes fall into three groups — other red woods passed off by colour, dyed pale woods, and unrelated materials sold under the name for their look. Each fails a different way, and each requires a different check.

| Substitute | Why it is used | Where it fails | Screening move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Other *Dalbergia* rosewoods | Similar density and working character; historically part of the 紫檀 category | Different species, different scent, often different colour depth | Botanical ID; grain and pore structure |
| Dyed pale hardwood | Cheap, accepts red stain convincingly | Colour is superficial; scratch shows pale core | Cut or scratch test; solvent wipe |
| "Bloodwood" and similar red timbers | Naturally red, visually close | Different density, different aroma, no santalin history | Density comparison; species documentation |
| Powder blends with added colourant | Consistent appearance at low cost | Colourant is not santalin; scent is thin | Ask for mill certificate; test staining behaviour |
| Old furniture offcuts sold as incense wood | Genuine-looking age and colour | May be a different rosewood; may carry finish or wax | Smell test on a clean face; finish contamination check |

The matrix is not a buyer's licence to guess. It is a way to match the check to the risk. A cheap powder is most likely a colour problem; a cheap solid piece is most likely a species problem. Neither is settled by a photograph.

## 紫檀 and dragon's blood: both red, nothing else in common

> **Direct answer:** Red sandalwood is a wood; dragon's blood is a resin tapped from certain palms. They meet only in the colour red. Confusing them is rare in the incense trade and common enough in dye and craft writing to be worth separating.

The comparison is a useful corrective to colour-based thinking, because it shows how little "red" tells you about a material.

| | 紫檀 (red sandalwood) | Dragon's blood (血竭) |
|---|---|---|
| Material class | Heartwood of a legume tree | Resin from palm fruits or stems |
| Botanical context | *Pterocarpus santalinus*, Fabaceae | *Daemonorops* and related palms, Arecaceae |
| Form in trade | Blocks, chips, powder | Lumps, tears, powder, tincture |
| Primary use | Carving, dye, incense wood | Pigment, varnish colour, historical medicine |
| Behaviour on heat | Woody, sweet, faintly rosy; smoulders as wood | Melts, spits, and gives a resinous, slightly sweet smoke |
| Failure mode | Substitution by other red woods | Adulteration with other resins and fillers |

A formulator who reaches for "the red one" without naming the material can end up with a resin in a wood-based formula, or a wood where a resin was intended. The two do not substitute, and the mistake is not cosmetic: the burn behaviour, the binder balance, and the residue all change.

## Storage and handling

> **Direct answer:** Store solid 紫檀 dry, dark, and sealed; store powder the same way but with more care, because powder picks up odours and moisture far faster than blocks. Neither form benefits from refrigeration.

Dense heartwood is stable. It does not need oil, and oiling a piece intended for incense is counterproductive because the oil becomes part of what burns. Blocks keep for years in a dry cupboard, away from direct sun, with air movement around them. The risks are physical: checking at the ends, insect attack in sapwood that was not removed, and surface grime from handling.

Powder is a different problem. It has a large surface area, so it equilibrates with the room. A jar left open in a kitchen will smell of the kitchen. A jar left open in a damp room will clump and may grow mould. The working practice is a sealed glass or food-grade container, a dark cupboard, a label with the milling date, and a small working jar that is refilled rather than a large jar opened every day.

Refrigeration is a common instinct and a poor one. Cold powder brought into a warm room condenses moisture on its surface, and repeated cycles are worse than a steady dry cupboard. If the ambient humidity is genuinely high, a sealed container with a separate desiccant sachet is more controllable than temperature control — as long as the sachet does not touch the powder.

Freezing is sometimes proposed for long storage. It works only if the material is sealed and allowed to return to room temperature before opening. Otherwise the same condensation problem appears, with more water.

## Where it sits in a blend

> **Direct answer:** In the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) logic of Chinese blended incense, 紫檀 reads as an assistant or supporting wood: it gives body, slows the burn, and adds a low sweet warmth. It is rarely the chief note, and it is not a fixative in the technical sense.

The classification is editorial, not classical dogma — the old formulas do not attach a rank to every material, and ranks shift with the formula. Used as a support, the wood does three jobs at once. It adds mass, which changes how the stick or cone burns. It adds a low-frequency sweetness that rounds off sharper resins. And it adds colour, which is a presentation function no other common incense wood performs so directly.

The limit is equally important. 紫檀 does not project. Its note sits close to the material and does not carry across a room the way a resin or a floral absolute does. A formula that leans on it for diffusion will disappoint; a formula that uses it to give weight and continuity will not. That is a judgement about behaviour in a blend, not a claim about quality.

## Working notes for makers

> **Direct answer:** Treat 紫檀 as a dense base material. Grind it fine but not to flour if you need burn control, expect it to slow the coal front, and expect the red to stain everything it touches.

A few practical points follow from the physics rather than from tradition:

- **Particle size sets the release rate.** Coarse powder gives a slower, woodier, less sweet burn. Fine powder gives a faster, sweeter top note and a shorter tail.
- **Density sets the burn speed.** A high proportion of 紫檀 in a stick slows the coal front. If the stick starts going out, the fix is usually more binder, more air, or a coarser grind — not more fragrance.
- **Colour migrates.** The pigment stains hands, tools, cloth, and pale surfaces. Work on a washable surface and keep the powder away from finished goods.
- **Moisture matters more than for soft woods.** A dense powder holds water at the surface and can read dry while the core is not. Weigh the water, do not judge by touch.
- **The note is heat-dependent.** Overheating produces smoke that masks the sweetness. In stick form, keep the aromatic load modest and let the wood do structural work.

None of this is a recipe. It is the set of behaviours a formulator needs to hold in mind before writing one. The broader material logic is covered in [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## How much confidence does a claim deserve?

> **Direct answer:** Match the claim to the evidence that can actually settle it. Most 紫檀 claims are settled by documents and cut surfaces, not by reputation, price, or the word of a seller.

| Claim | What settles it | What does not settle it |
|---|---|---|
| "This is *Pterocarpus santalinus*" | Botanical identification, herbarium reference, or a documented species determination | Colour, density, country of origin, price |
| "This shipment is legal" | CITES documentation matching the shipment and the species | A seller's assurance; a generic certificate for a different lot |
| "This is old heartwood" | Density, growth-ring structure, colour carried through the piece | A dark surface; an aged-looking finish |
| "This is incense grade" | A trial burn in the intended format, at the intended dose | Aroma of the raw block; a supplier's grade name |
| "This is traditional" | A cited text and a stated context | Repetition of the claim in marketing copy |

The table is deliberately blunt. Each row separates a proposition from the evidence that would make it credible, and each "does not settle it" column names the shortcut that circulates most widely. Reading down that column is a short education in how material claims fail.

## The traditional record, separated from evidence

> **Direct answer:** Use and belief are different records. The wood was certainly worked, burned, and dyed; the medicinal lines are tradition, not proof.

It helps to keep three kinds of statement apart. Craft evidence covers the objects: furniture, seals, beads, Buddhist implements, and the red colouring matter drawn from the wood. Aromatic evidence covers reputation: the material belonged to the precious-woods register and was burned for its scent. Medical evidence is the weakest of the three, consisting of materia-medica entries for cooling and astringent uses. This page reports those entries as traditional belief; it draws no conclusion about effect and offers no reason to burn or ingest the material for health.

## The dye tradition, and why it is not the incense trade

> **Direct answer:** 紫檀 entered craft history largely as a colouring material. Shavings were soaked or boiled to release the red extractive, and the liquor was used to colour textiles and to tint lacquer. That is a different process, a different product, and a different market from incense powder.

The pigment is not a surface paint. It is an extractive held inside the heartwood, released when the wood is cut, soaked, or boiled. The traditional sequence is simple: reduce the wood to shavings, expose it to water or another solvent, concentrate the liquor, and apply it to the substrate. The details of mordants, substrates, and repetition varied by craft and region, and they are not the subject of this page.

What matters for an incense reader is the separation of trades. A dye worker wants extractive yield. An incense maker wants aromatic volatiles and predictable combustion. The same heartwood can serve both, but it is graded on different criteria, and a batch optimised for colour may be ordinary for scent. This is the clearest illustration of the page's central point: 紫檀 is a material with more than one job, and the job determines the specification.

There is a historical irony in the trade names. The wood became famous in Europe and in the Indian Ocean trade as a colour source before it became known in English as a "sandalwood." The red pigment travelled under names tied to dyeing, while the aromatic reputation travelled under names tied to the 檀 register. Two supply chains, two vocabularies, one species.

## Running a trial burn

> **Direct answer:** Judge 紫檀 by a controlled small burn, not by a cold sniff. Warm a clean sample gently, note the first minute separately from the next five, and compare against a reference material you already know.

A trial burn is a procedure, not a ritual. The point is to make two samples comparable, so that a decision rests on a difference rather than an impression.

1. **Clean the surface.** Remove dust, finish, and handling grime from the sample. A dirty surface burns dirty.
2. **Use a neutral heat source.** An electric heater or a charcoal disc with a clean ash bed. Residue from a previous material will contaminate the result.
3. **Start small.** A pinch is enough. A large sample produces a large smoke plume, which overwhelms the nose and tells you less.
4. **Separate the phases.** Note the first warm phase before visible smoke, then the smoking phase. The first is the material's aromatic character; the second is combustion.
5. **Score specific attributes.** Sweetness, woodiness, rosiness, sharpness, smoke, and after-smell. Vague approval is not a result.
6. **Compare.** Run the same procedure on a reference sample in the same session. Memory across days is unreliable.
7. **Record.** Note the date, the sample, the form, and the impression. A notebook turns a hobby into a dataset.

The most common mistake is evaluating a burning stick as if it were the raw material. In a stick, the wood is fuel, the binder is present, and the combustion products dominate. That is a valid test of the formula, not of the ingredient. Keep the two tests separate.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** Smoke is the main hazard, and this wood adds dust to it. Ventilate the room, keep the censer clear of children, pets, and curtains, and protect the lungs when milling.

Two exposures are specific to this material. The first is combustion smoke, which is the general incense risk and is not reduced by the wood being expensive. The second is dust: grinding dense hardwood produces fine particles, and the red pigment stains as it settles. Grinding outdoors or with extraction, and wearing a dust mask, is ordinary workshop practice rather than an excess of caution. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice; the site's [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case.

## Buying checklist

> **Direct answer:** Species and legality first; colour, density, and mesh after. A seller who cannot clear the first two has nothing to sell you.

1. **Species, in writing.** Ask for *Pterocarpus santalinus*, not "紫檀."
2. **Shipment paperwork.** A CITES permit identifies a consignment; a general certificate does not.
3. **Fresh surface.** Judge colour on a cut face in daylight, never on a polished photograph.
4. **Density.** Compare against a reference piece rather than trusting a description.
5. **Mesh size.** For powder, an unstated mesh is an unspecified product.
6. **Trial before volume.** Buy small, burn small, then scale.

## 紫檀 against sandalwood and jiangzhenxiang

> **Direct answer:** The three are complements, not variants. 紫檀 supplies weight and colour, 檀香 supplies creamy diffusion, and 降真香 supplies its own historical character. They share a classical register and a character; they do not share a family.

The comparison that matters is functional. A formulator reaching for 檀香 wants a soft, milky, oil-rich diffusion that carries across a room. A formulator reaching for 紫檀 wants mass, a slow coal, and a low sweet undertone that sits underneath other notes. 降真香 belongs to a different conversation again, with its own documentary history and its own aromatic profile.

That is why the three are not interchangeable even when a formula lists them side by side. Substituting one for another changes the burn, the balance, and often the colour of the finished powder. The shared 檀 character is a mark of prestige in the classical vocabulary; it is not a statement about botany, chemistry, or function.

## FAQ

**Is red sandalwood the same as sandalwood?** No. 紫檀 comes from a legume; 檀香 comes from a sandalwood tree. One is grown for a red, heavy core, the other for oil. The resemblance lives in the English label, not in the plants.

**What does 紫檀 smell like when it burns?** Soft, dry wood sweetness with a faint rosy-spice edge, provided the heat stays low. Overheat it and the aromatic fraction is swamped by smoke. That is why the material is usually warmed or blended rather than burned alone.

**Why is the species CITES-listed?** A narrow range, slow growth, and a valuable core make it easy to over-harvest. Appendix II, in force since 2019, puts exports under a permit system. It does not ban trade; it makes documentation part of the transaction.

**Is "red sanders" a different wood?** No, it is the same species under its timber-trade name. The alias matters because export papers, sawmill lists, and customs entries may use it while the retail label says red sandalwood.

**Why does the powder look brown instead of crimson?** Fine particles scatter light, so the same wood reads duller as meal than as a cut block. Age and surface oxidation also push the colour toward brown. Neither observation proves or disproves identity.

## Summary

紫檀 is one material with three careers. As timber it is heavy, close-grained, and beloved of carvers. As colour it supplied a red extractive to dyers and lacquer workers long before it supplied a scent to anyone. As aroma it is a quiet, sweet, woody presence that rewards gentle heat and punishes flame. The plant behind all three is *Pterocarpus santalinus*, a slow-growing Indian legume whose scarcity turned a trade name into a regulated commodity. That regulation, and the older habit of using 紫檀 for several dark rosewoods, means the label is where inquiry starts rather than where it ends.

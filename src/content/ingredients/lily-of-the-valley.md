> **Direct answer:** Lily of the valley (铃兰, línglán) is *Convallaria majalis*, a rhizomatous woodland perennial with nodding white bells, placed in the asparagus family (Asparagaceae) and native across the northern temperate zone. Its scent is the model for the "muguet" note of modern perfumery, yet the plant is a cardiac-glycoside poison with no classical Chinese incense role. Read it as an ornamental and a fragrance reference — never as a burnable botanical.

The table is the argument in miniature: a Chinese name, a European scent tradition, and a toxicology file that outranks both. The rest of this page expands those three lines and keeps them separate, because most of the confusion around 铃兰 comes from letting a beautiful name imply a safe material.

## The poison comes before the perfume

> **Direct answer:** Every part of *Convallaria majalis* contains cardiac glycosides, chiefly convallatoxin and convalloside. They are toxic if eaten and can irritate skin and eyes, and the plant must never be burned, brewed, or turned into a home-made aromatic preparation.

Cardiac glycosides are plant compounds that interfere with the heart's electrical and mechanical behaviour. That description is the reason this plant is treated differently from a rose or a jasmine: the danger is not a rash or a bitter taste, it is a dose-dependent effect on the organ that keeps blood moving. The two glycosides named here, convallatoxin and convalloside, belong to the cardenolide group, and they are present in leaves, stems, flowers, and the red berries that follow the flowers.

The berries deserve a separate sentence because they are the part a child is most likely to pick. They are small, bright, and produced in late summer, which is exactly the window when gardens are full of children and the plant is least conspicuous among other greenery. Documented signs of poisoning start with nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, and can move on to dizziness, visual disturbance, and a slow or irregular pulse. Severe exposure is a medical emergency, and the correct response to any suspected ingestion is an immediate call to a poison service or emergency number, not a wait-and-see approach.

Drying does not disarm the plant. The glycosides survive the hay-like state, so a dried sprig kept in a jar is still toxic, and a dried flower head is not a safe craft material for a child's project. Steeping does not disarm it either: water draws soluble compounds out of the tissue, which is precisely what a tea, a tincture, or a floral water would do. Any instruction that begins "make a soothing infusion" and ends with 铃兰 is unsafe regardless of the source's confidence.

The handling chain is short and easy to break:

**Cut stem → sap at the cut end → fingers pick up the sap → hand reaches the mouth or an eye → exposure.** Gloves and hand-washing break that chain at the first link. In a household with toddlers, or with a dog that investigates everything, the more reliable break is not to bring the plant indoors at all.

Pets are a separate route. A dog that digs in a shaded border may chew a rhizome; a cat may bat at a hanging bell. The glycosides affect the heart in animals as they do in people, and the signs owners report are non-specific — vomiting, lethargy, a change in breathing or heart rate. None of that is a diagnosis, and none of it should be treated at home. The practical response is the same as for a child: remove the animal from the plant, call a veterinarian, and describe how much was accessible rather than guessing how much was eaten.

There is also a slow route that owners miss. A cut stem in a vase drips into the water, the water is tipped into the garden, and a pet drinks from the puddle. The amount is probably small, and "probably small" is not a safety standard. Tip vase water down a drain rather than onto the ground, and wipe the vase before it goes back into the cupboard.

## What the plant actually is

> **Direct answer:** *Convallaria majalis* is a low, rhizomatous perennial of the asparagus family, formerly placed in the lily family. It grows from a creeping rhizome, sends up two or three broad basal leaves, and in late spring produces a leafless scape hung with nodding white bells, followed by red berries.

The plant's architecture explains both its garden appeal and its persistence. A rhizome creeps just below the soil surface and throws up new shoots, so a small planting becomes a colony in a few seasons. The leaves are broad, elliptic, and upright, forming a loose clump that reads as foliage first and flower second. The flowers sit on a separate leafless stem, arranged along one side in a raceme, each bell hanging from a short pedicel. The effect in a shaded border is a row of small white lanterns at ankle height.

The family placement has moved. Flora of China treats *Convallaria* within the Liliaceae, the broad lily family of traditional botany. Modern classification places it in Asparagaceae, the asparagus family, alongside hosta, hyacinth, and agave. Both statements are true in their own frame: the older family is a historical grouping, the newer one reflects a reorganisation based on genetic relationships. For a reader, the practical point is that the English name "lily of the valley" and the Chinese name 铃兰 both describe a resemblance, not a lineage.

The native range is the cool temperate Northern Hemisphere. Flora of China records the species in northern China, which surprises readers who assume a plant with a French perfume name must be foreign. It is not a tropical species and it does not need heat; it wants shade, leaf litter, and a cold winter. Garden forms exist, including a double-flowered cultivar, and none of them is less toxic than the wild type. Cultivar status changes the look of the flower, not its chemistry.

For the botanical record, see [Convallaria majalis in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200027600) and the corresponding [GBIF entry](https://www.gbif.org/species/7459480).

In garden terms, the plant is a shade specialist with a long memory. It tolerates dry shade better than most woodland perennials once established, but it wants organic matter and a site that does not bake in summer. It spreads by rhizome rather than by seed in most gardens, which is why a clump can be divided in autumn and moved without much ceremony. The same rhizome makes it hard to remove: a fragment left in the soil can return the following spring. That persistence is a horticultural fact, and it is also a safety consideration, because a plant that keeps coming back keeps presenting the same berries and the same sap.

Nothing about the cultivar changes the toxicology. Double-flowered forms, variegated leaves, and named garden selections are the same species with the same glycoside profile. A reader who has been told that a particular cultivar is "safe" or "sterile" should treat the claim as unverified. Sterility, where it exists, affects seed set, not chemistry.

## The name 铃兰 and the company it keeps

> **Direct answer:** 铃兰 means "bell orchid": 铃 for the bell-shaped flower, 兰 for orchid. The plant is neither an orchid nor, in modern classification, a lily — both names describe a look, and neither says anything about incense.

Chinese plant names often work by analogy, and 兰 is one of the most generously applied characters in the vocabulary. It attaches to orchids proper, but also to plants that merely carry an elegant flower or a slender leaf. 铃兰 follows that pattern: the bell shape supplies 铃, and 兰 supplies the ornamental register. A reader who takes 兰 as a botanical claim will place the plant in the wrong family and, more importantly, may assume the cultural baggage that comes with orchids.

The English name has the same problem in reverse. "Lily" suggests Liliaceae, and the plant was indeed filed there for a long time, but the modern placement is Asparagaceae. Neither the English nor the Chinese name is a taxonomic statement. They are garden names, stable because gardeners like them, not because botanists endorse them.

The French name, muguet, is the one that matters commercially. It names the flower and, by extension, the perfumery accord built to resemble it. English folk names — "Our Lady's tears", "ladder to heaven" — belong to the same ornamental tradition and carry no incense history. None of these names appears in the classical Chinese incense literature, and none of them implies that the plant was ever dried, ground, and burned as a 香材.

That absence is the point of this section. A plant can have a Chinese name, a French name, and a place in every garden centre in Europe, and still have no place in the classical incense canon. The name is a label, not a provenance.

## Where the scent lives

> **Direct answer:** The scent comes from the open flowers, not from wood, resin, or root, and it is strongest in cool, still air. It reads green and dewy first, then sweet and floral, with a faint jasmine-like edge; on a cut stem it fades within hours.

The living flower's smell is unusually clean for a white floral. Compared with [jasmine (茉莉)](/ingredients/jasmine/), it has no indolic weight, no animalic undertow, and no heaviness at the base. Compared with [rose (玫瑰)](/ingredients/rose/), it lacks honey and spice; compared with [orris root (鸢尾根)](/ingredients/orris-root/), it has no powdery rootiness. What remains is a green, watery, slightly soapy freshness with a sweet centre — the quality perfumers call "dewy" because it suggests cold petals and wet leaves rather than a warm bouquet.

That profile is also why the flower is hard to use. The scent is carried by volatile molecules leaving living tissue, and the tissue has a short working life:

**Flower opens → volatiles evaporate into the surrounding air → the concentration at the nose is highest while the tissue is fresh → cutting the stem stops the supply → the bell wilts and the scent collapses within hours.** A material that cannot be stored cannot become a raw material. This is the mechanical reason there is no trade in dried 铃兰 flowers for incense, quite apart from the toxicity.

The bloom window is late spring in temperate gardens, and a colony stays in flower for a couple of weeks rather than months. Cool weather extends the display; heat shortens it. The flowers are at their most fragrant in still air, because moving air dilutes the plume before it reaches the nose. That is a sensory observation, not a chemical measurement, and it is the kind of detail that makes a garden plant memorable and a commercial crop impractical.

## How perfumery rebuilds the smell

> **Direct answer:** Modern muguet is an accord built from single molecules, not an extract. Hydroxycitronellal supplies the backbone; cyclamen aldehyde, florhydral, and related materials add lift; restricted molecules have been replaced over the past two decades.

An accord is a small formula that behaves like a single smell. The perfumer starts with a molecule that carries the main idea — for muguet, hydroxycitronellal is the classic choice, a material with the green, fresh, slightly soapy floral character that defines the note. Around it go materials that supply lift, volume, and a natural-looking irregularity: cyclamen aldehyde for the cool floral edge, florhydral for the airy top, and other muguet odorants for body. The result is not a copy of a flower in the chemical sense. It is a reconstruction of the impression the flower makes.

That reconstruction is in permanent revision, because the palette changes:

**A muguet molecule is restricted → the formula loses part of its body → the perfumer substitutes two materials to rebuild volume → the accord's texture shifts → evaluators re-judge the note against the current market rather than a fixed flower.** Lilial is the clearest example: the material was classified as a reproductive toxicant and prohibited in EU cosmetics in 2022, which forced reformulation across the fragrance industry. Lyral, another long-serving muguet material, has been restricted and must be declared. Neither fact makes a muguet fragrance unsafe in normal use; both facts explain why a 1990s muguet and a 2020s muguet do not smell identical.

The benchmark that perfumery still cites is Diorissimo, launched in 1956 and composed by Edmond Roudnitska. Its reputation rests on an entirely synthetic muguet accord that reads as a flower. That is the professional lesson: the note was never dependent on the plant. The plant supplied the idea, and the molecules supplied the material. For background on how this fits the wider trade, see [plant sources of incense scents](/blog/plant-sources-of-incense-scents/) and [natural vs synthetic incense](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/).

Fragrance families place muguet in the green floral group, and the placement explains a lot about how the note is used. A green floral is built to sit between the citrus-fresh top and the soft floral heart, which makes muguet a natural bridge in a formula: it can sharpen a heavy floral without turning it citrusy, and it can soften an aldehydic opening without making it sweet. That bridging function is why the note appears in so many compositions that are not marketed as muguet soliflores. The flower is the reference, not the brief.

## The "essential oil" that does not exist

> **Direct answer:** There is no commercial true essential oil or absolute of lily of the valley. Products sold under that name are fragrance compositions — legitimate as perfumery materials, but not plant extracts, and not evidence of a botanical origin.

The reasoning is straightforward once the economics are laid out. An essential oil requires enough volatile material in the plant to make distillation worthwhile, and the living flower's volatiles are present in tiny amounts and disappear after cutting. An absolute requires solvent extraction of fresh flowers in quantity, followed by careful removal of the solvent. Both routes demand a harvest that is bulky, perishable, and toxic to handle. No supplier offers either route at commercial scale, and no serious perfumery house claims one.

What exists is a fragrance compound: a blend of synthetic muguet molecules and supporting materials, sold as "lily of the valley" because that is the smell it reproduces. That is a normal, honest product category. The problem begins when the same liquid is described as a plant extract, a "pure floral essence", or a therapeutic oil.

| Label claim | What it can honestly mean | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| "Lily of the valley essential oil" | A fragrance blend named after the note | INCI list, allergen declaration, safety data sheet |
| "Muguet absolute" | Almost certainly a marketing name, not a commercial extract | Batch documentation and the extraction route |
| "Contains hydroxycitronellal" | A declared fragrance allergen, normal in a muguet accord | Patch-test awareness, not a purity verdict |
| "Natural muguet fragrance" | May use natural isolates, but not the flower itself | Which isolates, and at what concentration |
| "Lily of the valley incense" | Should not exist as a burned botanical | If offered, it is mislabelled or unsafe |

The table is a screening tool, not an accusation. A fragrance oil that smells of muguet is a perfectly ordinary industrial material. A supplier who cannot say whether the material is a compound or an extract has told you something useful about their command of the product. Ask for the INCI list, the allergen declaration, and the safety data sheet; a legitimate seller has all three at hand.

## Telling 铃兰 apart from its lookalikes

> **Direct answer:** White bell-shaped flowers are a shape, not an identification. Lily of the valley, snowdrop, hosta, true lily, and orchid are five different plants in four families, and their safety profiles are not interchangeable.

The confusion is practical rather than academic. A gardener who orders "white bells for shade" may receive any of several plants, and a parent who identifies a plant from a photograph may misjudge the risk. The table below separates the common cases by family, season, and flower structure.

| Plant | Family | Bloom | Flower | How to tell it apart | Safety note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lily of the valley (铃兰) | Asparagaceae | Late spring | Nodding white bells on a one-sided raceme | Two or three broad basal leaves; leafless scape; red berries later | All parts toxic; never burn or ingest |
| Snowdrop | Amaryllidaceae | Late winter to early spring | Small nodding white bell with green inner marks | Blooms months earlier; narrow grey-green leaves; much smaller | Toxic; different alkaloids, same rule |
| Hosta | Asparagaceae | Summer | Bell or funnel flowers on a tall scape | Broad ribbed leaves; flowers held well above the clump | Not in the same toxicological league, but not a substitute |
| True lily (*Lilium*) | Liliaceae | Summer | Large trumpet or Turk's-cap flowers | Stout stem, narrow leaves, prominent stamens | Highly dangerous to cats; keep out of reach |
| Orchid | Orchidaceae | Varies | Three sepals, three petals, complex labellum | Flower structure is completely different | Generally not toxic, but identification still matters |

Two rows deserve emphasis. The snowdrop is the plant most often mistaken for 铃兰 in early spring, because both are small white bells; the snowdrop's inner tepals carry green markings and it appears when 铃兰 is still dormant. The true lily is the plant most often confused by name, and it carries a separate veterinary hazard: *Lilium* and *Hemerocallis* are notorious for causing kidney injury in cats, which is a reason to keep bouquets away from animals rather than a reason to fear the garden.

The orchid row exists because of the Chinese name. 兰 points a reader toward Orchidaceae, and orchids have a wholly different flower plan. A plant can be called 兰 and have nothing to do with the orchid family. Once the name is set aside, the identification is easy: broad basal leaves, a leafless scape, and bells arranged along one side.

Foraging adds a further layer of risk. A forager who picks "white bells in the woods" may be right about the species and still wrong about the dose, and the same walk may produce a handful of snowdrops mixed in. The safe rule for a plant this toxic is not to forage it at all. Identification from a photograph is not a control measure, and a mixed handful is exactly the situation where a confident identification matters least.

## Quality signals for a fragrance, not a raw material

> **Direct answer:** With no legitimate botanical raw material, quality is judged on the accord: purity of the concentrate, consistency between batches, and fidelity to the green, dewy floral idea. A harsh opening or a flat dry-down is a formula problem, not a harvest problem.

The first check is structural. A muguet concentrate should smell coherent from the first second to the dry-down: green and fresh at the top, sweet and floral in the middle, clean rather than soapy at the base. A formula that opens with a sharp aldehydic edge and never softens is unbalanced, and no amount of "natural" language repairs it. A formula that turns flat and waxy after twenty minutes has lost its middle, which usually means the muguet backbone is under-supported.

The second check is batch consistency. A fragrance compound is a manufactured mixture, so two batches should match within a stated tolerance. Drift between batches is a documentation problem as much as a sensory one: ask for the batch code and the specification, and compare against a retained sample. This is ordinary purchasing discipline, not a claim about any particular supplier.

The third check is documentation. An INCI list tells you what is declared; an allergen declaration tells you what must be labelled; a safety data sheet tells you how the material should be handled and what its flash point is. None of these documents proves that a fragrance smells good, and their absence is a reason to pause.

What fails, in practice: a concentrate that discolours in the bottle, a smell that varies from order to order, a seller who describes a synthetic accord as a "flower extract", and a product that promises therapeutic effects from a fragrance. The last is the most serious, because it converts a perfumery material into a health claim the material cannot support.

## China: ornamental, not 香材

> **Direct answer:** In China, 铃兰 is a garden and cut flower, not an incense material. It has no classical 香材 status, no entry in the incense compendia, and no documented tradition of being burned.

The plant grows well in northern Chinese gardens, which is consistent with its wide temperate range. It wants shade, moisture, and a cold dormancy, and it spreads by rhizome into a ground cover. Its use is ornamental: borders, woodland plantings, cut stems for a small vase. That is a real horticultural presence, and it is the reason the plant has a Chinese name at all.

The contrast with Europe is cultural rather than botanical. In France, sprigs of muguet are traditionally exchanged on 1 May, a custom that has nothing to do with incense and everything to do with spring. The flower's fame in perfumery grew from that ornamental and sentimental base, not from a medicinal or liturgical use. A reader who encounters 铃兰 in a Chinese garden is seeing the same species that French perfumery turned into an idea.

What is missing is the incense record. The classical Chinese literature on 香材 is specific: it names materials, describes their preparation, and discusses their role in 合香. 铃兰 does not appear in that literature as a burned material, and the plant's toxicity makes the absence unsurprising. A poisonous woodland ornamental does not become a 香材 because a modern perfumer likes its smell.

This is the misconception the page exists to correct. "It has a Chinese name" does not mean "it is a Chinese incense material". "It smells beautiful" does not mean "it can be burned". The two claims are independent, and only one of them is true here.

## Traditional use vs documented evidence

> **Direct answer:** The botanical identity and the toxicity are established facts. The classical Chinese incense use is absent, the European folk-medicine use is traditional belief rather than evidence, and this page makes no health claims of any kind.

| Layer | Status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established | *Convallaria majalis*, Asparagaceae; Flora of China and GBIF agree on the species |
| Classical Chinese incense use | None known | No entry in the 香乘 or comparable compendia |
| European folk medicine | Traditional belief | A documented historical practice, not evidence of safety or efficacy |
| Modern perfumery use | Established | A synthetic accord, not a flower extract |
| Toxicity | Established | Cardiac glycosides in all parts |
| Health claims | None made | This page is editorial knowledge content |

The folk-medicine row needs care. The plant has a history of use in European herbal practice, and that history is real; it is also a history of a poison being dosed by people who did not know its margin. Traditional belief is not a safety data sheet. The fact that a practice is old does not make the material less toxic, and the fact that a compound is pharmacologically active is precisely why self-experimentation is a bad idea.

The perfumery row is established in a different sense. There is no question that the muguet accord exists, that it is manufactured, and that it is used across fine fragrance and functional products. The only claim worth qualifying is the marketing one: a fragrance that smells of muguet is not thereby a botanical product.

No medical claims are made here, and none should be inferred from the toxicity discussion. Describing what a poison does is safety information; it is not a promise that the plant can treat anything. Readers who want the general combustion-safety picture should see our [safety guide](/safety/).

## Safe handling, in plain rules

> **Direct answer:** Do not burn it, do not eat it, do not brew it, and do not extract it at home. Wear gloves when handling cut material, keep all parts away from children and pets, and treat dried plants and vase water as contaminated waste.

1. **Never burn any part.** Incense use is not a legitimate application for this plant, and combustion is not a decontamination step.
2. **Never ingest any part.** That includes flowers, leaves, stems, berries, and anything made by steeping them.
3. **Do not make home extracts.** Oils, tinctures, floral waters, and syrups all concentrate or carry the glycosides.
4. **Wear gloves when cutting or dividing.** Sap on the skin is an irritation risk, and hand-to-mouth transfer is the route that matters for children.
5. **Wash hands and tools afterwards.** Secateurs, gloves, and any surface that held cut stems should be cleaned before food contact.
6. **Keep berries and flowers out of reach.** The red berries are the most attractive part and appear in late summer, when gardens are busiest.
7. **Treat dried material as toxic.** A dried sprig in a craft project or a jar is not safer than a fresh one.
8. **Handle vase water as contaminated.** Keep it away from pets and children, and discard it rather than leaving it standing.
9. **Dispose of cuttings in household waste.** Do not add them to compost that will be used on edible beds.
10. **If ingestion is suspected, call a poison service or emergency number immediately.** Bring a piece of the plant for identification if it is safe to do so.

Any incense produces smoke and fine particles, so burn it where air moves and keep your face out of the plume. That rule is about smoke, not about this plant; for 铃兰 the stronger rule is that there should be no combustion at all.

## Editorial view: scent without a material

> **Direct answer:** Lily of the valley is the cleanest case in perfumery of a scent that has been fully separated from its source. The separation is not a compromise forced by fashion; it is the only responsible design, and it is the professional lesson the plant teaches.

Three points follow, and they are editorial judgments rather than neutral facts.

**The accord is a reconstruction, and that is the honest outcome.** Muguet was never a viable natural extract: the flower's volatiles are too fleeting, the yield too small, and the plant too toxic to handle at scale. The synthetic route did not displace a working natural material; it replaced an impossibility. Perfumery is often criticised for preferring molecules to flowers, and in most cases the criticism has some force. Here it has none.

**The safety separation is absolute, and it belongs before the aesthetics.** A page about a beautiful flower invites the reader to imagine the flower in a bowl or a blend. This page has to interrupt that imagining, because the plant is a cardiac-glycoside poison and the ordinary incense gestures — drying, grinding, burning — are all unsafe. The discipline generalises: every poisonous botanical in an encyclopedia needs its warning before its poetry.

**For anyone specifying a fragrance, "lily of the valley" is a note name, not a material name.** A brief that asks for muguet is asking for an accord with a green, dewy, sweet-floral profile. It is not asking for a supplier to ship flowers. A supplier who offers a "lily of the valley" botanical incense material is either confused about the material or misrepresenting it, and either answer is a reason to look elsewhere.

In our view, 铃兰 is best understood as the definitive "scent without a material" case: a beloved note whose namesake is a poison, and whose enduring lesson is that fragrance and safety are separate questions that must both be answered.

## Summary

Lily of the valley (铃兰) is *Convallaria majalis*, a rhizomatous woodland perennial of the asparagus family, native across the northern temperate zone and recorded in northern China. Its nodding white bells inspire the "muguet" note of modern perfumery, a note built from synthetic molecules because the flower's scent is fleeting and the plant is poisonous. All parts contain cardiac glycosides, including convallatoxin and convalloside; drying does not remove them, burning is not safe, and no part should be ingested. There is no classical Chinese incense record and no commercial true essential oil. The plant is an ornamental and a fragrance reference, never a 香材.

## FAQ

**What is lily of the valley (铃兰)?** It is *Convallaria majalis*, a low woodland perennial of the asparagus family with broad basal leaves and nodding white bell flowers. It is native across the northern temperate zone and is recorded in northern China. Its scent is the model for the perfumery note called muguet, and the whole plant is toxic.

**Is lily of the valley poisonous?** Yes. All parts contain cardiac glycosides, chiefly convallatoxin and convalloside, which are toxic if eaten and can irritate skin and eyes. Drying does not remove them, and burning does not make the plant safe. Keep it away from children and pets, wear gloves when handling it, and seek immediate medical advice if any part is swallowed.

**Is lily of the valley used in Chinese incense?** No. It has no classical 香材 status and no documented place in the incense compendia. In China it is a garden and cut flower. The plant's Chinese name, 铃兰, is an ornamental name meaning "bell orchid", not a materials term.

**Is there a real lily of the valley essential oil?** No commercial true essential oil or absolute exists. The flower's volatiles are too fleeting and the plant too toxic for that trade. Products sold as "lily of the valley oil" are fragrance compositions built from muguet molecules such as hydroxycitronellal; they are legitimate perfumery materials but not plant extracts.

**Can I grow lily of the valley in a garden with children or pets?** It is possible to grow it, but the risk is real and the plant is toxic in all parts, including the berries. If the garden is used by small children or by pets that chew plants, the safer choice is not to plant it, or to grow it in a container that cannot be reached. Gloves should be worn for division and cleanup.

**What does lily of the valley smell like?** Green, dewy, and sweet, with a fresh floral centre and a faint jasmine-like edge. Perfumers describe the effect as clean and almost soapy, which is why the note reads as "fresh" rather than "heavy". The natural flower's scent fades within hours of cutting, so the smell in a fragrance is an accord rather than an extract.

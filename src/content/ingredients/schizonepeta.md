> **Direct answer:** Schizonepeta (荆芥, jīngjiè) is the dried aerial herb of *Schizonepeta tenuifolia*, a mint-family annual that current taxonomy usually files as *Nepeta tenuifolia*. It smells green and minty with a warm, faintly peppery edge — closer to a kitchen herb than to a temple incense. Classical texts record it under the name 假苏 (jiǎsū), and its documented role is the fragrant sachet (香囊) and the materia medica, not the burner.

## What kind of material this is

**Direct answer:** 荆芥 is a leaf-and-stem herb, not a wood, a resin, or a root. The whole above-ground plant is harvested and dried, and the aroma lives at the leaf surface rather than in a stored resin.

An annual is a different sort of commodity from a forest aromatic. It is sown, grown, and cut inside one season, and the sack that reaches a blender holds the growth of a single field in a single year. Nothing accumulates over decades. Nothing is aged into value. Two consequences follow for anyone buying it. The supply is ordinary and renewable, so scarcity stories attached to this herb deserve suspicion. Quality is a snapshot, so two lots of the same species can differ more by harvest week and drying method than by region of origin.

The family habit explains the rest. Lamiaceae — the mint family — generally carries square stems, opposite leaves, and lipped flowers, and its aromatic members hold volatile oil in glands on the leaf surface. 荆芥 follows that pattern. The scent is not locked in a heartwood or exuded as a resin; it sits in a thin layer at the surface, which is why the material is fragile in a way that a wood is not.

That fragility shapes the trade. A dense wood can be shipped in a container, stored for years, and sold on a grading scale built around resin density. A dried leaf cannot. Every handling step — cutting, drying, bagging, transport, storage — either preserves the surface glands or breaks them. The buyer who understands this stops asking which mountain the herb came from and starts asking when it was cut and how it was dried.

## The taxonomy changed; the herb did not

**Direct answer:** Older literature places the plant in the small genus *Schizonepeta*; modern treatments sink that genus into *Nepeta*, the catmint genus. Flora of China records it as *Nepeta tenuifolia*, and GBIF treats *Schizonepeta tenuifolia* as a synonym of that name.

The shift is a decision about relationships, not the discovery of a new plant. Growers, harvesters, and traders did not change what they cut. What changed is the label a buyer finds in a database, and therefore the label that belongs on a specification sheet. A supplier who lists only *Schizonepeta* is not wrong. A supplier who lists only *Nepeta* is not wrong either. A supplier who cannot say that the two names point at one plant is telling you something about the paperwork.

The older name still earns its place. It narrows a search to the East Asian herb and separates it from the many ornamental catmints sold under *Nepeta*. Keeping both names visible is not botanical nostalgia; it is a practical hedge against a database that splits one plant across two entries.

There is a mechanism behind the confusion, and it runs through every catalogue:

- a genus is revised → the species is moved to a new name → databases update at different speeds → two entries describe one plant → a buyer comparing suppliers sees "two species" → a substitution is made or a premium is charged for a distinction that does not exist.

The lesson is to match the species epithet, not the genus label. If the specific epithet *tenuifolia* is present and the plant is the East Asian aromatic herb, the material is the same whether the genus reads *Schizonepeta* or *Nepeta*.

## Three names, one herb — and the plants that are not it

**Direct answer:** 荆芥, 假苏, and *Nepeta tenuifolia* all refer to the same plant. 薄荷, 猫薄荷, and 紫苏 do not, despite shared characters or shared family ground.

| Name in circulation | What it names | Status | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|---|---|
| 荆芥 (jīngjiè) | *Schizonepeta tenuifolia* | The modern Chinese name for this herb | The name used in trade and in kitchens |
| 假苏 (jiǎsū) | The same herb, under its classical name | *Bencao Gangmu* entry name | A textual name, not a second species |
| *Nepeta tenuifolia* | The current botanical placement | Accepted in Flora of China and GBIF | Same plant as *S. tenuifolia* |
| 薄荷 (bòhé) | *Mentha haplocalyx* | A different genus | Sharper, menthol-cool, no warm pepper |
| 猫薄荷 (māobòhé) | *Nepeta cataria* | Same genus, different species | Catmint; broader leaf, different scent |
| 紫苏 (zǐsū) | *Perilla frutescens* | A different genus | Purple leaf, perilla aroma, culinary use |

The substitutions that matter are rarely malicious. They come from shared words. 荆芥 and 假苏 are one plant separated by a few centuries of naming. 荆芥 and 薄荷 are two plants separated by a genus, and the same name can point at different materials once a supplier starts working from a list rather than a specimen. 猫薄荷 is the sharpest trap of the set: it sits in *Nepeta* with the herb on this page, so a taxonomic search can surface it as a relative, while its scent and its garden use are something else entirely.

The classical record is worth reading closely here. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), in the section 草之三, lists the herb under 假苏 (jiǎsū) and records 荆芥 among its names: "荊芥（《吳普》）…此即菜中荊芥也". The same entry preserves an older uncertainty about whether the two names covered one plant or two. A modern reader can treat that as a footnote, or as a warning that a name is a claim about identity rather than a guarantee of it.

## Why the scent reads green, not sweet

**Direct answer:** The oil sits at the cool, herbal end of the mint family, without peppermint's menthol blast or spearmint's candy sweetness. It reads as crushed leaf: green first, warm pepper second, sweetness only as a trace.

A leaf holds its volatiles in surface glands. Crush it and the glands break. Warm it and the same compounds lift without any crushing at all. The molecules responsible are light, so they reach the nose early and leave early. That is the physical reason 荆芥 behaves as a top note, and the reason a sachet smells strongest on the day it is filled and on the day it is squeezed.

The chain is short enough to remember:

- dried leaf → gland walls rupture on crushing or heating → light volatiles release within seconds → the green-mint impression peaks → heavier waxes and dry fibre remain → the note flattens into hay.

Drying decides how much of that top note survives. Slow drying in shade or in a cool air stream lets water leave without cooking the leaf. Fast heat collapses cells and drives oil to the surface, where oxygen goes to work on it. The visible signal is colour. A dull grey-green leaf usually still has something to say; a brown or yellow leaf has usually already said it. This is a judgment from handling material, not a laboratory result, and it is the kind of judgment a buyer can make in ten seconds with a warm palm.

Storage repeats the same story at a slower pace. Every time a bag is opened, fresh air replaces the saturated headspace and carries volatiles away. Every warm afternoon accelerates the loss. The herb does not spoil in the way a food spoils; it forgets. What is left is fibre with a tea-like smell, useful as bulk and useless as a top note.

## Where it comes from, and why the origin story is thin

**Direct answer:** The species is native to China and Korea and is grown as a cultivated annual for its aromatic tops. It is a common crop with an ordinary supply chain, so origin claims carry less weight than harvest timing, drying, and cleaning.

Compare the supply model with agarwood or sandalwood. Those materials are valued partly for age, rarity, and the difficulty of obtaining them, and a plantation story, a wild-harvest story, or a resin-density grading system all make sense. None of that transfers to a fast annual herb. The plant is easy to grow, the material is bulky, and the price is set by agricultural cost rather than by scarcity. A vendor who charges a premium for "wild" 荆芥 is selling a story about a plant that is normally cultivated.

What varies is handling. Cut at the right stage, dry it without scorching, keep it off the floor, screen out stems and grit, and the lot is good. Cut it late, dry it in a hot shed, or store it in a damp corner, and the same species becomes hay. The differences between suppliers live there, in decisions that leave no trace on an invoice.

This is also why the herb has no meaningful grade hierarchy of the kind that surrounds precious materials. There is no "superior" and "inferior" mountain. There is fresh and stale, clean and dusty, leafy and stemmy, well dried and scorched. Those are the only distinctions the material supports, and a page that invents more is decorating rather than describing.

## Sachet first, burner second

**Direct answer:** 荆芥 belongs in a sachet, where its volatiles diffuse at room temperature. Burned on a stick it can only be a small accent, because a dry leaf burns fast and hot and leaves almost no base behind.

A sachet works by evaporation, not combustion. Air moves through the cloth, the volatile oil leaves the leaf slowly, and the bag releases a green top note for as long as the oil lasts. Squeezing the bag ruptures fresh glands and gives a short second bloom. Nothing burns, so the note stays green instead of turning smoky. The limitation is the same physics: the light molecules go first, and what remains smells of dry leaf.

A burning stick works by heat. The ember drives off whatever is volatile ahead of the front, and the leaf contributes a sharp opening that is gone almost immediately. Because the leaf has little resin or gum, it also burns hot and fast, adding acrid smoke rather than a slow base. In a stick or a cone, then, 荆芥 is a seasoning: a small fraction that lifts the opening, never the material the blend is built on.

| | Sachet (香囊) | Burned in a stick or cone |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | Evaporation at room temperature | Combustion in a heat front |
| Dose | Grams of dried leaf | A small fraction of the blend |
| Aroma shape | Green top note that fades over days and weeks | Sharp opening, little middle, no base |
| Failure mode | Smells of hay once the volatiles go | Acrid smoke, hot burn, hollow scent |
| Natural partners | Other dried herbs, citrus peel, woody chips | Resins and dense woods that carry the base |
| What it cannot do | Project across a room | Hold a composition together |

What follows for a formulator is a division of labour. Use the herb where delivery is cool and slow — a sachet, a padded pillow, a dry blend in a closed drawer. Use a resin or a dense wood where delivery is heat. Trying to make the leaf do a wood's job produces a stick that opens sharply, smells hollow in the middle, and leaves nothing at the end. The reverse mistake, putting a precious resin in a sachet and expecting it to fill a room, is just as common and just as disappointing.

A workable sachet is built in layers rather than around a single herb. The green note comes from 荆芥 and its relatives; a sweeter aromatic such as citrus peel gives the bag a second register; a heavier material — a woody chip or a mild resin — holds what is left after the top note leaves. The proportions are a matter of taste, but the structure is not. A bag of one dried herb is a smell with a deadline.

## When the herb is not available

**Direct answer:** There is no honest one-for-one substitute for 荆芥. The nearest materials differ in chemistry and in effect, so the choice is between waiting for the right lot and redesigning the top note around a different herb.

| Candidate | What it shares | Where it diverges | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Another lot of *S. tenuifolia* | Species, plant part, general profile | Harvest and drying | The only true substitute |
| 薄荷 (*Mentha haplocalyx*) | Mint-family green note | Colder, menthol-led, much stronger | Not a substitute; it takes over |
| 藿香 (*Agastache rugosa*) | Herbal, slightly sweet | Rounder, more floral-anise, less peppery | A different top note, not a stand-in |
| 紫苏 (*Perilla frutescens*) | Culinary herb, green | Distinct perilla aroma, often purple leaf | Different material entirely |
| 猫薄荷 (*Nepeta cataria*) | Same genus under modern taxonomy | Different species, different scent | Avoid; the name is the only link |

The pattern in that table is worth stating plainly. Substitution works when two materials share a function, not when they share a family. 薄荷 and 荆芥 are both Lamiaceae and both green, and a formulator who swaps one for the other will get a blend that is colder, sharper, and louder. The sachet will still smell pleasant. It will no longer be the blend that was designed.

There is a practical alternative to substitution: change the structure. If the green top note cannot be sourced, a blend can open on citrus peel or a light floral instead, with the herb omitted rather than replaced. That is a redesign, and it should be labelled as one. Quiet substitution is how a product drifts away from its own specification, one unavailable lot at a time.

## Reading a lot before you buy it

**Direct answer:** Grade in a dried herb is a matter of leaf share, colour, scent on crushing, and cleanliness. Stem weight, brown colour, and dust are the usual ways a cheap lot disguises itself.

| Signal | Strong lot | Weak lot | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaf-to-stem ratio | Mostly leaf and fine top | Thick pale stem, few leaves | Stem carries little oil and adds bulk |
| Colour | Dull green to grey-green | Brown, yellow, or blackened | Browning tracks oxidation and rough drying |
| Scent on crushing | Green, minty, faint pepper | Hay, dust, mustiness | The top note has already gone |
| Foreign matter | Clean, few other leaves | Sand, twigs, other herbs | Shows the screening discipline |
| Moisture feel | Dry, crisp, breaks cleanly | Soft, limp, clumps | Raises the risk of mould in store |
| Cut | Whole leaf or coarse | Fine powder with heavy dust | Powder loses aroma faster and hides identity |

Open the bag and look before you smell. A thick pale stem is bulk with little oil. Brown patches mean the leaf was heated or stored wet. Fine powder at the bottom means handling has already broken the glands that hold the aroma. Then take a leaf, crush it between thumb and finger, and smell within a few seconds. The green note should arrive before the dry, tea-like base. A lot that smells of hay in the hand will not improve in a sachet.

Paperwork has a narrower role than sellers suggest. A certificate of analysis is useful for contamination and moisture, and it says nothing about whether the top note survived. Ask for the binomial, the plant part, the harvest year, and the form — whole leaf, coarse cut, or powder. Whole leaf is easier to verify and keeps its aroma longer. Powder is convenient and fades faster. Neither is dishonest, but they are not interchangeable in a blend.

## Storage: air, light, and time

**Direct answer:** Dried 荆芥 is a volatile material held in a paper matrix, and it loses its best note before it loses its looks. Storage is part of quality, not an afterthought.

Three processes run at once. Light molecules evaporate, oxygen attacks what remains, and moisture invites mould. A sealed glass jar or a barrier bag in a dark cupboard slows all three. A clear jar on a sunny shelf speeds them up. A bag left open beside a stove is the fastest way to turn a good lot into hay.

Keep the material whole until use, buy in quantities that will be consumed within a season, and write the harvest year on the container. If a recipe calls for powder, grind a small amount and use it soon; the increased surface area that makes powder convenient also releases the aroma into the air of the storage room. Do not freeze dried herb unless it is sealed against moisture, because the condensation cycle on repeated opening does more damage than the cold prevents.

There is a practical significance here for anyone who blends at home. The first jar of a herb is usually the best one a buyer will own, because it is the freshest. Everything after that is a race against the headspace. Small packages are not a premium affectation; they are the only way to keep the top note in the material instead of in the room.

## Tradition, evidence, and the line this page draws

**Direct answer:** The classical record is genuine. The medical claims sometimes built on top of it are not established, and this page does not repeat them.

Traditional use is evidence of a tradition. It tells us that people classified, harvested, and used a plant in a particular way. It does not tell us that a modern therapeutic claim is true. Where this page mentions the materia-medica role, it means exactly that and no more. We do not convert a classical entry into a health promise, and we do not dismiss the entry as worthless because it is old. The honest position is narrower than either: this is a documented aromatic herb with a long record, and its medicinal uses are traditional belief rather than established clinical fact.

A common misconception is that an entry in a great compendium settles the question of identity. It does not. The *Bencao Gangmu* is a record of what was known, believed, and debated in its own time, and it preserves the debate rather than dissolving it. That is precisely what makes it useful to a modern reader. It shows that the people closest to the material were unsure which names belonged to which plant, and it gives us permission to be careful rather than credulous.

There is a second discipline in the naming history. The compendium itself flagged that two names might cover one plant or two. That is an unusually modern gesture — an admission of uncertainty inside an authoritative text — and it gives the buyer a model. When a name has been disputed for centuries, the response is not to pick the most convenient reading. The response is to confirm the species and say which name you are using.

The distinction matters for how the page is read. A claim that 荆芥 "is used for" a condition belongs to the history of medicine, not to the chemistry of a sachet. A claim that 荆芥 smells green and fades quickly belongs to the material, and it can be checked by anyone with a bag of the dried herb. Keeping those two kinds of statement apart is the whole editorial discipline of this entry.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The main risk is smoke, not the herb. Burning anything indoors adds particulate matter to the air, and a sachet avoids combustion without becoming a medicine.

Burn in a ventilated space, keep the ember away from fabric, paper, and children, and do not leave a lit stick unattended. Stop using any aromatic product if it irritates the eyes, nose, or throat. A sachet is a scent object, not a remedy, and it should not be placed where a child or a pet can chew it.

The herb itself is ordinary plant material. Handling dried leaf raises dust, and anyone who knows they react to plant dust should open bags outdoors or wear a simple mask. That is housekeeping rather than a medical warning, and it applies to every dried herb in this encyclopedia.

## FAQ

**What is schizonepeta (荆芥)?** The dried aerial herb of *Schizonepeta tenuifolia* (syn. *Nepeta tenuifolia*), a mint-family annual native to China and Korea. It has a green, minty, faintly spicy scent and belongs to the sachet and materia-medica tradition rather than to the lead incense materials.

**Is 荆芥 the same as 假苏?** Yes. 假苏 (jiǎsū) is the classical name under which the *Bencao Gangmu* lists the herb, with 荆芥 recorded among its other names. The two labels point at one plant; the classical entry also preserves an old debate about whether they named one species or two.

**Is 荆芥 the same as mint or catnip?** No. 薄荷 is *Mentha haplocalyx*, a different genus with a colder, menthol-led scent. 猫薄荷 is *Nepeta cataria*, a relative in the same genus as the herb's modern placement, but a different species with a different leaf and smell.

**Does 荆芥 belong in incense?** As a small accent, yes; as a lead material, no. Its dry leaf burns fast and hot and leaves little base, so it works best as a green top note in a blend or as the aromatic charge of a sachet.

**Why do suppliers list two scientific names?** Because the genus has been revised. Older literature uses *Schizonepeta tenuifolia*; modern treatments place the plant in *Nepeta* as *Nepeta tenuifolia*. The plant is the same, and a specification should accept either name while requiring the epithet *tenuifolia*.

**How should I store dried 荆芥?** Whole, sealed, dark, and cool. Buy small amounts, label the harvest year, and grind only what a recipe needs. The herb loses its green top note long before it looks spoiled.

## Summary

荆芥 is a small, ordinary herb with an unusually layered paper trail. Botanically it is *Schizonepeta tenuifolia*, now usually *Nepeta tenuifolia*, an annual of the mint family grown across China and Korea. Aromatically it is a green, faintly peppery top note that lives in surface glands and leaves the material quickly. Historically it is 假苏, a name the *Bencao Gangmu* preserved along with its own uncertainty about identity. Its proper place is the sachet and the materia-medica record, not the centre of a burning blend. The buyer who confirms the binomial, inspects the leaf, and stores it small will get more from it than any origin story can provide.

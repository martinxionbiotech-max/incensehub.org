> **Direct answer:** Grapefruit (葡萄柚, pútáoyòu) is *Citrus × paradisi*, a rue-family (Rutaceae) evergreen that exists only in cultivation, as a cross between the pomelo and the sweet orange. The coloured outer rind yields a cold-pressed oil that smells fresh, bittersweet and faintly green-woody. Nothing in the classical Chinese incense record mentions the plant, and the reason is botanical rather than editorial: the hybrid did not exist in China while that canon was forming.

## Identity: what the name actually covers

> **Direct answer:** *Citrus × paradisi* is a small evergreen grown for its fruit, and the aromatic material is the coloured outer layer of the rind. Juice, pulp and wood are not fragrance materials.

The tree is compact for a citrus, with glossy ovate leaves and white flowers. Its fruit hangs in clusters, which is where the English name came from: the bunches looked like grapes to Caribbean growers. That detail is worth keeping because it explains the name without implying any relationship to the vine.

The rind has two layers that matter commercially. The flavedo is the thin coloured skin, and it is packed with spherical oil glands. Under it sits the albedo, the thick white sponge that carries most of the bitterness and almost none of the scent. When a supplier says "grapefruit peel oil", the useful question is whether the oil came from the flavedo alone or from whole-peel maceration, because the albedo contributes a flat, pithy note and dilutes the top.

Cultivars matter more than most buyers expect. Marsh is the older white-fleshed type; Ruby Red, Star Ruby and Rio Red are pigmented selections with different acid-sugar balance. Pigment does not determine oil quality, but cultivar and maturity do shift the ratio of nootkatone to limonene, and a buyer comparing two oils from different cultivars is not comparing like with like.

The multiplication sign in the botanical name is not decoration. It marks a hybrid, and a hybrid does not breed true from seed. Every commercial grapefruit is propagated by grafting, which means the planted population is genetically narrow and the "species" is really a managed clone set. The accepted name and family placement are recorded at [GBIF's *Citrus × paradisi* page](https://www.gbif.org/species/7469645).

## Provenance: why there is no classical Chinese entry

> **Direct answer:** Grapefruit arose in the West Indies in the eighteenth century as a chance cross of pomelo and sweet orange. Chinese incense literature was already centuries old by then, so the plant's absence from it is a matter of timing, not of lost knowledge.

The story is short and unusually well bounded. A pomelo and a sweet orange growing near each other in the Caribbean produced a seedling with intermediate fruit; growers noticed, selected it, and carried it into orchards. The plant reached Florida in the nineteenth century and became a commercial crop there, then spread through the warm citrus belts of the Americas, the Mediterranean and subtropical Asia.

Chinese cultivation is later still. The tree grows in southern China now, and nursery stock is ordinary, but it is an introduced crop with no native wild population. Flora of China does not treat it as an indigenous species, and no Chinese materia medica of the imperial period gives it an entry.

That timeline settles the editorial question. A material can be absent from the classical canon for two very different reasons: it was known and not used, or it was not known at all. Grapefruit is the second case. Ming-dynasty compendia such as 香乘 could not have listed a Caribbean hybrid that European botanists had not yet described, and a modern page that implies otherwise is inventing a lineage.

The useful consequence for a reader is a clean test. When a seller describes a grapefruit incense as "traditional" or "ancient Chinese", the claim is false on its face, whatever the rest of the description says. The plant's own history rules it out.

## The naming trail: 葡萄柚, 西柚 and 柚

> **Direct answer:** 葡萄柚 is a modern descriptive coinage meaning "grape pomelo", and 西柚 ("western pomelo") is the everyday southern Chinese name for the same fruit. Neither term is classical, and both contain 柚, the word for pomelo, which is exactly where confusion starts.

The characters map the history. 葡萄 means grape, borrowed for the fruit's clustered habit; 柚 is pomelo, the citrus Chinese growers already knew. The compound name says, in effect, "the foreign citrus that fruits in bunches". It is a calque of the English word rather than a term inherited from any herbal tradition.

西柚 works the same way from the other direction: 西, "west", marks the fruit as an import. In Guangdong and Hong Kong, 西柚 is the name a greengrocer will use; 葡萄柚 is the more formal and more northern written form. They are the same fruit, and a label that lists them as different products is either confused or trading on the confusion.

Then there is 柚 on its own. 柚 is *Citrus maxima*, the pomelo, a much larger fruit with a thick spongy peel that has a long history in southern China as food, offering fruit and a citrus note in incense. The peeled rind is 柚皮, treated separately on this site under [pomelo peel](/ingredients/pomelo-peel/). The grapefruit is the pomelo's child, not its synonym.

A practical note for anyone reading Chinese supplier lists: 柚子 almost always means pomelo, 西柚 almost always means grapefruit, and 胡柚 refers to a different hybrid group altogether. The characters are short and easy to skim past, and a one-character difference changes the species, the price and the scent.

## Grapefruit against pomelo: ancestor and descendant

> **Direct answer:** The two are different species with different fruit, peel and scent. Pomelo is the larger, older, Asian parent; grapefruit is the smaller, modern, American hybrid, and its oil is sharper, more sulfurous and more bitter.

| | Grapefruit (葡萄柚 / 西柚) | Pomelo (柚 / 柚子) |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Citrus × paradisi* | *Citrus maxima* |
| Status | cultivated hybrid (pomelo × sweet orange) | species, native to Southeast Asia |
| Fruit | 10–15 cm, thin rind, pink or white flesh | often 15–25 cm, very thick spongy rind |
| Peel oil | sharp, sulfurous, bitter-woody | softer, floral-bittersweet, less sulfur |
| Chinese history | modern import, no classical entry | long cultivation in southern China |
| Incense standing | none in the classical canon | traditional citrus-peel note |
| Typical use | perfumery, home fragrance | peel for incense, tea, cooking |

The table is not a ranking. Pomelo peel is the more interesting incense material precisely because it has a documented life in Chinese practice, and its oil is gentler on the nose. Grapefruit has the more assertive top note and the more modern commercial story.

What follows from the parentage is a trap worth naming. Because the pomelo is one of the grapefruit's two parents, a supplier can describe grapefruit peel as "pomelo-type" and be technically accurate about ancestry while being useless about identity. Ancestry is not identity. A buyer who wants the traditional material should ask for 柚皮 and expect a thick, pale, spongy peel; a buyer who wants the modern top note should ask for expressed grapefruit oil and expect a liquid, not a peel.

## The citrus confusion table

> **Direct answer:** Six citrus materials turn up under overlapping Chinese and English names, and only two of them have a classical Chinese incense standing. Matching the name to the species is the first step in any sourcing decision.

| Name | Species | Material | Scent register | Classical incense standing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 葡萄柚 / 西柚 | *Citrus × paradisi* | cold-pressed rind oil | sharp, sulfurous, bittersweet | none — modern hybrid |
| 柚 / 柚子 / 柚皮 | *Citrus maxima* | dried peel, peel oil | soft, floral-bittersweet | traditional peel note |
| 香橼 | *Citrus medica* | peel, candied fruit | thick, sweet-floral, faintly soapy | classical fragrance fruit |
| 佛手 | *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis* | peel, whole fruit | bright, floral, lemon-like | classical fragrance fruit |
| 柠檬 | *Citrus × limon* | rind oil | clean, sharp, acidic | modern oil, limited classical use |
| 甜橙 / 橙 | *Citrus sinensis* | rind oil, dried peel | round, sweet, orange-flower edge | peel used, oil modern |

Two patterns fall out of the table. First, the classical materials are fruits that could be grown, offered whole, candied or peeled in China; the modern materials are oils extracted for perfumery. Second, the English names collapse distinctions the Chinese names keep. "Citrus" covers all six, "lemon" covers two, and "grapefruit" covers a fruit whose Chinese name advertises its pomelo parentage.

For a collector, the table is a screening tool rather than a shopping list. A jar labelled "citrus peel" with no species is not a material yet; it is a category. The species determines the scent, the classical standing and the price band, and no amount of descriptive prose substitutes for it. ## Inside the peel: the chemistry of the smell

> **Direct answer:** The oil is mostly limonene, with smaller amounts of other terpenes, the ketone nootkatone and a trace sulfur compound that supplies the characteristic "grapefruit" sharpness. The bitterness and the freshness come from different fractions, which is why the oil changes as it ages.

Limonene is the bulk of the oil, and on its own it smells like orange peel and paint thinner at the same time: bright, solvent-like, short-lived. It is the carrier, not the signature. Myrcene and pinene add a resinous green edge, and small amounts of aldehydes give the "just-cut fruit" impression.

Nootkatone is the compound most often named in grapefruit work. It is a sesquiterpene ketone, first isolated from the Nootka cypress and later identified as a major contributor to grapefruit aroma. It smells dry, woody and bitter-citrus, and it lingers far longer than limonene. If the top of the oil is the fruit, nootkatone is the pith and the peel oil underneath it.

The sharpest character comes from a sulfur compound, 1-p-menthene-8-thiol, sometimes called grapefruit mercaptan. It is present at parts-per-billion levels and detectable at far lower concentrations, and it is the reason a good grapefruit oil can smell almost rude next to a bland orange oil. Remove it and the oil reads like generic citrus; overdose it and the oil reads like rubber.

Expressed citrus oils also carry coumarins and furocoumarins, including bergamottin, picked up from the peel along with the waxes. These are not aroma compounds in any useful sense. They matter because they are the fraction responsible for phototoxicity on skin, and because their presence distinguishes a cold-pressed oil from a distilled one.

## Why "bittersweet" is the right word

> **Direct answer:** The word describes two separate sensations arriving together: a sweet citrus lift from limonene and a dry bitter tail from nootkatone and the coumarins. Neither fraction alone explains the effect.

Smell the oil twice, once immediately and once after ten minutes on a strip, and the structure becomes obvious. The first pass is all brightness: limonene, a little green from pinene, a suggestion of flowers. The second pass is drier, with a bitter edge and a woody shadow that was not obvious at the start.

The chain runs like this: the oil glands rupture → limonene and the light terpenes evaporate first → the thiol and nootkatone remain on the surface → the residue smells dry, bitter and woody → the overall impression shifts from "fruit" to "peel". That is why a grapefruit note in perfumery is often described as a top note with a bitter tail rather than a simple citrus.

There is a second chain at work in the mouth, and it explains the word "bitter" more precisely. The albedo contains flavanone glycosides such as naringin, which taste bitter and are not volatile. A cold-pressed oil that carries pith debris therefore has a bitterness the nose reads as "grapefruit" but which is really taste-adjacent, a learned association rather than a smell. Filtration removes most of it; over-filtration removes some of the character with it.

For a formulator, the practical consequence is that grapefruit is not a substitute for lemon at the same dose. Lemon is a clean acid line; grapefruit is a bright line with a bitter shadow. Swapping one for the other changes the structure of a blend, not just its flavour.

## From fruit to oil: four routes

> **Direct answer:** Cold pressing is the standard route for grapefruit peel oil, because it preserves the thiol and the juicy top. Steam distillation, folding and furocoumarin removal are the other three routes, and each changes the smell in a predictable direction.

Cold pressing is mechanical. The rind is scarified or pressed, the oil is collected with water, separated by centrifuge, chilled to drop waxes and filtered. No heat is applied beyond what friction generates, so the thiol survives and the oil keeps its juicy edge. The trade-off is that everything else in the peel survives too: waxes, coumarins, and a certain amount of pith.

Steam distillation passes steam through the peel and condenses the oil. Heat and water do two things: they strip the most fragile sulfur compounds and they leave the heavier, less volatile fraction behind in the still. The result is a cleaner, more terpenic oil that smells like citrus peel rather than citrus fruit. It is a legitimate product, and it is not the same product.

Folding concentrates an oil by removing part of the terpene fraction, usually under vacuum. A five-fold or ten-fold oil is much stronger and much more expensive per kilo, and it is also less nuanced, because the concentration step does not preserve every minor component in proportion.

Furocoumarin-free treatment removes the phototoxic fraction, typically by vacuum distillation or solvent partitioning. The oil becomes safer for skin use and slightly less round on top. A label that says FCF without saying which treatment was used has told the buyer half of what matters.

The route matters because the four products are not interchangeable in a formula. A distilled oil at the same percentage will smell thinner and will not carry the bitter tail; a folded oil will dominate a blend that was built around a single-fold material.

## What processing does to the character

> **Direct answer:** Every step that removes impurities also removes some aroma. Heat strips the sulfur top, filtration strips the bitter body, and concentration flattens the middle.

Trace the heat chain and the outcome is predictable. Steam and heat reach the oil → the thiol, which is the most fragile part of the signature, degrades first → nootkatone stays behind in the peel or the still → the condensate is bright but shallow → the oil smells like citrus in general and grapefruit in particular only if you are told what it is.

The filtration chain runs the other way. Chilling and filtering remove waxes that would cloud the oil, and they also remove pith particles carrying naringin → the oil becomes cleaner and less bitter → the "bittersweet" balance tips toward sweet → a taster who knows the fruit finds the oil polite but incomplete.

Neither chain is a defect. They are trade-offs, and the right choice depends on the job. A candle or a reed diffuser benefits from the cleaner, cheaper distilled or folded oil, because the wick and the reed do not reward subtlety. A fine fragrance or a high-end incense paste benefits from the expressed oil, because the bitter tail is the point.

The trap is buying on price without knowing the route. Distilled and folded oils are cheaper per unit of odour strength, so a cost comparison against expressed oil is meaningless unless the comparison is per finished formula, not per kilo.

## Reading a grapefruit label: a working grade table

> **Direct answer:** Five labels cover most of the market, and each implies a different process, a different smell and a different risk. The label should name the process, not just the fruit.

| Label | What it should mean | How it reads | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold-pressed / expressed | mechanical pressing of fresh rind, no steam | juicy, sharp, bitter-woody tail | coumarin content; phototoxicity warning |
| Steam-distilled | steam through peel, condensate collected | cleaner, more terpenic, shallow | thiol largely absent; not a fault, a type |
| Folded (5-fold, 10-fold) | terpenes partly removed under vacuum | very strong, less nuanced | fold number; price per odour unit |
| FCF | furocoumarins removed after pressing | close to expressed, slightly rounder | which removal method; residual coumarins |
| "Grapefruit type" | reconstruction from isolates and other citrus | uniform, thin, no bitter body | not a single-plant oil; must be declared |

The first four are single-material products with different process histories. The fifth is a flavour-house construct, and it is legitimate as long as it is labelled as such. The problem is not reconstitution; the problem is a reconstituted oil sold as expressed peel oil, because the buyer then pays single-origin prices for a blend.

Two checks catch most of the mislabelling. The first is documentary: a credible supplier can name the cultivar, the country of origin, the extraction route and the lot. The second is sensory: expressed grapefruit oil has a bitter tail that distilled and reconstructed oils lack, and a taster who has smelled both will not confuse them.

## Reading the fruit before you read the oil

> **Direct answer:** A grapefruit's rind tells you something about the oil inside it: thin, gland-dense peel from a ripe fruit carries more aroma than thick, puffy peel from an early pick. The cue is useful at a market and useless on a drum.

Start with thickness. The flavedo is where the oil lives, and a fruit with a thin coloured layer and a tight albedo has proportionally more oil-bearing tissue than one with a thick white sponge. Growers see the same variation in the field: heavy irrigation and fast growth push size at the expense of oil density.

Then look at the surface. Fine, closely spaced glands give a pebbled texture and a stronger smell when the fruit is scratched; a smooth, waxy surface often means a younger fruit or a different cultivar. Colour is a weaker cue, because pigmented and white-fleshed cultivars differ by genetics rather than ripeness, and commercial waxing hides the skin.

Weight is the least useful test. A heavy fruit is a juicy fruit, and juice is not the product. The buyer who squeezes for weight is measuring the part of the fruit that the oil trade discards.

The boundary matters. These cues describe fruit for eating or for small-scale pressing, where the peel is processed within hours. A commercial drum has passed through pressing, chilling, filtration and months of storage, and by then the only reliable evidence is the document and the strip. Reading the fruit is a garden skill, not a purchasing protocol.

## Sourcing checks that catch a wrong oil

> **Direct answer:** Identity, route and freshness are three separate questions, and a certificate of analysis answers only the first. Buyers who treat a CoA as a complete quality document tend to overpay for average oil.

Identity is the species question. *Citrus × paradisi* oil should be distinguishable from pomelo, sweet orange and lemon oils by composition, and a supplier who cannot state the species in writing has not established identity. The smell alone is not proof: a blend of orange terpenes with a trace of nootkatone and thiol can pass a casual nose.

Route is the process question. Expressed, distilled and folded oils behave differently in a formula, and the label should say which one is in the drum. This is the check buyers skip most often, because the word "grapefruit" feels like enough information.

Freshness is the age question. Citrus oils oxidise, and an old oil can be perfectly authentic and still smell tired. Ask for the fill date and the lot, and expect a document that ties the drum to a production batch rather than a generic specification sheet.

The fourth check is less formal: price sanity within a category. Expressed grapefruit oil and folded grapefruit oil sit in different price bands, and an offer that undercuts the band by a wide margin is usually a different product or a diluted one. This is not a rule about honesty in general; it is a rule about how commodity markets work.

## Storage: the slow turn from fresh to stale

> **Direct answer:** Citrus oil fails by oxidation, and the change is one-way. Light, warmth and headspace accelerate it; a full, dark, cold bottle slows it.

The chemistry is straightforward. Oxygen attacks limonene → limonene converts to carveol, carvone and p-cymene → the oil acquires a stale, minty-turpentine edge → the bright top disappears and the bitter tail dominates. A three-year-old bottle can still be "pure" and still be unusable in a fine fragrance.

Three habits extend shelf life. Keep the bottle full, because the air above the liquid is the oxidant; decant into small containers rather than opening a kilo drum weekly. Keep it dark, because light catalyses the same reactions. Keep it cold, because reaction rates fall with temperature, and citrus oils are the one family where household refrigeration is normal trade practice.

There is a boundary worth stating. Oxidation is not the same as contamination, and a faintly stale oil is not necessarily unsafe; it is simply no longer the material the formula was built around. For incense, where the oil is a minor component of a burning mixture, a slightly oxidized citrus oil is often undetectable. For a perfume, where the top note is the first thing a customer smells, it is fatal.

## Forms, and what each is for

> **Direct answer:** The oil is the fragrance form; the fruit and the dried peel are food and culinary materials. Choosing a form is choosing a supply chain, not a strength setting.

| Form | What it is | Where it fits | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expressed rind oil | cold-pressed flavedo oil | fine fragrance, premium incense paste | phototoxic; oxidises |
| Distilled rind oil | steam-distilled peel oil | candles, diffusers, soap | shallow top, no bitter tail |
| Folded oil | concentrated terpene-reduced oil | industrial fragrance, strong throw | less nuance, higher cost |
| FCF oil | furocoumarin-reduced expressed oil | skin-contact products | process must be declared |
| Dried peel | culinary peel, not a fragrance stock | cooking, tea, garnish | weak, pithy when burned |
| Fresh fruit | food | eating, juice, zest | not an incense material |

The table also explains a common disappointment. A buyer who dries supermarket peel and burns it expects the oil's perfume and gets scorched pith instead, because the aromatic glands are thin, the peel is mostly albedo and the water content is high. The oil is a concentrate; the peel is not.

For incense work the practical answer is usually expressed oil at a low percentage, or a distilled oil where cost and colour matter more than fidelity. Dried peel belongs in the kitchen unless it has been specifically prepared and stored for burning.

## How the oil behaves in a blend

> **Direct answer:** Grapefruit is a top note with an unusually long shadow for a citrus. It lifts a blend in the first minute and leaves a bitter-woody trace that most citrus oils do not.

In perfumery the citrus family is called hesperidic, after the golden apples of the Hesperides, and grapefruit sits at the sharp end of it. It is brighter than orange, less floral than bergamot, and more bitter than lemon. That combination makes it a useful opener for woody and aromatic compositions, where a clean citrus lift keeps the top from feeling heavy.

The pairing logic is structural rather than a list of favourites. Grapefruit with rosemary or lavender gives an aromatic-hesperidic accord that reads clean and somewhat medicinal. Grapefruit with cedar or vetiver gives a dry, grown-up citrus over wood, the bitter tail meeting the resin. Grapefruit with neroli or jasmine softens the sharpness and pushes the blend toward floral. Grapefruit with black pepper adds a dry spice that echoes the thiol's bite.

Dosage is where the material punishes enthusiasm. Citrus top notes are cheap in effect and expensive in balance: a little opens a blend, a lot flattens it into "cleaning product". The usual instinct to add more because the top fades is the wrong response, because the top always fades; that is what a top note is.

For an incense paste, the same logic applies at a smaller scale. The oil is a surface note on the stick, and it will be smelled before lighting and in the first moments after. It cannot carry a stick on its own, and a stick built around it will smell like an empty room ten minutes in.

## How grapefruit behaves in incense

> **Direct answer:** Grapefruit oil is a surface top note in incense, not a structural material. Most of it leaves the stick before the ember reaches it, so the citrus impression is strongest cold and in the first minute of burning.

The mechanism explains the disappointment. The ember advances → heat conducts into the stick ahead of the burn line → the most volatile terpenes vaporise before they can combust → the citrus reaches the nose early and briefly → what actually burns is the less volatile residue, the binder and the wood or charcoal base. A stick that smells wonderful in the box can smell like nothing much in the room.

Three practical responses follow. First, dose for the cold throw as well as the burn, because a large part of the experience happens before ignition. Second, anchor the citrus with a heavier material, since a top note with no base is a short conversation. Third, accept that a citrus-forward incense is a lights-on experience rather than a long burn, and design the rest of the blend around that.

There is an editorial boundary here. None of this makes grapefruit a bad incense material; it makes it a top note, and top notes behave this way across the family. The mistake is expecting a citrus oil to do the work of a resin.

## Diffuser, candle, stick: three delivery systems

> **Direct answer:** The same oil reads differently depending on how it is released. A diffuser evaporates it at room temperature, a candle heats it, and a stick burns it, and each route exposes a different fraction of the oil to the nose.

A reed diffuser works by capillary action and passive evaporation. The oil climbs the reed, meets room-temperature air and releases the lightest terpenes first. The result is a fresh, thin, continuous citrus that fades in the bottle long before the bottle is empty, and the bitter tail becomes proportionally stronger as the top drains away. Diffusers are the most forgiving format for a cheap oil and the least rewarding for an expensive one.

A candle adds heat but not flame contact. The melt pool warms to well below boiling, so the mid-weight compounds come off along with the terpenes, and the throw is fuller than a diffuser's. The limit is the wax: citrus oils are volatile enough to migrate and to accelerate the wax's own oxidation, so a grapefruit candle often smells best in its first third.

A stick is the harshest of the three. Combustion reaches several hundred degrees at the ember, and the heat front ahead of it drives off the volatiles before they can burn. What reaches the room is a fast citrus puff followed by whatever the base materials contribute. The stick is the format where grapefruit is most clearly a top note and least able to pretend otherwise.

| Delivery | Energy input | What dominates | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reed diffuser | none, passive | light terpenes, then bitter residue | cheap oil is adequate; refresh the reeds |
| Candle | gentle heat | terpenes plus mid-notes | check wax compatibility; expect fading |
| Incense stick | combustion | flash-off top, then base | dose for cold throw, anchor with a base |

The judgment that follows is simple. Match the oil grade to the delivery, not to the price list. An expressed oil in a diffuser is mostly wasted subtlety; a distilled oil in a fine fragrance is mostly missing body. The same drum can be right for one format and wrong for the next.

## Modern use, without the health claims

> **Direct answer:** Grapefruit oil is used as a fragrance in perfumery, candles, diffusers, room sprays and cleaning products. Its documented role is aromatic; no therapeutic claim is made here.

Perfumery uses the oil for the reasons above: a sharp natural top with a bitter tail that reads more sophisticated than orange. Home fragrance uses it because citrus sells, and because the note survives encapsulation and candle formulation well enough to be recognisable. Cleaning products use it as a scent marker for "fresh", a association strong enough that the smell now carries the meaning.

Aromatherapy is the category where language needs care. The oil is used in diffuser blends and massage oils, and there is a large popular literature around it. This page does not repeat efficacy claims, because the evidence base for specific outcomes is not something an ingredient reference can responsibly assert. What can be said is narrower and still useful: the oil has a defined composition, a defined sensory character and a defined set of handling risks.

That distinction matters for buyers as much as for readers. A supplier who markets on therapeutic claims is telling you something about their regulatory posture, not about the oil. A supplier who documents composition, origin and process is giving you information you can actually use.

## What the record supports, and what it does not

> **Direct answer:** Botany, parentage and oil chemistry are documented. Classical Chinese use is not, and no medical effect is established here. The honest page is mostly a botany page.

The layers separate cleanly. The species is established: *Citrus × paradisi*, Rutaceae, a hybrid of pomelo and sweet orange. The parentage is established by both morphology and genetic work. The oil chemistry is characterised, including the terpene profile, nootkatone and the sulfur thiol. The introduction history is documented well enough to date the plant's spread.

What is not established is any classical Chinese incense role, and this page does not manufacture one. Nor is there a body of clinical evidence that would justify a therapeutic claim, and none is made. Traditional belief about citrus oils as "cleansing" or "uplifting" exists in modern popular practice; it is a belief, not a finding, and it is labelled as such when mentioned.

For a reader, the practical significance is that grapefruit can be enjoyed as what it is: a nineteenth- and twentieth-century commercial citrus with a genuinely distinctive oil. It does not need a forged pedigree to be interesting, and the forgery is the only thing that would make it untrustworthy.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** Burning incense produces smoke and particulate matter, and expressed citrus oils can be phototoxic on skin. Ventilate the room, keep the oil off skin before sun exposure, and treat the oil as a concentrate.

Combustion is the first risk and the one most often ignored. Any burning incense releases fine particles and combustion gases; a small room with a closed door concentrates them. Burn in a ventilated space, keep the stick away from curtains and children, and do not treat a pleasant smell as evidence of clean air.

The oil carries its own handling rules. Expressed grapefruit oil contains furocoumarins, which can cause phototoxic reactions when treated skin is exposed to sunlight; FCF grades exist for that reason. Dilute before any skin contact, keep it away from eyes, and do not ingest the oil. Citrus oils are also flammable, so keep bottles away from flame and wipe up spills.

Pets and small children deserve a specific mention because incense and diffusers run for hours in enclosed rooms. Cats in particular are sensitive to many essential-oil constituents, and a diffuser in a closed bedroom is a different exposure from a stick burned in a ventilated kitchen. The safe default is ventilation, short sessions and no direct exposure.

One more scoping note. Grapefruit juice is the subject of a well-documented food–drug interaction literature, which concerns ingestion and intestinal metabolism. That literature is outside the scope of an incense material page, and nothing here is medical advice. See our [safety guide](/safety/) for the general incense-safety framework.

## An editorial view: provenance is a discipline, not a disclaimer

> **Direct answer:** Grapefruit is the cleanest test case in the citrus family for honest provenance. Its absence from the classical canon is a fact about the plant, and stating it plainly is more valuable than inventing a lineage.

Three judgments follow, and they are editorial rather than documentary.

The first is that "modern" is not a downgrade. A material can be recent, cultivated, commercial and still worth a page, a formula and a collector's attention. The habit of retrofitting antiquity onto modern materials comes from marketing, not from history, and it produces pages that are useless to anyone trying to source or date a material.

The second is that ancestry is a trap for the unwary. The pomelo is the grapefruit's parent, the Chinese names share a character, and the peel oils sit in the same family. None of that makes grapefruit a traditional Chinese material. The distinction matters because the two materials behave differently in a blend and cost different amounts, and a buyer who conflates them will get the wrong drum.

The third is that the oil, not the fruit, is the material. Most of the confusion in the market comes from people comparing a fruit they have eaten with an oil they have bought. The oil is a concentrate of the flavedo, with the pith, the juice and the water removed. Judging it by the fruit is like judging a perfume by the flower.

What follows for the encyclopedia is a rule I would apply to every modern crossover: state the origin, state the route, state the limits, and let the material stand on its chemistry. A reader who wants tradition can go to pomelo peel, citron or finger citron. A reader who wants a sharp modern top note knows where to find one.

## Summary

Grapefruit (葡萄柚) is the fruit and peel of *Citrus × paradisi*, a Rutaceae hybrid of pomelo and sweet orange that appeared in the West Indies in the eighteenth century and spread through the citrus-growing world after that. Its flavedo yields a cold-pressed oil whose character comes from limonene, nootkatone and a trace sulfur thiol, with furocoumarins riding along as a safety consideration. It has no classical Chinese incense standing, and that absence is explained by the plant's own timeline rather than by any gap in the record. In practice the material is a top note: sharp, bittersweet, short-lived on the stick, and best used with a heavier anchor. The buyer's real questions are species, route and freshness, in that order.

## FAQ

**What is grapefruit (葡萄柚) in one sentence?** The fruit and rind of *Citrus × paradisi*, a cultivated hybrid of the pomelo and the sweet orange, whose cold-pressed peel oil is used as a fresh, bittersweet top note in modern perfumery and home fragrance.

**Does grapefruit appear anywhere in the classical Chinese incense canon?** No. The hybrid arose in the Caribbean in the eighteenth century, long after that canon took shape, and it has no entry in the classical literature. The Chinese name is a recent description rather than an inherited term, and the oil belongs to modern perfumery.

**Are 葡萄柚 and 西柚 the same thing?** Yes. Both name *Citrus × paradisi*; 西柚 is the everyday southern Chinese term and 葡萄柚 the more formal written one. Neither is the pomelo, which is 柚 or 柚子, a different species with a much older Chinese history.

**Why does bottled grapefruit oil smell sharper than the fruit?** Because the oil is a concentrate of the coloured rind, not the juice. The thiol that gives grapefruit its bite is present at trace levels and survives cold pressing, while the sugar and water that soften the fruit are absent. The result reads more aggressive and more bitter than a mouthful of fruit.

**Can I burn dried grapefruit peel as incense?** You can, but it will not smell like the oil. The peel is mostly white pith and water, the oil glands are thin, and burning it produces a scorched, pithy smoke with only a ghost of citrus. The oil is the fragrance material; the peel is a kitchen ingredient.

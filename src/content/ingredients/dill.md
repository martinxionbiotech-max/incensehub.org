> **Direct answer:** Dill (莳萝, shíluó) is *Anethum graveolens*, a fast-growing annual of the carrot family grown for its thread-like leaves and its small ribbed seeds. Both taste clean, green, and faintly liquorice-like, and both belong to the kitchen rather than the censer. Chinese sources record the plant as a seasoning 香药 under the names 慈谋勒 and 小茴香, not as a material of classical blended incense.

That last point is worth stating before anything else, because the internet is untidy about it. Dill is an aromatic plant. It is not, in the Chinese tradition, an incense wood or resin. Its page belongs in an incense encyclopedia for two honest reasons: the material has a documented Chinese aromatic record, and its naming history is a textbook case of how a single label can point at two different plants. Everything below is written with that framing.

## The plant in the hand

> **Direct answer:** Dill is a slender annual with hollow stems, finely cut leaves, and flat umbels of tiny yellow flowers that ripen into small brown seeds. The whole plant is aromatic, but the seed carries the strongest and most stable scent.

The leaf is the part most cooks know. It is divided so finely that it looks like green thread, and it bruises easily, which is why it is added late to a dish. The stem is hollow and ribbed, and the plant bolts quickly in heat — a trait that makes it a poor companion for a long growing season and a good one for a short cool one.

The flower head is an umbel: a spoke-like arrangement of small flowers on a common centre, characteristic of the carrot family. The seeds that follow are technically fruits — schizocarps, each splitting into two halves — and they are what the spice trade calls dill seed. They are brown, oval, longitudinally ribbed, and light for their size.

Botanically the plant is an annual, reproduced from seed each year. It self-seeds readily where it is happy, which is why dill turns up in gardens and field margins far from where it was planted. Flora of China records the species as cultivated and adventive in several Chinese provinces rather than as a native.

## Seed and leaf are two different spices

> **Direct answer:** The leaf is green, delicate, and fleeting; the seed is warmer, rounder, and keeps far longer. They are not interchangeable in a recipe, and they behave differently in storage.

| | Leaf (dill weed) | Seed (莳萝子) |
|---|---|---|
| Colour | Bright green | Brown to grey-brown |
| Aroma | Fresh, grassy, sharp | Warm, sweet, anise-like |
| Volatile life | Days when fresh; months when dried | A year or more in good storage |
| Best use | Finishing a dish, cold sauces, salads | Pickling, bread, stews, spice blends |
| Heat tolerance | Low; aroma collapses with long cooking | Higher; releases slowly in liquid |
| Culinary role | Herb | Spice |

The distinction is not cosmetic. A recipe that calls for dill seed is asking for warmth and persistence; a recipe that calls for dill weed is asking for freshness and colour. Substituting one for the other changes the dish rather than merely adjusting it.

The same distinction matters for anyone assessing quality. Leaf quality is judged by colour and by the sharpness of the crushed leaf. Seed quality is judged by uniformity, colour, and the aroma released when the seed is rubbed between the fingers.

## Why "anise-like" is a register, not a species

> **Direct answer:** Several unrelated plants produce a similar sweet, liquorice-adjacent impression because they share related volatile compounds. The resemblance is perceptual, not botanical, and it is the reason names get confused.

Dill's characteristic note is usually attributed to carvone, with contributions from other volatiles such as dill ether. Fennel and anise lean more heavily on anethole. The two chemistries read as cousins to the nose: both are sweet and aromatic, both sit close to liquorice, and neither tastes of sugar.

This is the first place where a buyer can go wrong. "Anise-like" describes an impression shared by dill, fennel, anise, star anise, and several other materials. It does not name a plant, a species, or a specification. A supplier who describes a lot only as "anise-like" has told you what it smells like and nothing about what it is.

**Mechanism chain — why the seed smells sweeter than the leaf:**

- The seed is a compact package of oil-bearing tissue with a protective outer coat → volatiles are held in place → the aroma persists for months.
- The leaf is thin, watery, and exposed → volatiles escape and oxidise quickly → the green sharpness fades within days of picking.
- Crushing the seed ruptures the oil cells → release is immediate and concentrated → the sweetness reads stronger than the intact seed suggests.
- Heating the seed in liquid draws the volatiles into the surrounding medium → the spice flavours the dish rather than the air.

That chain explains a practical rule: buy seed whole, crush it when you need it, and treat the leaf as a perishable.

## The 小茴香 problem

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* lists 莳萝 under the alternate names 慈谋勒 and 小茴香. The second is also the ordinary name for fennel, so a classical label can point at either plant. The name is not a specification.

This is the heart of the page. Chinese culinary and materia-medica writing used 小茴香 for the small, anise-scented umbellifer seed in general, and both dill and fennel could answer to it depending on region and period. A reader who encounters 小茴香 in an old text cannot resolve the plant from the name alone.

The consequences are practical:

- **In a recipe**, the difference between dill seed and fennel seed changes the dish: dill is greener and more herbaceous, fennel sweeter and rounder.
- **In a purchase**, the same label can deliver either species, and a supplier may not know which they hold.
- **In a reference work**, a claim about "小茴香" must state which plant is meant, or it is not a claim about anything in particular.

The reliable key is the scientific name. *Anethum graveolens* is dill. *Foeniculum vulgare* is fennel. *Pimpinella anisum* is anise. Everything else is a label with a history.

## Telling the three apart at the bench

> **Direct answer:** Seed shape, colour, and the way the aroma develops separate dill, fennel, and anise more reliably than any label. The differences are small but consistent once you have handled all three.

| Feature | Dill (莳萝) | Fennel (小茴香) | Anise (茴芹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scientific name | *Anethum graveolens* | *Foeniculum vulgare* | *Pimpinella anisum* |
| Seed length | Roughly 3–5 mm | Generally larger, 4–10 mm | Small, 3–5 mm, pear-shaped |
| Seed shape | Straight, oval, flat-ish | Curved, plumper, with prominent ribs | Teardrop, ridged, slightly hairy |
| Colour | Brown to grey-brown | Greenish-brown to pale green | Grey-green to brown |
| Aroma when rubbed | Green, sharp, slightly warm | Sweet, round, liquorice-heavy | Sharp liquorice, almost medicinal |
| Taste | Herbaceous, faint anise | Sweet anise | Concentrated anise |
| Typical use | Pickles, bread, fish | Sausage, tea, braises | Liqueur, baking, medicine |

The table is a guide, not a laboratory key. Growing conditions, age, and processing move colour and size. What does not move is the direction of the aroma: dill keeps a green, herbaceous edge that fennel and anise lack.

A second check is the leaf, if any is present in the sample. Dill leaf is thread-fine and collapses quickly; fennel leaf is coarser and more feathery in a different pattern. That distinction is obvious in fresh material and useless in a bag of seed.

## Botany, range, and the Chinese record

> **Direct answer:** *Anethum graveolens* is a glabrous, strongly aromatic annual 30–75 cm tall, occasionally reaching about 100 cm. Its basal leaves are divided three or four times into thread-like segments; its fruit is brown, 3–5 mm long and 2–2.5 mm wide, with pale, narrowly winged lateral ribs.

Flora of China gives the flowering period as May to August and the fruiting period as July to September. It records the species as cultivated and adventive in Gansu, Guangdong, Guangxi, and Sichuan, with the native range in the Mediterranean region and Southwest Asia, and it notes that the fruits are used in traditional Chinese medicine as "shi luo" and as the spice dill.

Two details in that description are worth pausing on. The first is "glabrous" — the plant is hairless, which distinguishes it from several relatives. The second is the fruit measurement: a few millimetres, which is why dill seed is sold by weight and looks like a different order of object from fennel seed at a glance.

The Chinese record matters for a different reason. A plant listed as cultivated and adventive is a plant that has moved with people, not a wild endemic. Dill arrived in China as a garden and field herb, and its Chinese names reflect a long presence in culinary and medical writing rather than a deep wild history.

## What the chemistry explains

> **Direct answer:** The aroma is a mixture of volatile oils, dominated in dill by carvone and related compounds. The mixture changes with the part of the plant, the stage of growth, and the way the material is dried and stored.

A volatile oil is a mixture of small, mobile molecules that evaporate at room temperature. The nose detects them at very low concentrations, which is why a pinch of seed can scent a whole pot. Their proportion, not their total amount, determines the character: more carvone reads greener and sharper, more anethole reads sweeter and more liquorice-like.

This has three practical implications.

First, freshness is a chemical property, not a vague impression. Old seed has lost the light, mobile fraction and retains the heavier, duller notes. That is why stale dill smells like hay rather than like dill.

Second, grinding accelerates loss. A whole seed protects its oil behind a coat; a crushed seed exposes it to air. Grind close to use.

Third, heat drives volatiles off. A long simmer reduces the aromatic impact of the leaf almost to nothing, while the seed gives up its oil more slowly and therefore survives longer cooking.

**Mechanism chain — why storage destroys the note:**

- Whole seed → oil held inside intact cells → slow diffusion to the surface → aroma persists.
- Crushed or ground seed → cells ruptured → large surface exposed → oxidation and evaporation accelerate → the green note flattens within weeks.
- Warm, light, and humid storage → oxidation and moisture uptake → the oil converts to duller compounds → the seed smells of dust and hay.
- Sealed, dark, cool storage → low oxygen and low moisture → the profile stays closer to the fresh state → the spice keeps its character.

The chain is ordinary food chemistry, and it is the reason a spice rack near a stove is a bad place for dill.

## In the kitchen: four traditions

> **Direct answer:** Dill appears in several distinct culinary traditions, each using a different part for a different effect. Northern Europe uses the leaf with fish and the seed in pickles; India uses dill seed in spice blends; China records it as a seasoning herb; the modern kitchen uses both forms fresh.

The Nordic and Baltic kitchen is where dill is most visible. Fresh leaf goes into cold sauces, cured fish, potato dishes, and open sandwiches, usually added at the end so the volatile note survives. The seed goes into pickling brines, where its slower release suits a liquid that will be kept for weeks.

The Eastern European and Ashkenazi kitchen uses dill in pickles, soups, and salads, again with a preference for the fresh leaf and a supporting role for the seed. The flavour is associated with cucumber, cabbage, and fish rather than with sweetness.

The Indian kitchen uses dill seed, often under the name suva or sowa, in spice mixtures and with vegetables and pulses. The seed is usually tempered in hot fat, a technique that blooms the volatiles and carries them through the dish.

The Chinese record places 莳萝 among seasonings used to "harmonise the five flavours" — a phrase that describes blending and correcting rather than a specific recipe. The plant appears in culinary and materia-medica writing, not in the incense manuals.

None of these traditions treats dill as a fragrance material. That is the pattern worth noticing: a plant can be intensely aromatic and still belong entirely to the kitchen.

## Is dill an incense material?

> **Direct answer:** Not in the classical Chinese tradition, and not in any documented incense tradition this page can point to. Dill is a culinary aromatic — a 香药 — and the honest answer is that it has no established place in blended incense.

The question deserves a direct answer because search results blur it. Dill is aromatic, it is used in scenting, and it appears in perfumery as a top note in some modern compositions. None of those facts makes it a traditional incense ingredient.

A useful test is to ask what a material contributes to a burning blend:

| Requirement | What it means | Does dill meet it? |
|---|---|---|
| A stable aromatic note under heat | The material must smell acceptable when warmed or burned | Poorly — the leaf scorches, the seed turns dull |
| A structural role | Fuel, binder, or body in a stick or cone | No — it is a seasoning, used in pinches |
| A documented formula history | Appearance in classical recipes or manuals | No documented incense use |
| A fixative or modifier function | Alters how other materials behave | No established function |
| Cultural continuity | A recognised place in a tradition | Culinary, not cultic |

The table is deliberately functional. It asks what the material would do in a censer, not whether it smells nice in a jar. By that test, dill is a kitchen ingredient with an aromatic reputation and no incense role.

This does not make the page pointless. It makes the page honest, and it gives the encyclopedia a place to record a plant that would otherwise be either ignored or over-claimed.

## 香药 and 香材: two categories, not one

> **Direct answer:** 香药 (xiāngyào) names aromatic materials used to flavour, correct, or perfume food and medicine; 香材 (xiāngcái) names materials used to make incense. Dill belongs to the first category. The distinction is old, practical, and routinely ignored in English summaries.

The Chinese terms are useful because they separate two kinds of aromatic work that English lumps together under "spice" and "incense." A 香药 is judged by how it tastes and how it modifies a dish or a preparation. A 香材 is judged by how it burns, how it combines with other materials, and how the smoke reads.

The categories overlap at the edges. Some resins are used in both medicine and incense; some woods are chewed, carved, and burned. Dill sits firmly on the 香药 side, and the *Bencao Gangmu* record is a culinary and materia-medica record rather than an incense record.

That placement matters for a reader who arrives from a search engine expecting a "dill incense" article. The correct answer is that the plant is a seasoning with a long record, and that the record does not include a censer. Anything else would be invention.

## Reading the classical record

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* names 莳萝 with the aliases 慈谋勒 and 小茴香 and notes that people of the time used it mainly to season food. That is a culinary statement, and it is the strongest documentary fact about the plant's Chinese history.

The phrasing "今人多用和五味" is worth translating carefully. It means something like "people today mostly use it to harmonise the five flavours" — a statement about cooking, not about medicine or incense. The commentator is describing contemporary practice, which makes the line unusually concrete: it tells us how the plant was actually used, not how it was theorised.

The aliases are the complicating factor. 慈谋勒 is a transliteration-like name that appears in the same entry; 小茴香 is the ordinary name for fennel seed. The entry therefore preserves both a distinct name and a shared one, and a later reader cannot tell from the label alone which plant a given prescription intended.

For a modern reference entry, the honest treatment is to report the record, name the ambiguity, and refuse to resolve it by assertion. That is what this page does.

## Quality in the seed

> **Direct answer:** Good dill seed is clean, evenly sized, brown to grey-brown, and strongly aromatic when crushed. Poor seed is dusty, pale, full of stems, or smells of hay — signs of age, poor drying, or poor cleaning.

| Signal | Good | Poor | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | Even brown to grey-brown | Pale, washed out, or patchy | Immature seed, bleaching, or age |
| Size | Uniform | Mixed sizes, many small | Poor grading or mixed lots |
| Cleanliness | Free of stems and grit | Stem fragments, dust, stones | Weak cleaning after harvest |
| Aroma when crushed | Green, sweet, sharp | Hay, dust, flat | Oxidised oil; old stock |
| Moisture feel | Dry, free-running | Clumping, cool to the touch | Uptake of water in storage |
| Foreign seed | None visible | Rounder or larger seeds present | Admixture with fennel or other umbellifers |

The last row is the one that matters commercially. Dill and fennel seed are similar enough that admixture is easy and hard to see in a crowded sack. A careful buyer spreads a sample on a white tray and looks at shape: dill is straighter and flatter, fennel plumper and curved.

There is no formal incense grade for dill, because it is not an incense material. The grading that exists is culinary and, in some markets, pharmacopoeial. A supplier quoting an "incense grade" for dill is inventing a category.

## Adulteration and substitution

> **Direct answer:** Dill is cheap enough that outright adulteration is less common than mislabelling and admixture. The realistic risks are fennel mixed in, old stock sold as new, and leaf sold as seed or the reverse.

| Problem | How it shows up | How to check |
|---|---|---|
| Fennel admixture | Larger, curved, greener seeds in the lot | Spread and inspect; smell a crushed sample |
| Old stock | Flat aroma, pale colour, dusty feel | Rub and smell; compare with a fresh reference |
| Leaf sold as "dill" | Green, thread-like fragments | Visual inspection; leaf is obvious |
| Stem and chaff | Brown sticks and dust | Sift a sample through a coarse screen |
| Moisture pickup | Clumping, dull surface | Squeeze a handful; listen for crunch |
| Irradiation or fumigation odour | Flat, slightly chemical smell | Hard to detect; rely on supplier documentation |

Substitution is a different issue. In a kitchen, dill seed can be replaced by fennel seed in some pickles, with a sweeter result. In a Chinese-medicine context, the substitution is exactly the confusion the page is warning about, and it should not be made casually.

For an incense formulator, the only honest substitution advice is: do not substitute dill for an incense material. It is not one.

## Growing it, and why the garden dictates the flavour

> **Direct answer:** Dill grows fast, bolts in heat, and loses aroma quickly once cut. The garden conditions that produce tender leaf — cool weather, steady moisture, quick growth — are not the conditions that produce the best seed, which wants a full season to ripen.

Dill is direct-sown because it resents transplanting; the taproot does not recover from disturbance. Seed germinates over a range of temperatures, and successive sowings give a longer leaf harvest than one large sowing. Once the plant begins to flower, leaf production stops and the energy goes into the umbel.

That transition is the gardener's trade-off. Cutting the flower heads delays bolting and extends the leaf season; letting them ripen gives seed. A garden can do both if it is planted in succession, with some plants kept for leaf and others left for seed.

Harvest timing matters for aroma. Leaf is best cut in the cool of the morning, before the sun has driven off the volatile oil. Seed is harvested when the umbels turn brown but before they shatter; a paper bag over the head catches the ripe seed as it falls.

The plant self-seeds prolifically. A single umbel can produce a large number of seeds, and seedlings appear the following spring without help. That trait is convenient in a garden and inconvenient in a rotation, because dill can become a weed of its own bed.

## Drying, storage, and the fading note

> **Direct answer:** Drying preserves the shape of the aroma but not its brightness. The leaf loses most of its fresh character; the seed holds longer. Both should be stored sealed, dark, and cool, and both should be smelled before use.

Drying is a race between water loss and volatile loss. Heat speeds water removal but also drives off the aromatic molecules, which is why slow, cool drying preserves more character than a hot oven. The leaf is thin and dries quickly; the seed needs more time because the coat protects the interior.

Storage is the second race. Oxygen, light, and moisture all degrade the oil. An airtight jar in a dark cupboard is the ordinary answer; a spice rack above the stove is the wrong one. Whole seed keeps far better than ground, and the leaf keeps better than most cooks expect if it is dried fast and stored sealed.

Freezing the leaf is a reasonable compromise for colour, less so for texture. The volatile profile survives better than with heat drying, but the leaf collapses on thawing and is only useful in cooked dishes.

There is a useful test before any batch is used. Rub a few seeds between the fingers and smell. If the note is sharp and green, the material is alive. If it is dusty and sweet in a flat way, the oil has gone. No amount of extra quantity will bring back a note that is not there.

## The oil, and the modern fragrance trade

> **Direct answer:** Dill seed yields an essential oil by steam distillation, and that oil is used in flavouring and in perfumery as a fresh, green top note. The oil is a concentrate, not the same thing as the seed, and its behaviour in a blend is different.

Steam distillation passes water vapour through the material and carries volatile compounds into a condenser. The result is an oil that contains the light, mobile fraction of the plant and leaves behind the heavy, resinous, and water-soluble parts. That is why an essential oil can smell sharper and more one-dimensional than the material it came from.

In perfumery, dill oil is a top note — a material that appears early and fades. It is used in small amounts to give a green, aromatic lift to herbaceous or cucumber-like compositions. The dose is usually low because the note is powerful and can read as savoury rather than wearable at higher levels.

For an incense maker, the oil is a temptation and a trap. A few drops on a neutral base will produce a strong dill smell, but the result is not a traditional incense and the oil volatilises fast under heat. If the goal is an experimental aromatic piece rather than a classical formula, that is a legitimate experiment. It should not be presented as tradition.

## What "harmonising the five flavours" means

> **Direct answer:** 和五味 is a culinary idea, not a medical formula. It describes the work of balancing sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, and salty impressions in a dish, and an aromatic herb like dill earns its place by adjusting that balance rather than by contributing a course of its own.

The phrase appears in the *Bencao Gangmu* note on 莳萝: people of the time, it says, used the plant mainly to harmonise the five flavours. Read plainly, that is a statement about cooking. The plant was a seasoning — something added to a dish to round it, lift it, or correct it.

The concept behind the phrase is worth unpacking because it explains why a small quantity of an aromatic can matter out of proportion to its weight. A dish is not a sum of ingredients but a balance of impressions. A fatty braise needs an edge; a flat broth needs a lift; a heavy spice mix needs something green to keep it from closing in. Dill does those jobs in pinches, not handfuls.

That is also why the culinary record is thin on quantities. A harmonising ingredient is added to taste, in the way a cook adds salt or lemon. The absence of precise doses in the old texts is not an omission; it reflects the nature of the work.

The same logic explains why dill never became a 香材. A material used to adjust a dish is judged at the table, not in a censer. The classical writers were not ignoring an incense use; they were describing the use the plant actually had.

## Traditional use and modern evidence## Traditional use and modern evidence

> **Direct answer:** The culinary use is documented and uncontroversial. The medicinal claims attached to dill in various traditions are traditional belief, not established clinical fact, and this page makes no health claims for the plant.

Three layers are worth separating:

- **Documented culinary use.** Dill is a food seasoning with a long record in Europe, the Middle East, South Asia, and China. This is ordinary food history.
- **Traditional medicinal use.** Various traditions describe dill preparations for digestive complaints and similar purposes. These are traditional beliefs. They are not evidence of efficacy, and they are not instructions.
- **Modern research.** There is scientific literature on dill constituents and on the plant's essential oil. This page does not rely on it for health claims, and a reader should not treat a constituent study as proof that eating the plant does anything in particular.

The boundary matters because aromatic plants attract a great deal of speculative writing. A compound being present in an oil is not the same as a demonstrated effect in a person. The page stays on the safe side of that line: it describes the plant, its chemistry, and its culinary role, and it labels the rest as tradition.

## Safety, in the kitchen and at the bench

> **Direct answer:** Dill as a food herb is ordinary and widely eaten. The concentrated oil is a different matter: it is potent, it should not be applied neat to skin, and it should be kept away from children. Incense smoke, if dill is ever burned experimentally, carries the usual particulate risk.

Three separate exposures deserve separate sentences.

As food, dill is a seasoning used in normal culinary quantities. The page makes no medical claim about it and gives no dose.

As an essential oil, it is a concentrate. A drop of oil is not equivalent to a pinch of herb, and undiluted application to skin can irritate. Anyone using the oil should follow the supplier's safety documentation and keep it out of reach of children and pets.

As smoke, any burning aromatic material produces fine particles and combustion products. A dill experiment in a censer is not exempt from that rule. Ventilate, do not inhale concentrated smoke, and keep burning material away from anything flammable. The site's [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case.

## Buying checklist

> **Direct answer:** Buy by species and form, inspect the lot, and smell before you commit. For culinary use, freshness is the whole specification; for anything else, ask why dill is the right material at all.

1. **Species.** Confirm *Anethum graveolens*. If the label says 小茴香, ask which plant is in the bag.
2. **Form.** Whole seed, ground seed, dried leaf, fresh leaf, or oil. Each has a different shelf life and a different use.
3. **Colour and cleanliness.** Even brown seed, no stems, no dust, no clumping.
4. **Aroma.** Crush a few seeds. Green and sharp is good; hay and dust is old.
5. **Admixture.** Spread a sample and look for larger, curved fennel seed.
6. **Packaging and date.** A harvest or packing date is more useful than a best-before year.
7. **Purpose.** If the intended use is incense, stop and reconsider; dill is not an incense material.

## Dill against its neighbours

> **Direct answer:** Dill, fennel, anise, star anise, cumin, and coriander are all aromatic umbellifers or spice seeds with overlapping uses, and each has a distinct profile. The comparisons below are the ones a cook or buyer actually needs.

| | Dill | Fennel | Anise | Star anise | Cumin | Coriander |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant family | Apiaceae | Apiaceae | Apiaceae | Schisandraceae | Apiaceae | Apiaceae |
| Part used | Seed, leaf | Seed, leaf, bulb | Seed | Fruit | Seed | Seed, leaf |
| Dominant impression | Green, herbaceous anise | Sweet liquorice | Sharp liquorice | Warm liquorice-wood | Earthy, warm | Citrus, faintly floral |
| Culinary home | Pickles, fish, bread | Sausage, braise, tea | Liqueur, baking | Braises, five-spice | Chilli, cumin-heavy blends | Curry, pickles, gin |
| Resembles dill? | — | Closest, but sweeter | Sweeter and sharper | Very different | Very different | Very different |

The practical value of the table is negative: it shows how few of these materials are actually interchangeable. Dill and fennel are the closest pair, and even they diverge in the direction of sweetness. The rest are neighbours only in the sense that they share a shelf.

For the naming problem, the table is also a reminder that "anise-like" covers at least four of these six. A description that stops at "anise-like" has not distinguished anything.

## FAQ

**What is dill (莳萝)?** *Anethum graveolens*, an annual herb of the carrot family, grown for its thread-like leaves and ribbed seeds. It is a culinary plant with a fresh, green, faintly anise-like aroma, and it has a documented Chinese record as a seasoning.

**Does dill appear in the Bencao Gangmu?** Yes. The entry lists 莳萝 with the alternate names 慈谋勒 and 小茴香, and notes that people of the time used it mainly to season food. That culinary note is the strongest documentary statement about the plant in the Chinese record.

**Is dill the same as fennel?** No. They are different species in the same family. Dill is *Anethum graveolens*; fennel is *Foeniculum vulgare*. The confusion arises because the *Bencao Gangmu* applies the name 小茴香 to dill, and the same name is used for fennel.

**Is dill a classical incense ingredient?** No. It is a 香药 — an aromatic food seasoning — rather than a 香材, a material of blended incense. There is no documented classical incense use, and this page does not invent one.

**Why does dried dill taste like hay?** Because the volatile oil that gives the leaf its green note oxidises and evaporates during drying and storage. What remains is the heavier, duller fraction. Fast drying and airtight, dark storage slow the change but do not stop it.

**Can dill seed be used instead of fennel seed?** In some pickles and breads, yes, with a greener and less sweet result. In a Chinese-medicine context, no: the substitution is precisely the 小茴香 confusion, and the two plants are not equivalent.

**Is dill oil the same as the herb?** No. The oil is a steam-distilled concentrate of the volatile fraction, far stronger than the seed and different in balance. It is used in drops, not spoonfuls, and it is not a substitute for the herb in cooking.

## Summary

Dill (莳萝) is *Anethum graveolens*, a Mediterranean and Southwest Asian annual that became a kitchen herb across Europe and Asia. Its seed is warm and anise-like, its leaf green and fleeting, and both belong to the spice shelf rather than the censer. The Chinese record names it 莳萝, 慈谋勒, and — confusingly — 小茴香, the last of which is also the ordinary name for fennel; that overlap is the page's central lesson. Dill is catalogued here as a documented 香药 with a genuine naming problem, not as an incense material. The reliable key to the plant is the scientific name, and the reliable test of the spice is a crushed seed between the fingers.

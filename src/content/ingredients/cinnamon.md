**Direct answer:** In a Chinese incense workshop, "cinnamon" means cassia — the dried bark of *Cinnamomum cassia*, 桂皮 (guìpí). It is a thick, hard, reddish-brown bark with a hot sweet-spicy scent driven by cinnamaldehyde. It enters a blend as a seasoning rather than a foundation: a small proportion adds warmth and a food-adjacent top note, and a large one flattens everything around it.

The material is easy to buy and easy to buy wrong. The word "cinnamon" covers at least four different things — Chinese cassia bark, Ceylon cinnamon bark, the young twig of the same tree, and a distilled oil — and they behave differently in a stick. This page separates them, describes how the bark is harvested and graded, and sets out what a buyer can actually check.

## One English word, four materials

**Direct answer:** "Cinnamon" is a label that hides a species, a bark layer, a plant part, and a processed oil. Each one smells of cinnamon and none of them substitutes cleanly for another.

Start with the species. *Cinnamomum cassia* is the Chinese tree; *C. verum* is the Ceylon tree the Europeans called "true cinnamon." Both are laurels, both yield bark that curls as it dries, and both contain cinnamaldehyde. The resemblance stops at the thickness of the bark and the softness of the scent. Cassia is the louder material: thicker bark, higher oil load, a rougher edge.

Then the layer. A cassia trunk carries a corky outer bark and a finer inner bark, and the Chinese trade names them separately: 桂皮 for the coarse outer bark, 肉桂 for the finer inner one. English collapses both into "cinnamon." 

Then the part. 桂枝 is the young twig of the same tree, and it is not bark at all. It is lighter, greener, woodier, and it belongs to a different place in the materia medica. A recipe that says 桂枝 is not asking for 桂皮 with the bark left on.

| Name | What it is | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| 桂皮 (guìpí) | Coarse outer bark of *C. cassia* | Cooking, everyday incense spice |
| 肉桂 (ròuguì) | Fine inner bark of *C. cassia* | Higher-grade spice and formula work |
| 桂枝 (guìzhī) | Young twig of *C. cassia* | A separate materia-medica part |
| *C. verum* | Ceylon cinnamon bark | European and American kitchens |
| Cassia oil | Distillate of bark or leaf | Flavour and fragrance, not incense powder |

The fourth material is the oil. Cassia oil is a distillate, not a ground bark, and it behaves like an aroma concentrate: a few drops on a stick will outperform a spoonful of powder in intensity and will also burn differently. Treating oil and powder as interchangeable units is a common source of over-dosed formulas.

## The tree and the bark

**Direct answer:** *Cinnamomum cassia* is an evergreen laurel native to southern China and northern Vietnam, grown for its bark. The aromatic tissue is the inner bark, where oil cells sit among the phloem fibres; the corky outer layer protects it and is usually scraped away before drying.

A laurel bark is a layered structure. On the outside sits the cork, a dead protective layer that is rough, grey-brown, and more or less tasteless. Beneath it lies the cortex and then the phloem, the living tissue that carries sap, and it is here that the oil cells are concentrated. A cassia tree puts several years of growth into that phloem before the bark is thick enough to strip in commercial lengths.

The harvest follows the growth of the tree. Branches and stems are cut, the bark is scored lengthwise and levered off in slabs, and the outer cork is scraped from the slab while it is still green and flexible. What remains is a thick, damp sheet of inner bark with a warm, sweet smell that is much stronger than the dried spice suggests.

Drying does the rest. The slab curls as the outer face loses water faster than the inner, and the curl is the origin of the quill. A thick slab curls into a coarse, open roll; a thinner slab curls tighter. The familiar cinnamon stick on a supermarket shelf is a dried cross-section of that process, and the tightness of the roll is a rough record of how thick the bark was and how it was handled.

The species is recorded in [*Cinnamomum cassia* in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200008698) and in the GBIF record for [*Cinnamomum aromaticum*](https://www.gbif.org/species/3033982), which treats the two names as synonyms. The nomenclatural tangle is worth knowing because a supplier may use either name on a certificate of analysis, and neither is wrong.

## Aroma: heat as a design element

**Direct answer:** Cassia smells sweet and hot at once, with a pungent top and a woody-balsamic tail. The sweetness arrives first because cinnamaldehyde is volatile; the heat sits behind it, and it is the part that survives a burn.

Smell a fresh quill and the sequence is quick. There is a sweet, almost fruity opening that reads like the spice jar, then a sharp, slightly bitter pungency at the back of the nose, then a dry woody note that lingers on the fingers. Break the quill and the interior is stronger than the surface, because the oil cells are inside and the outer cork has been scraped away.

Cinnamaldehyde dominates the volatile fraction, and it is the compound most people recognise as "cinnamon." It is also the reason cassia behaves the way it does in a blend: the molecule is small and volatile, so it lifts early and loudly.

> **Mechanism:** ground cassia → exposed oil cells → cinnamaldehyde evaporates ahead of the heavier wood and resin notes → the blend opens with spice → the spice fades before the base develops → the middle of the burn feels hollow unless a resin fills it.

That early peak is useful. A warm-spice note at the top of a stick gives the first minute a shape, and it covers the transition from lighting to steady burn, which is often the least pleasant part of an incense experience. The catch is that the same peak can dominate a delicate floral formula and make it smell like a kitchen.

The tail is where cassia differs from its relatives. Clove carries eugenol, which reads cooler and more dental; cassia carries cinnamaldehyde, which reads sweeter and more bakery. Put the two side by side and the difference is obvious: clove is a sharp high note, cassia is a rounded warm one. That is why the two are often paired rather than substituted, and why a five-spice blend uses both.

## Cassia or Ceylon: the identification table

**Direct answer:** Cassia bark is thick, hard, single-layered, and reddish-brown; Ceylon quills are thin, brittle, multi-layered, and pale tan. The two are different species with different oil loads and different prices, and a label that says only "cinnamon" has not chosen between them.

| Feature | Chinese cassia (*C. cassia*) | Ceylon cinnamon (*C. verum*) |
|---|---|---|
| Quill wall | Thick, one main layer | Thin, several layers rolled together |
| Surface | Rough, reddish-brown, cork often scraped | Smooth, pale tan |
| Break | Hard, granular, splintery | Brittle, feathery, easy to snap |
| Aroma | Strong, sweet, pungent, slightly bitter | Milder, finer, more floral |
| Oil load | High | Lower |
| Price | Low | High |
| Typical market | Chinese cooking, incense, US spice aisle | European and specialty baking |

The physical test is simple and needs no laboratory. Take a quill and try to snap it. Ceylon bark breaks with a light, papery crack and the edges look like layers of pastry. Cassia resists, then breaks with a woody snap, and the broken face is solid rather than laminated. Look at the end of the roll: a Ceylon quill shows concentric rings of thin bark; a cassia quill is a single thick wall.

The aroma test follows. Scratch the inner surface with a fingernail and smell. Cassia gives a blunt, sweet-pungent hit that fills the nose quickly; Ceylon gives a softer, more complex smell with a floral edge and less heat. Neither is better in the abstract — they are different materials for different jobs — but a Chinese incense formula that specifies 桂皮 is not asking for the Ceylon type.

Powder removes every one of those cues. Once the bark is milled, thickness, layering, and break are gone, and a buyer has only colour and smell to work with. That is the strongest argument for buying whole bark when the species matters and milling it in-house.

## Where it comes from, and why supply is steady

**Direct answer:** Cassia is native to southern China and northern Vietnam and is cultivated across the region, with Guangxi and Guangdong as the long-standing producing areas. Because it is a farmed tree rather than a wild-harvested aromatic, supply is stable and the material is inexpensive relative to resins and woods.

The tree grows on hillsides and in village plots across the warm, humid south. It is planted, coppiced, and harvested on a cycle measured in years rather than seasons, and the bark is a by-product of a tree that also yields leaf and twig material. That structure makes the trade resilient: a bad year in one district is covered by another, and the price does not swing the way agarwood or sandalwood does.

The commercial consequence is worth stating plainly. Cassia is not a luxury material, and a buyer should not pay luxury prices for it. The premium, where one exists, belongs to the inner bark (肉桂), to carefully scraped and evenly dried quills, and to lots with a strong, clean smell. Paying more for a famous district name without those qualities is paying for a story.

There is also a practical link between origin and use. A bark that grew fast and thick behaves differently from a slow-grown, thinner bark: the fast one is more fibrous and holds more water, the slow one is denser and smells more concentrated. The difference is not visible in a photograph and rarely appears on an invoice, which is why a sample matters more than a country of origin.

## A spice with a long paper trail

**Direct answer:** Cassia has moved along trade routes for a very long time, and most of what Europeans called cinnamon for centuries was cassia from southern China. The confusion is old, commercial, and only partly resolved by modern taxonomy.

The Chinese side of the story is domestic and ordinary. Cassia grows in the warm south, and the bark has been a cooking spice and a medicine for as long as there are records of Chinese kitchen practice. It travelled north as a dried good, sold by the catty in spice shops and used in braises, pickles, and the pharmacy. There was no mystery about it: it was a farmed bark with a strong smell, cheap enough for daily use.

The maritime trade added distance and confusion. Cassia and other spices moved through the southern ports and along the routes that connected South China to Southeast Asia, India, the Gulf, and eventually the Mediterranean. In the markets of the west, the bark arrived as "cinnamon," a name that carried a premium without carrying a species. Cassia was the cheaper and stronger material, and it is reasonable to assume that a good deal of the cinnamon sold in medieval Europe was cassia.

Ceylon changed the picture. The island's cinnamon — *C. verum* — was finer, thinner, and easier to sell at a high price to a European market that had learned to value delicacy. The Portuguese, then the Dutch, then the British organised the trade around it, and "true cinnamon" became a commercial category as much as a botanical one. Cassia did not disappear; it became the strong, cheap alternative, and in North America it became the default.

The botanical naming settled later than the trade. *Cinnamomum verum* and *C. cassia* are now clearly separate species, and the differences in bark, oil, and coumarin are documented. What the trade has not settled is the labelling habit: a jar that says "cinnamon" may contain either species, and in most of the world it contains cassia.

For a reader of an incense page, the history explains a small oddity. Chinese recipes assume cassia because cassia is what the region grows. European pastry recipes assume a milder bark because the European trade spent centuries selling Ceylon as the good stuff. The two traditions are not arguing about quality; they are using different plants and calling them the same word.

## Two old words: cinnamon and cassia

**Direct answer:** English inherited two separate words from the ancient spice trade — cinnamon and cassia — and then blurred them in everyday use. The etymology explains why the confusion is so durable: the words named trade goods before they named species.

The Greek texts that European botany inherited used kinnamōmon and kasia for two barks that arrived from the east. The first is the ancestor of "cinnamon," the second of "cassia." Both words were borrowed into Latin, then into the European vernaculars, and both travelled with the spice rather than with the plant. A medieval merchant selling kasia and a modern supplier selling cassia are using a word with a very long commercial life.

The further etymology is uncertain, and it is worth saying so. Kinnamōmon is usually derived from a Semitic source, with the Hebrew qinnāmōn as a commonly cited relative, but the trail is not clean. Kasia has been connected to several Near Eastern words for bark or peel. What is clear is that the Greeks were not describing Chinese botany; they were naming an imported product whose origin was vague to them.

Arabic and Persian added their own layer. The Arabic phrase for cinnamon bark, dar sini, means something close to "Chinese tree," which is a rare case of a name that records the origin honestly. Persian darchin carries the same idea in a shorter form. Those names travelled with the trade into India and Central Asia, and they sit behind the modern dalchini and darchin in the table above.

The lesson for a buyer is not linguistic but practical. When a word has named a commodity for two thousand years, it accumulates commercial meanings that no botanical revision can remove. "Cinnamon" will keep meaning cassia in most of the world's markets, and no amount of taxonomy will change the shelf label. The species name is the only part of the label that is not carrying two millennia of baggage.

## Harvest, drying, and the shape of a quill

**Direct answer:** Bark is stripped from cut stems, scraped of cork, and dried until it curls into quills or contracts into flat strips. The thickness of the slab, the speed of drying, and the amount of cork removed decide the shape and the grade.

The sequence is short but unforgiving. Cutting is done in the cool season, when the sap is low and the bark lifts cleanly. Stems are cut into lengths, scored along their axis, and the bark worked free with a knife or wedge; a clean lift gives a broad slab, a careless one gives torn edges.

Scraping comes next. The outer cork is removed with a curved knife, and the skill lies in taking the rough layer without cutting into the oil-bearing phloem underneath. A badly scraped slab carries cork into the dried product, which shows up as a dull, grey-brown surface and a flatter smell.

> **Mechanism:** thick slab → outer face dries and contracts first → the inner face is still damp and plastic → the slab curls around the dry face → a tight quill forms only if the thickness and the drying rate are matched; a hot, fast dry sets a brittle curl that cracks along the roll.

Drying is usually done in the sun or in a shaded airy shed. Sun gives a faster, harder curl and a brighter colour; shade gives a slower, more even dry and, in the experience of the trade, a rounder smell. A lot dried too fast can be damp at the core of the quill even when the outside feels hard, and that moisture shows up later as mould on the inner surface.

The dried bark is sorted by length, thickness, and completeness. Long, even, well-scraped quills go to the spice trade as a premium presentation; broken pieces and short lengths go to grinding. Neither is a different material. The difference is how much of the bark's structure survived the process.

## 桂皮 and 肉桂: what the grades mean

**Direct answer:** 桂皮 is the coarse outer bark and 肉桂 the finer inner bark of the same tree; the inner bark is denser, more aromatic, and more expensive. The distinction is a grade and a layer, not a species difference.

| | 桂皮 (guìpí) | 肉桂 (ròuguì) |
|---|---|---|
| Layer | Outer bark, cork removed | Inner bark, closer to the cambium |
| Thickness | Thick and coarse | Thinner, denser |
| Aroma | Strong, blunt, hot | Richer, rounder, more persistent |
| Price | Low | High |
| Kitchen use | Braises, pickles, five-spice | Finer spice work, some medicinal use |
| Incense use | The default warm-spice accent | A more refined accent at higher cost |

The reason the inner bark is better is structural. The phloem nearest the cambium is younger tissue with a higher density of oil cells and thinner fibre walls, so a gram of it carries more aromatic oil and less woody bulk. The outer bark has more cork and more fibre, which is why it reads blunt and why it is ground rather than chewed.

For incense, the practical question is whether the extra cost of 肉桂 buys anything in the finished stick. Sometimes it does: a fine, sweet, long-lasting warm note that 桂皮 cannot produce at the same dose. Often it does not: the difference disappears under a heavy resin or a strong floral. A formulator should test the substitution at the intended dose rather than assume that the more expensive layer is always the better one.

## Forms and how they behave

**Direct answer:** Quills are for whole-bark use and identification, strips for blending and cooking, powder for sticks cones, and oil as a separate concentrate. Each form releases aroma at a different rate and each has its own failure mode.

| Form | On heat | Best use | Failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole quill | Slow, uneven, sweet at the edges | Identification, long infusions, display | Damp core; scorched surface |
| Broken strips | Steady release, easy to dose | Blending, cooking, small batches | Inconsistent piece size |
| Ground powder | Fast, loud, short | Sticks and cones | Clumping, over-dosing, stale |
| Distilled oil | Very fast, very strong | Flavour, fragrance, dipping | Volatilises in storage; stains |

Powder is where most incense work happens, and it is the form with the least margin for error. A cassia powder is dense and heavy compared with a floral powder, so it sinks in a mix and packs tightly. That density is useful in a stick because it helps the material hold shape, and it is dangerous in a formula because a small volume carries a large aromatic load.

Particle size changes the burn. A fine powder exposes more oil to the heat and releases the spice in a sharp burst; a coarse grind releases it more slowly and leaves visible flecks. For a stick that should smell warm from the first moment, fine is the usual choice. For a stick that should unfold, a mix of coarse and fine gives the spice somewhere to hide.

## Why cassia is in Chinese incense

**Direct answer:** Cassia supplies warmth. In a classical compound it sits among the assistants, adding a sweet-pungent top note that makes a resinous or woody blend feel fuller and more familiar. It is rarely the chief ingredient and it should not be.

The old scheme of 君臣佐使 — chief, minister, assistant, guide — is a way of describing hierarchy in a compound. Cassia is a natural assistant: it does not set the character of a blend, but it changes how the character is perceived. A sandalwood formula without spice can feel smooth to the point of dullness; a small amount of cassia gives it an edge and a beginning.

The culinary overlap explains half its usefulness. Cassia is one of the five spices in 五香粉 (five-spice powder), alongside star anise, clove, Sichuan pepper, and fennel. A nose that has eaten Chinese braised food recognises the note instantly, which is why cassia in incense reads as "familiar" rather than "perfumed." That familiarity is an asset in a blend meant to evoke a kitchen or a temple, and a liability in one meant to smell abstract.

> **Mechanism:** cassia at a low dose → cinnamaldehyde lifts the opening → the resin underneath holds the middle → the spice reads as warmth rather than food → the blend gains depth without gaining weight.

At a high dose the same mechanism inverts. The aldehyde dominates the first minute, the bitter woody tail arrives before the base has developed, and the stick smells like a spice rack on fire. The threshold is not a fixed percentage; it depends on the resin, the wood, and how much sweetness the rest of the formula already carries.

## Cassia in the kitchen

**Direct answer:** Cassia is a cooking spice before it is an incense material, and the kitchen explains its smell better than any perfumery description. It goes into braises, pickles, spice blends, and sweet baked goods, always in small quantity and always with something else carrying the dish.

The Chinese use is the one that matters for context. 桂皮 is a standard aromatic in red-braised pork and beef, in soy-based braises, in pickled vegetables, and in the spice bag that goes into a pot of soup. It is added whole and left to soften, or broken into short pieces so that more cut surface meets the liquid. The bark gives up its sweetness slowly in water, which is why a braise tastes of cassia at the end rather than at the start.

五香粉, five-spice powder, is the blend that fixes cassia in the Chinese palate. The five are star anise, clove, Sichuan pepper, fennel, and cassia, and the proportions vary by region and by cook. Cassia's role in the mix is the warm base note: star anise supplies the aniseed top, clove the sharp high note, Sichuan pepper the citrus-tingling edge, fennel the sweet liquorice middle. Take the cassia out and the blend goes hollow in the middle.

Western baking uses the same bark differently. Ground cassia goes into cinnamon rolls, apple cakes, and spice biscuits, where the heat of the oven drives the aldehyde off and the sugar carries the rest. The result is sweeter and heavier than the Chinese braise, and it is the reason many European and American eaters think of cinnamon as a dessert spice. It is the same material in a different culinary frame.

The kitchen also supplies a practical test. A cook who tastes a piece of bark in a braise can tell whether it is cassia or Ceylon within a minute: cassia turns the mouth dry and hot, Ceylon stays softer and more floral. That test is rougher than a laboratory analysis and considerably more available.

For incense, the culinary frame is a warning as much as a guide. A stick that smells like a braise is a stick that has crossed the line from warm to edible, and very few formulas want that. The useful transfer is the technique, not the flavour: add cassia early and in small amount, let it sit under the other materials, and judge the result after the first minute rather than at the moment of lighting.

## Cassia in other kitchens

**Direct answer:** Cassia travels well, and most of the world's warm-spice cooking has a place for it. Indian masalas, Middle Eastern baharat, European baking, Mexican mole, and Vietnamese broth all use a thick cinnamon bark, and most of them are using cassia rather than Ceylon.

In India, cassia is one of the whole spices that go into a garam masala, along with cardamom, clove, black pepper, cumin, and coriander. The blend varies by household and region, and the cinnamon element may be cassia bark, cassia leaf, or true cinnamon depending on price and habit. It is usually toasted before grinding, which drives the aldehyde off and leaves a rounder, nuttier note.

In the Middle East, baharat and similar blends use cassia or cinnamon with nutmeg, cardamom, black pepper, and sometimes dried lime. The spice goes into rice, meat, and stews, and the effect is a warm background rather than a distinct cinnamon flavour. The bark is often cooked whole in the liquid and removed before serving.

In Europe, cassia is the baker's spice. It goes into sweet doughs, into mulled wine and cider, into gingerbread and speculaas, and into the sugar coatings on pastries. The heat of the oven drives the volatile fraction into the dough, and the sugar carries the rest. European bakers who insist on Ceylon are making a deliberate choice for a softer, more floral result; the default in most commercial baking is cassia.

In Mexico, canela — usually a soft, thick cassia — goes into mole, into hot chocolate, and into spiced coffee. The Mexican preference is for a bark that is easy to break by hand and mild enough to use in quantity, which is a different selection from the hard, thick quills of the Chinese trade.

In Vietnam, cassia is part of the spice bag that goes into a long-simmered beef broth. The bark is toasted first, along with star anise and clove, and the broth is strained before serving. The result is a savoury use of a spice that Europeans mostly meet in sugar.

The pattern across all five is the same: cassia is used in a blend, in a small quantity, with other warm spices, and it is almost never the only aromatic. That is the same role it plays in incense, and the same reason it is easy to over-dose. A kitchen that uses one stick in a pot for four people is making the point that a little goes a long way.

The chemistry behind the cooking is worth knowing. Cinnamaldehyde is fat-soluble, so the spice blooms in oil, butter, or fat before it blooms in water. That is why toasting the bark in fat before adding liquid gives a fuller flavour, and why a stick dropped into a water-based stew tastes thinner. The same principle applies when a cassia tincture or oil is used in a fragrance: the carrier decides how much of the spice reaches the nose.

## What it is called elsewhere

**Direct answer:** Almost every cuisine that uses the bark has its own word for it, and the words usually mean cassia even when an English dictionary translates them as "cinnamon." The table is a reminder that the English ambiguity is not universal — but the species ambiguity often is.

| Place | Local name | Usual form | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| China | 桂皮 (guìpí) | thick quills, broken strips | braise, five-spice |
| India | dalchini | whole bark, ground | garam masala, rice |
| Iran | darchin | whole bark | khoresh, rice, sweets |
| Turkey | tarçın | ground, whole | baking, coffee |
| Mexico | canela | soft thick bark | chocolate, mole |
| Vietnam | quế | whole bark | pho broth |
| Sri Lanka | kurundu | thin quills | curry, baking |
| Germany | Zimt | ground | cakes, mulled wine |

Two things follow from the table. The first is that the local word rarely distinguishes species: dalchini, darchin, tarçın, canela, and quế all translate as "cinnamon" and all usually denote a cassia-type bark. The second is that the form follows the cooking. Cuisines that simmer bark in liquid use whole quills or thick strips; cuisines that bake use ground spice. The Chinese braise and the German cake are the same plant in different physical states.

The names also carry a price signal. In markets where Ceylon cinnamon is the premium product, the local word may be reserved for it and the cassia sold under a qualifier. In markets where cassia is the default, the word is unmarked and the Ceylon type is the special-order item. A buyer working across languages should ask for the species, not the translation.

## The warm-spice family, and where cassia sits

**Direct answer:** Cassia belongs to a small group of warm spices that Chinese and European cooking use in similar ways: clove, star anise, fennel, Sichuan pepper, nutmeg, cardamom, and ginger. Each has a different kind of heat, and comparing them shows what cassia specifically contributes.

The family is defined by aromatic compounds that read as warm rather than cool. Clove carries eugenol, which is dental and numbing. Star anise carries anethole, which is sweet and liquorice-like. Fennel carries the same compound in a softer form. Sichuan pepper carries sanshool, which produces the tingling numbness that is not heat at all. Nutmeg and cardamom carry terpenes that read as woody and resinous. Ginger carries gingerols, which produce a pungent, lingering bite. Cassia carries cinnamaldehyde, which is sweet, pungent, and bakery-like.

| Spice | Main character | Heat type | Usual role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cassia | Sweet, pungent, woody | Warm and rounding | Base note in blends |
| Clove | Sharp, dental, medicinal | High and numbing | Accent, small dose |
| Star anise | Liquorice, sweet | Warm and sweet | Top note in five-spice |
| Fennel | Soft anise, green | Mild and sweet | Background sweetness |
| Sichuan pepper | Citrus, tingling | Numbing, not hot | Aromatic edge |
| Nutmeg | Woody, resinous | Warm and dry | Depth in sweet spice |
| Cardamom | Camphoraceous, floral | Cool-warm | Lift in sweet and savoury |
| Ginger | Pungent, fresh | Sharp and lingering | Fresh heat |

Read the table as a set of tools rather than a ranking. Cassia's specific contribution is roundness: it fills the middle of a warm blend without adding a sharp edge. Clove adds the edge, star anise the sweetness, Sichuan pepper the tingle. A blend that uses cassia alone is warm but flat; a blend that uses clove alone is sharp and thin. The combination is what makes five-spice work.

The same logic applies in incense. A warm-spice accord built only on cassia reads like a bakery; built only on clove it reads like a dentist's office. Cassia with a little clove and a citrus peel is the classic Chinese arrangement, and it is classic because the three occupy different parts of the nose at different times.

There is a further distinction worth making between warm spices and resinous warm materials. Benzoin, labdanum, and frankincense also read warm, but they are heavy and sweet in a balsamic way, and they hold a blend together. Cassia is volatile and does not hold anything. It sits on top of the resins rather than beside them, which is why a formula needs both: the resin for structure, the spice for the opening.

## Bark oil, leaf oil, and the distillation divide

**Direct answer:** Cassia yields at least two commercial oils — bark oil, dominated by cinnamaldehyde, and leaf oil, rich in eugenol — and they are different products with different uses and prices. Neither is the same thing as ground bark, and treating them as interchangeable is a formulation error.

Bark oil is the one that smells like the spice. Steam distillation of the dried bark gives a heavy, sweet, pungent oil with cinnamaldehyde as its main component, and it is used in flavour work and in fragrance where a warm spicy note is wanted. It is potent: a small amount carries a long way, and an over-dosed fragrance turns harsh and cloying.

Leaf oil is the cheaper product and the different one. Distilled from the leaves and twigs rather than the bark, it is dominated by eugenol — the compound that gives clove its dental, numbing character — with far less cinnamaldehyde. The smell is warmer, more medicinal, and closer to clove than to cinnamon. It is used in cheaper fragrance applications and in some industrial flavour work, and it is not a substitute for bark oil in a fine composition.

The distinction matters to an incense maker for two reasons. The first is authenticity: a product described as "cassia oil" may be leaf oil, bark oil, or a blend, and the label rarely says. The second is behaviour: leaf oil's eugenol reads cool and dental, while bark oil's aldehyde reads sweet and hot. A formula built around one will not smell right with the other.

There is a third product worth knowing. Cassia buds — the unripe fruits of the tree — are sold as a spice in some markets, with a smell between bark and clove. They are a niche item and rarely reach incense, but they appear in spice catalogues and can confuse a buyer who is comparing "cassia" products by name alone.

For a workshop, the practical position is conservative. Use bark powder when the formula calls for the spice, and use a distilled oil only when the formula is built for it and the oil's identity is documented. Do not assume that a drop of oil and a spoon of powder are the same material at different strengths. They are different fractions of the plant, and the nose can tell.

## Blending: a decision table

**Direct answer:** Use cassia when a blend needs warmth, a familiar top note, or a bridge between wood and spice. Keep it out of delicate floral formulas and out of any blend where the base is already sweet and heavy.

| Aim | Dose posture | Partners | Failure if overdone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm a sandalwood base | Small, early | Sandalwood, a little clove | Spice sits on top like a garnish |
| Build a five-spice theme | Moderate, with star anise and clove | Anise, clove, citrus peel | Reads as food, not incense |
| Add depth to a resin blend | Small, under the resin | Benzoin, frankincense | Pungency curdles the sweetness |
| Bridge wood and floral | Very small | Rose, orange blossom | Floral is buried |
| Make a winter-style stick | Moderate, with citrus | Orange peel, clove | Sharp and dry |

Two rules of thumb are worth carrying. The first is that cassia competes with florals and cooperates with resins. The second is that cassia is louder in a stick than in the jar: the heat drives the aldehyde off fast, so a formula that smells balanced as powder can smell spice-heavy when lit. Test on heat, not on the bench.

## Judging a lot

**Direct answer:** Judge cassia by species, layer, thickness, colour, smell, and moisture. Whole bark gives you more to check than powder, and a clean break and a sweet interior are worth more than a famous region name.

| Look at | Accept | Reject |
|---|---|---|
| Species | Named *C. cassia* or *C. verum* | "Cinnamon" with no species |
| Layer | Consistent 桂皮 or 肉桂 | Mixed layers sold as one grade |
| Surface | Evenly scraped, reddish-brown | Grey cork patches, green mould |
| Break | Hard, granular, dry | Soft, fibrous, damp |
| Smell | Sweet, pungent, clean | Musty, sour, flat |
| Moisture | Dry to the touch, no clumping when milled | Cakes in the hand |

Moisture deserves its own note. Cassia is hygroscopic, and a quill that has absorbed water during shipping will smell dull and will mould in a sealed jar. The test is crude but effective: break a quill and look at the inner face. A dry break is pale and dusty; a damp one is darker and slightly flexible at the core.

Colour is a weaker signal than most buyers assume. A bright reddish-brown surface can be the result of scraping and fast drying rather than quality, and a dull surface can simply be a different grade of bark. The nose and the break tell you more.

## Coumarin: the fact and the limit of the fact

**Direct answer:** Cassia bark contains coumarin, at higher levels than Ceylon cinnamon. That matters for people who eat large amounts of the spice; it is a compositional fact about the bark, not a measured statement about incense smoke.

Coumarin is a naturally occurring compound in several plants, and cassia's level is the reason some food authorities publish intake guidance for cinnamon. The guidance is about eating, where the dose can be grams per day and the exposure is repeated. A stick that contains a small fraction of cassia and is burned in a room is a different exposure route, and it would be wrong to translate a dietary limit into a claim about smoke.

It would be equally wrong to use that distinction as reassurance. Burning anything produces particulate matter, and the safe habit is ventilation rather than a calculation. The coumarin discussion belongs to the kitchen; the smoke discussion belongs to the room.

## Substitution and mislabelling

**Direct answer:** The realistic risk with cassia is not counterfeiting but substitution: Ceylon sold as cassia, cassia sold as Ceylon, twig sold as bark, and powder sold without a species. Whole bark is auditable; powder is not.

Cassia is cheap, so there is little profit in faking it. There is profit in the other direction: passing a cheaper cassia off as Ceylon "true cinnamon," which commands a higher price in Western markets. The physical cues in the table above are the defence, and they only work on whole bark.

The second risk is a plain category error. A supplier who sells 桂枝 as "cinnamon bark" has sold a different plant part, and a buyer who grinds it into a formula will get a lighter, greener, woodier note than expected. The third is the powder problem: once milled, species and layer are invisible, and the label is the only evidence.

For a buyer, the practical protocol is short. Buy whole bark when the species matters. Ask for the species name in writing. Keep a reference sample of each lot. Mill in-house if the formula is sensitive to freshness. The general screening logic for substitutions is set out in the [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/) guide and in the site's [scent guide](/blog/scent-guide/).

## Cassia in Chinese medicine

**Direct answer:** Chinese materia medica treats 肉桂 and 桂枝 as two separate drugs with different actions, and the distinction is one of the clearest examples of how a single tree can yield several medicines. Both are described in traditional language; neither description is a modern clinical finding.

肉桂, the inner bark, is classed as a warming drug that enters the kidney, spleen, and heart channels. Traditional texts describe it as warming the interior, assisting the body's fire, and dispersing cold — language that belongs to a system of correspondences rather than to pharmacology. It appears in formulas for cold patterns, in combinations with other warming materials, and in the pharmacy as a powdered or sliced bark.

桂枝, the young twig, is classed differently. It is described as releasing the exterior and harmonising the constructive and defensive qi, and it is the named herb in 桂枝汤 (Guizhi Tang), one of the classical formulas of the Shanghan Lun tradition. The same tree, a different part, a different action, and a different place in the formula hierarchy.

That difference is the practical reason a supplier must specify the part. A buyer who orders "cinnamon" for a formula that calls for 桂枝 has bought the wrong tissue, and no amount of grinding will convert one into the other. The twig is lighter, woodier, and less sweet; the bark is denser and hotter.

The traditional claims should be read as traditional. Chinese medicine describes warming the interior and dispersing cold in a vocabulary that predates modern physiology, and it would be a mistake to translate those phrases into claims about circulation, blood pressure, or infection. What can be said is that the bark and the twig have been used in this system for a long time, that they are recorded separately, and that the separation is meaningful to the practitioners who use them.

For incense, the medical record is context rather than instruction. It explains why the twig exists as a commercial category and why a Chinese customer may ask for 桂枝 by name. It does not tell you how either part burns.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Incense smoke contains particulate matter, so burn in a ventilated space and do not sit in the smoke. Cassia bark also contains coumarin, which is a dietary consideration rather than a reason to treat burning as risk-free.

Ventilation is the first rule and it does not change with the material. A small room, a closed window, and a long burn is a bad combination regardless of whether the stick is sandalwood or cassia. Anyone with asthma or another respiratory condition should be more careful, not less.

The coumarin note is about eating, not burning. It is included because cassia is a food spice and a reader may meet the compound in a nutrition article; it is not a licence to ignore smoke. See the [safety guide](/safety/) for the general guidance that applies across the site.

## Storage

**Direct answer:** Keep bark dry, dark, and sealed; keep powder drier and use it faster. Cassia is hygroscopic and its aroma migrates into packaging, so glass or a lined container beats paper.

Whole quills hold their aroma for a long time because the oil is locked inside intact cells. The enemies are moisture, which softens the bark and invites mould, and light, which fades the surface and accelerates the breakdown of the volatile fraction. A cool cupboard and a sealed jar are enough for a working stock.

Powder is a different clock. Milling opens the oil cells, and the sweet top note begins to leave immediately. A powder that smells vivid on the day it is ground can read flat after a couple of months, even in a good container. Buy it in amounts that will be used, and keep a quill as the reference for what the powder should smell like.

Odour transfer goes both ways. Cassia will scent a jar, a shelf, and anything stored beside it, and it will pick up the smell of a neighbouring resin. Label the container and keep it away from delicate floral stocks.

## FAQ

**Is Chinese cinnamon the same as Ceylon cinnamon?** No. They are different species: *C. cassia* and *C. verum*. Cassia bark is thicker, harder, and stronger, with a higher oil load; Ceylon bark is thin, layered, brittle, and milder. Both are sold as cinnamon, which is why the species name matters more than the word on the front of the packet.

**What is the difference between 桂皮 and 肉桂?** Layer and grade, not species. 桂皮 is the coarse outer bark, cheap and strong; 肉桂 is the finer inner bark, denser and more aromatic. Both come from *C. cassia*.

**Does cassia contain coumarin?** Yes, at higher levels than Ceylon cinnamon. That is a dietary consideration for people who eat a lot of the spice. It is not a measurement of incense smoke, and it does not make burning the bark harmless.

**Is cassia the cinnamon in five-spice?** Yes. 五香粉 normally contains cassia, star anise, clove, Sichuan pepper, and fennel. The overlap is why cassia smells familiar to anyone who has eaten Chinese braised food.

**Why does my cassia incense smell like the spice jar?** Because the same aldehyde that makes the spice recognisable is the first thing to reach the nose when the powder is heated. That is the material working as designed. If the effect is too strong, reduce the dose and give the formula a resin or a wood to sit on.

## Summary

Cassia is the bark of *Cinnamomum cassia*, a farmed laurel of southern China and Vietnam, and the material a Chinese recipe means by "cinnamon." It is thick, hard, sweet-pungent, and cheap; its job in a blend is warmth at the top without dominance. 桂皮 and 肉桂 differ by layer and density; cassia and Ceylon differ by thickness, aroma, and price. Coumarin is a dietary fact, not a smoke measurement. Buy whole bark when identity matters, mill fresh, dose small, and judge on heat.

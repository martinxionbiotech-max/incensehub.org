> **Direct answer:** 藿香 (huòxiāng) is a dried leaf herb — *Agastache rugosa*, the Chinese giant hyssop — with a mint-green opening and an anise-sweet heart. It belongs in the assistant layer of a Chinese blend, and it is routinely confused with patchouli because the two share part of a name.

## Key facts

> **Direct answer:** What a bag of 藿香 actually is depends on several things: species, plant part, form, crop age, drying quality, and whether the label is specific enough to repeat an order.

- **Species:** *Agastache rugosa*, the only plant this page treats as 藿香.
- **Trade name:** 藿香 or 土藿香; the near-homograph 广藿香 is patchouli and belongs to another page.
- **Part:** the upper herb — leaf, fine stem, and flower top — dried.
- **Scent:** cool green mint at the opening, sweet anise in the middle, dry herb at the close, with no resin base.
- **In fire:** quick and light as loose leaf; a supporting smoulder inside a bound stick.
- **In 合香:** an assistant or guide note (佐/使) that lifts the opening rather than carrying the blend.
- **Supply:** a widely grown crop with no scarcity premium; the premium worth paying is for cleanliness and correct drying.

Three things go wrong most often, and each is a paperwork problem rather than a matter of taste: an unnamed plant, an unexpected form, and a crop that has lost the fraction worth buying.

## What 藿香 actually is

> **Direct answer:** It is a herbaceous perennial in the mint family, grown for its leafy upper growth rather than for wood, root, or resin. The traded material is the cut and dried aerial herb, and the leaf is where the aroma sits.

*Agastache rugosa* is a stout perennial of the Lamiaceae, the family that also holds mint, basil, perilla, lavender, and patchouli. Mint-family plants share a set of structural habits that are useful in the field: opposite leaves set in pairs along the stem, a stem that is usually square in cross-section, and aromatic glands on the leaf surface. Those glands are the reason a handful of dried herb releases scent when it is crushed, long before any heat touches it.

The Flora of China account gives the measurements a field botanist would use:

- **Habit:** erect stems reaching 0.5–1.5 m.
- **Leaf:** blade 4.5–11 cm by 3–6.5 cm, cordate-ovate to oblong-lanceolate, margin serrate.
- **Flower:** purplish-blue, opening June through September.

The flowering tops are part of the harvest, which matters for grading: leaf-only material and leaf-plus-bud material are not the same product, even when both are sold as 藿香.

"Chinese giant hyssop" is a genus nickname, not a relationship. True hyssop (*Hyssopus officinalis*) sits in a different genus, with a sharper, camphoraceous leaf. An order for "hyssop" from a garden list is not an order for 藿香, and the invoice will not tell you so.

One more distinction belongs here rather than in a later section. Chinese materia medica has used 藿香 for the native plant for centuries, while the imported patchouli leaf took the prefix 广 (Guang, from Guangdong and the southern trade) to become 广藿香. That is a naming convention built on trade geography, not on botany. The two plants are both in Lamiaceae, which is where the resemblance ends: one is a fresh, anise-minty herb, the other an earthy, woody, balsamic leaf whose scent dominates a room.

## The name map: one character, four plants

> **Direct answer:** 藿香 is not a species name; it is a Chinese word that has attached itself to more than one traded material. Reading a label correctly means asking which plant, which part, and which trade name the seller is using.

The map below is the disambiguation this page exists to provide. It is also the first thing to check when a supplier's specification and a formula's assumption disagree.

| Written name | Plant | Family | Scent | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 藿香 / 土藿香 | *Agastache rugosa* | Lamiaceae | Minty, green, anise-sweet | The native Chinese herb; this page |
| 广藿香 | *Pogostemon cablin* | Lamiaceae | Earthy, woody, balsamic | Patchouli; a different genus and a different trade |
| "藿香" alone on a retail label | Undetermined | — | Either of the above | A label failure, not a product |
| True hyssop | *Hyssopus officinalis* | Lamiaceae | Sharp, camphoraceous, slightly bitter | A different genus, confused through English common names |
| 多摩羅跋香 and related sutra names | *Agastache rugosa* | Lamiaceae | As above | Liturgical names for the same herb, not separate species |

Two habits follow. Treat a binomial as the minimum acceptable identification: *Agastache rugosa* is a specification, while "藿香" is a word. Then read old sources with trade geography in mind — a Song or Ming text listing 藿香 means the native herb, while a modern invoice listing 广藿香 means imported patchouli.

The sutra names are often mistaken for plant names. 多摩羅跋香 (*tāmalapatra*), 兜娄婆香, 钵怛罗香, and 迦筭香 are transliterations for fragrant substances used in Buddhist offering, and their number reflects liturgical importance rather than taxonomic confusion.

## Aroma: mint, anise, and a green edge

> **Direct answer:** The scent opens green and minty, turns sweet and anise-like in the middle, and closes dry and herbal. There is no resinous base underneath, which is why the herb reads as a lift rather than a foundation.

The first impression of good dried leaf is cool and green, close to the smell of crushed mint but softer and less sharp. Within a few seconds the anise note arrives, sweet and slightly liquorice-like, and it is this middle that separates 藿香 from most other mint-family herbs. The close is drier: hay, leaf, a faint bitterness at the back of the nose. Nothing in the profile is smoky, balsamic, or animalic.

That structure is chemical before it is poetic. The anise-sweet character of *A. rugosa* is associated with methyl chavicol, also called estragole, the same molecule that gives anise, fennel, and tarragon their sweetness. The green-minty opening comes from monoterpenes such as limonene, and the balance between the two fractions shifts with population, harvest timing, and drying. This is why two samples sold under the same name can smell like cousins rather than twins, and why a formula that depends on the anise note needs a smell check at each lot.

A useful way to think about the profile is by time, not by intensity:

- **Opening, first minute:** green, cool, slightly grassy. This is the fraction that escapes the leaf most easily, and the fraction most easily lost in storage.
- **Middle, minutes two to ten:** sweet anise over dry herb. This is the identifying note, the one that says 藿香 rather than "some mint."
- **Close, after the sweet note fades:** hay and leaf dust, with a faint bitterness. There is no resin, no wood, no balsam to hold the scent in place.

The absence of a base is the most consequential fact about the material. In a blend, 藿香 gives a lift in the opening and then hands the composition over to whatever else is there. It cannot carry a formula's drydown, and any recipe that asks it to do so will smell thin in the second half of the burn. That is not a defect; it is what a leaf does.

Local landraces differ in leaf size, oil content, and the balance of anise-sweet to green-minty notes. A buyer who finds a lot that works should record its origin: the next lot may be the same species and a different scent.

## Where the plant grows, and why abundance matters

> **Direct answer:** *Agastache rugosa* is native to East Asia and recorded as widely distributed in China, with a range that reaches Japan, Korea, Russia, and North America. Nothing about it is rare, regulated, or difficult to obtain.

Abundance shapes the economics more than any other single fact. This is not a tree that must be wounded to produce resin, nor a slow-growing wood that takes decades to reach a useful grade. It is a perennial that can be raised from seed or division, cut in its first or second season, and dried with ordinary equipment. The cost of the finished herb sits mostly in labour, drying, and sorting, and the gap between a mediocre sack and a good one is a gap in handling rather than in rarity.

Three consequences follow for anyone buying it.

First, there is no supply-risk argument for paying a premium. A premium should buy species certainty, cleanliness, correct drying, and a traceable lot. It should not buy a story about scarcity, because the plant is not scarce.

Second, quality is easy to lose in a cheap supply chain. When the unit value is low, material can sit in a humid warehouse, be swept up with stems and soil, or be blended across seasons without anyone keeping a record. Low price does not prove low quality, but it does mean the burden of verification rests with the buyer.

Third, abundance makes the herb a workhorse rather than a headline. Formulators reach for it when they want freshness and lift at a cost that allows generous use, and they reach for patchouli or a resin when they want depth. The two roles are different jobs, and confusing them is the most common way a beginner misuses this material.

Wild and cultivated material both circulate. The line between them is often blurred: plants may be harvested from field margins, encouraged in semi-wild stands, or grown in rows. For incense, the cultivation question matters less than the drying record. A well-dried wild leaf beats a badly dried cultivated leaf, and no origin story changes that.

## From field to powder: harvest, drying, storage

> **Direct answer:** The crop is cut around flowering, dried in shade with moving air, stripped from coarse stem, and either bundled whole or milled. Moisture control is the whole game: leaf packed before it is stable will mould, and mould ends the material's usefulness.

The sequence is short, and each step has a characteristic failure.

**Cutting.** Stems are taken while the leaf is sound, generally as the plant comes into flower. Cut material is bulky and light, so it heats and sweats if it is piled before drying begins.

**Drying.** The crop is spread thin, out of direct sun, with air moving through it. Shade drying is slower and usually preserves a rounder green note. Sun drying is faster but bleaches colour and drives off the lightest volatiles. Drying in still, damp air is the worst option of the three, because the leaf can mould before it is stable.

**Stripping and sorting.** Once dry, leaf is separated from coarse stem and picked over for soil, grit, and foreign plant matter. This step is what separates a clean trade sample from a dusty one, and it is labour rather than technology.

**Bundling or milling.** Whole herb is tied for storage and sale. Material destined for blending is cut or milled, and the milling decision is a formulation decision, not a cosmetic one.

**Storage.** Dried leaf is hygroscopic; it pulls moisture from the air. It belongs in a dry, dark, ventilated place, away from strong-smelling goods, and it should be inspected rather than assumed.

Three mechanism chains explain most complaints about "bad 藿香." The first is a moisture chain:

> damp leaf → moisture migrates through the bundle → inner pockets stay wet → mould colonises the compressed centre → musty, cellar-like off-note → the note survives into the burn and contaminates the whole blend.

The second is a heat chain, and it produces a different failure:

> leaf dried too hot or too fast → surface volatiles flash off → the green top note disappears → what remains is hay and stem → the herb reads flat in a blend even though the leaf looks normal.

The third belongs to grinding, and it explains why powder behaves unlike leaf:

> leaf milled fine → surface area multiplies → aroma escapes faster in storage → the powder smells weaker after a few weeks → the formulator adds more to compensate → the blend turns sharp and dusty rather than sweeter.

All three chains point to the same buying habit: ask how the material was dried and stored, then confirm with the sample in your hand. Leaf that crumbles rather than bends is dry enough to keep. Leaf that bends like leather is still holding moisture. Leaf that smells of a cellar will not recover in your warehouse.

## Forms, and what each one does

> **Direct answer:** Whole herb, cut herb, powder, and essential oil are the same plant in different physical states. The form decides how fast the aroma leaves, how evenly the material packs, and which failures are likely.

| Form | Behaviour | Best use | Characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole dried herb | Slowest aroma loss; bulky; stems hold moisture | Storage, decoction-style blends, sachets | Mould in the stem bundle; uneven drying |
| Cut or crushed herb | Middle ground; releases well when heated | Loose blended incense, potpourri | Segregation of leaf and stem in the bag |
| Powder | Fast release, fast loss; packs into sticks | Incense sticks and cones | Stale aroma; dust; over-dosing to compensate |
| Flowering tops | Slightly sweeter, more delicate | Fine sachets, premium blends | Scarce; often mixed into leaf lots without labelling |
| Essential oil | Concentrated, not a classical form | Perfumery, experimental work | Solvent or carrier questions; not interchangeable with the herb |

The form question is where formulation mistakes start. A recipe written for powder cannot be followed with whole leaf at the same weight, because leaf occupies more volume and releases aroma more slowly. The reverse swap delivers the top note in a rush and then fades. Neither is wrong; both require rebalancing.

The essential oil deserves a boundary. It is a legitimate aromatic material, and it is not the same thing as the dried herb in a classical blend. The oil concentrates the volatile fraction and leaves behind the leaf's non-volatile matter, which changes how the material behaves in heat and how it smells in the bottle. This site treats the oil as a modern material with its own rules, not as a shortcut to an old recipe.

## The job it does in 合香

> **Direct answer:** In blended incense (合香) 藿香 works as an assistant or guide note: it lifts the opening, cuts sweetness, and adds a green transparency. It is almost never the chief note, and it cannot supply a drydown.

The classical framework for a blend is 君臣佐使 — chief, minister, assistant, guide. The terms describe hierarchy rather than chemistry: one material sets the character, others support it, one or two adjust the edges, and one carries the composition through. In that grammar, 藿香 reads as 佐 or 使, an assistant or guide. That placement is an editorial judgment drawn from how the material behaves in heat, not a quotation from a manual, and it is worth stating plainly because it prevents a common error: treating a bright herb as the centre of a formula.

What the herb actually contributes can be described in three moves.

**Lift.** The volatile fraction leaves the leaf early, so the first part of a burn is where 藿香 speaks. In a blend of sweet resins or heavy woods, that early green note makes the opening feel less dense.

**Cut.** The anise-sweet middle interrupts sweetness. A formula that reads cloying from benzoin or sandalwood can be opened up by a small proportion of this herb, in the way a squeeze of something acidic opens a rich sauce.

**Transparency.** The dry herbal close adds a clean, unsweetened edge that keeps a blend from feeling perfumed. This is the effect that makes the herb useful in clothing sachets, where the scent must read as clean fabric rather than as a perfume counter.

The mechanism behind all three is the same:

> high-volatility leaf → rapid release at low temperature → aromatic peak in the first third of the burn → volatile fraction exhausted → the blend's middle and base take over → the drydown belongs to other materials.

That chain has a practical consequence. A formula that leans on 藿香 for its identity will smell wonderful at the start and anonymous at the end. A formula that uses it as a lift will smell interesting throughout, because the herb's job finishes before the base's job begins.

What the herb cannot do is equally worth naming. It cannot add weight, it cannot fix a blend that has no base, and it cannot substitute for patchouli in a formula built on depth. A small amount of 藿香 in a patchouli-heavy blend reads as a green flash at the opening; a large amount reads as a mistake, because the two materials are pulling in opposite directions.

## Quality: a working grading table

> **Direct answer:** There is no industry grading ladder for 藿香. What follows is an editorial working scale built from the variables that actually change performance: species certainty, leaf condition, stem and dust load, aroma, and storage history.

| Grade | Species certainty | Leaf condition | Stem and dust | Aroma | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Named *A. rugosa*, lot traceable | Whole, green, intact, snaps cleanly | Low; sorted | Bright mint-anise, no must | Fine blends, sachets, reference samples |
| B | Named species, origin vague | Mostly intact, some brown edges | Moderate | Correct but quieter | General blending, sticks |
| C | "藿香" only, no binomial | Broken, faded, mixed leaf and stem | High | Hay, dust, faint anise | Utility work, test batches only |
| Reject | Ambiguous or wrong species | Mould, caking, off-colour | Variable | Musty, sour, or absent | Do not burn |

The ladder is ours, not a standard, and it is deliberately ordered by consequence rather than by appearance. Species certainty sits first because a grade-A patchouli leaf and a grade-A agastache leaf are not interchangeable, and no amount of leaf quality fixes a wrong plant. Aroma sits last only because it is the easiest thing to check and the easiest thing to fake with a fresh-looking sample.

Grading language tends to collapse these variables into one word, usually "premium." That word carries no information. A useful specification replaces it with a species, a part, a form, a crop window, and an agreed aroma reference.

## Telling good leaf from bad: six checks

> **Direct answer:** Six checks settle most questions without a laboratory: ask for the binomial, feel for dryness, look at the stem ratio, look at the dust, smell the closed bag, and ask whether the leaf has been distilled.

1. **Ask for the binomial.** *Agastache rugosa* on the paperwork, not just 藿香. If the supplier cannot say, the sample is a gamble rather than a purchase.
2. **Feel for dryness.** Leaf should snap or crumble. Leaf that bends and stays bent is holding moisture, and moisture is the precondition for every storage failure.
3. **Look at the stem ratio.** Stems are structural filler with little aroma. A bag that is half stem is a bag sold at leaf price with stem weight inside it.
4. **Look at the dust.** Fine dust at the bottom of a bag is partly handling loss and partly a sign that the material has been moved and re-bagged several times. Some dust is normal; a layer is not.
5. **Smell the closed bag before opening it.** The first rush should be green and anise-sweet. Mustiness, sourness, or a cellar smell means the lot was stored wet, and no amount of airing fixes mould.
6. **Ask whether the leaf has been distilled.** Steam-distilled leaf can be dried and sold as herb. It looks plausible and smells hollow, because the volatile fraction has already been taken. The check is simple: a genuinely aromatic leaf keeps giving scent when you rub it between your palms; exhausted leaf gives a brief ghost and then nothing.

Colour misleads in both directions. Fresh leaf is green and older leaf browns, so colour says something about age, but it does not prove quality, and vivid uniform green in an old crop is a question rather than a verdict. Smell and feel are harder to fake.

## When 藿香 is not to hand: substitutions and their costs

> **Direct answer:** No other herb reproduces 藿香 exactly, because the anise-sweet middle is fairly specific. Substitution is therefore a matter of choosing which part of the profile you can afford to lose.

| Candidate | What you gain | What you lose | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mint (*Mentha* spp.) | Stronger cool-green opening | The anise middle; adds menthol sharpness | You need lift and can accept a colder profile |
| Perilla leaf (紫苏) | Green, slightly shiso-like top | Sweetness; perilla reads more savoury | You want a green note in a food-adjacent blend |
| Elsholtzia (香薷) | Herbal, slightly medicinal lift | Anise sweetness; rougher in smoke | You are working inside a classical herb palette |
| Basil (罗勒) | Sweet-green, anise-leaning | Mint coolness; basil is softer and more food-like | You want sweetness without the mint |
| Patchouli (广藿香) | Depth, longevity | Everything else — this is the opposite material | Never as a like-for-like swap |
| True hyssop | Camphoraceous lift | The anise note and the softness | Almost never; the name is the only link |

The table is a decision aid, not a licence. The honest summary is that a blend built around 藿香's specific anise-mint character has no true substitute; the closest approximations lose either the coolness or the sweetness, and both losses are audible in smoke.

There is a second, less obvious substitution problem: 藿香 is sometimes used as a cheap green note in blends that name a more expensive herb. That is a commercial decision with a sensory cost, and a buyer who approves a sample should ask whether the approved sample and the production lot share a specification. Silent substitution is the failure mode this whole page keeps circling back to.

## What happens when it burns

> **Direct answer:** As loose leaf it burns fast, smokes heavily, and struggles to stay lit. As a minor component in a bound stick it smoulders steadily and releases its aroma early. Smoke and fine particles are the unavoidable output of any burn, so the session belongs in a ventilated room rather than a closed one.

Loose dried leaf is a poor solo burn. The leaf is thin, the volatile fraction is high, and the structure is not dense enough to hold an ember. The result is a flare, a lot of smoke, and a quick collapse into ash. This is not a defect in the herb; it is why the classical use is blending and scenting rather than solo burning.

In a stick or cone, the herb is one powder among several. Its particles sit inside a matrix of wood powder, binder, and other aromatics, and the ember moves through that matrix at a rate set by density, particle size, and airflow. Fine herb powder increases surface area, which means the aromatic fraction is released earlier and more completely. Coarse powder releases more slowly but packs less evenly.

The burn sequence is predictable:

> ember reaches the herb particles → heat drives off the volatile fraction → the anise and green notes enter the smoke stream → the leaf's non-volatile residue chars → the aromatic contribution ends → the base materials carry the rest of the stick.

Understanding that sequence explains a complaint that puzzles beginners: "the incense smelled great for two minutes and then smelled like nothing." The herb did its job. The formula asked it to do a job it does not have the structure to do.

Smoke is the other half of the story. Any incense burn produces particulate matter and combustion gases, and aromatic leaf is no exception. The correct practice is ventilation, distance, and restraint; the incorrect practice is treating a pleasant smell as evidence of clean air. See the [safety guide](/safety/) for the general rules.

## Sachet versus stick: two different release problems

> **Direct answer:** A sachet releases aroma at body temperature over days, so the sweet middle dominates. A stick releases it at combustion temperature in seconds, so the green opening dominates. The same leaf gives two different perfumes.

Clothing use is not a folk substitute for burning; it is a different physical regime. Inside a sachet, the leaf sits at roughly skin temperature and loses its volatiles by slow evaporation. There is no flame, no pyrolysis, and no ash. The molecules that leave first are the lightest ones, but the rate is so slow that the anise-sweet fraction has time to dominate the experience, and the scent persists for as long as the leaf retains oil.

In a stick, heat arrives fast. Cellulose and lignin break down, the oil is driven off in a burst, and part of the aromatic fraction is altered by the heat itself. The result is brighter and smokier, with the green top note forward and the sweetness compressed.

| Question | Sachet | Stick |
|---|---|---|
| Heat source | Body heat, ambient warmth | Glowing ember |
| Release rate | Slow, over days or weeks | Fast, over the burn |
| Dominant note | Anise-sweet middle | Green mint opening |
| Residue | None; leaf stays in the bag | Ash and smoke |
| Main failure | Absorbing odours from the room | Over-dosing to chase the top note |

That difference explains a puzzle in the classical record: the same herb appears both in compounded incense and in clothing, and the two uses are not redundant. A formulator who tests only in a stick will underestimate the sweetness; a sachet maker who tests only in the bag will overestimate how long the green note lasts.

## The classical record

> **Direct answer:** The documented record covers three uses — blended incense, scenting clothing, and liturgical naming — plus a materia-medica tradition that this page treats as traditional rather than clinical.

**Blended incense (合香).** 藿香 appears as a fresh-herb component in compounded incense, where its function matches what the smell does: lift the opening and cut heaviness. The classical recipes that include it tend to be built around a resin or wood base, which is consistent with the mechanism described above.

**Clothing sachets.** The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records the use of 藿香 in clothing, with the phrase 可着衣服中 — it may be placed among clothes. This is scenting, not burning, and it fits the material: a clean green note that reads well on fabric and leaves no resin.

**Liturgical names.** The Song *Xiangpu* (香譜) records 藿香 under the Buddhist name 多摩羅跋香, and the *Xiangcheng* lists several sutra names, including 兜娄婆香, 钵怛罗香, and 迦筭香. Their number is the strongest evidence that the herb mattered in Buddhist offering practice — evidence about liturgy, not about botany.

**Materia medica.** The herb holds a prominent place in the Chinese pharmacopoeia tradition, where it is grouped under 化湿 — "transforming dampness" — and neighbouring categories. That vocabulary belongs to a humoral system of correspondences. Read it as traditional belief expressed in traditional technical language; it is not a description of a pharmacological mechanism, and it does not convert into a modern medical claim.

A short table holds the record together:

| Layer | What is recorded | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| Incense | 合香 use as a fresh-herb component | Consistent with the material's behaviour in heat |
| Clothing | 可着衣服中 in the *Xiangcheng* | A scenting use, not a burning use |
| Liturgy | Sutra names in the *Xiangpu* and *Xiangcheng* | Evidence of religious importance |
| Medicine | 化湿 and related categories | Traditional belief; not clinical evidence |

## Where tradition stops and evidence begins

> **Direct answer:** The botanical identity is established, the historical uses are documented, and the medicinal claims are traditional. Nothing on this page should be read as a clinical finding, and no medical claim is being made here.

The boundary is worth drawing explicitly because incense writing tends to blur it. A plant can have a long record of use, a distinctive chemistry, and a place in religious practice without any of that constituting evidence that it treats a disease. Those are different kinds of claim, and mixing them is how folklore becomes marketing.

What can be said with confidence is narrower than the traditional literature suggests: a named Lamiaceae perennial, a characteristic volatile oil, documented use in compounded incense and clothing sachets, a cluster of sutra names, and a place in the materia medica under a humoral framework. What cannot be said is that burning it produces a health outcome, that its old categories map onto modern diagnoses, or that safety in one use implies safety in another.

That boundary is not a rejection of tradition. It is what lets the tradition be described accurately, with the classical language labelled as classical and the established facts visible as such.

## Sourcing: a spec that survives a repeat order

> **Direct answer:** A repeatable purchase needs a written specification, an approved sample, and a lot record. Without those three, the second order is a new experiment.

The specification should answer these questions in writing:

- **Species.** *Agastache rugosa*, with the Chinese name 藿香 or 土藿香. Reject 广藿香; it is a different material.
- **Part.** Aerial herb, with the leaf-to-stem expectation stated and flowering tops included or excluded.
- **Form.** Whole, cut to an agreed size, or milled to an agreed particle range.
- **Crop window.** Which harvest, and how it was dried — shade-dried and sun-dried are different products.
- **Cleanliness.** Foreign matter limits, dust expectation, and whether the material has been sieved.
- **Aroma reference.** A retained sample both sides have smelled, described in words: green, minty, anise-sweet, no must.
- **Storage and distillation.** Where the lot has been kept, for how long, and whether the leaf was steam-distilled before sale.

Two process habits make the specification stick. The first is sample approval against a written description rather than a memory: approve a sample, keep half of it sealed, and compare each incoming lot to the retained half. The second is a lot record that travels with the material, so that a good batch can be repeated and a bad one traced.

The commercial risk with this herb is not scarcity; it is silent substitution. Because the material is cheap and the name ambiguous, a supplier can change species, origin, or crop year without the paperwork changing. The defence is boring and effective: named species, retained samples, and a willingness to reject a lot that does not match.

## Storage and shelf life

> **Direct answer:** Dried 藿香 keeps best in a sealed, dark, dry container away from heat and strong smells. Whole herb outlasts cut herb, and cut herb outlasts powder. None of the forms improves with age.

The enemies, in order of speed, are moisture, heat, light, and odour transfer. Moisture invites mould and also accelerates the loss of the volatile fraction. Heat drives off the top notes. Light bleaches colour and oxidises aromatic compounds. Strong smells migrate into the leaf, because dried plant material is an absorbent.

Store whole or cut herb sealed, in a dark cupboard at a stable temperature, away from spices, soap, and essential oils. Powder belongs in small quantities used within a season, because its surface area works against it. Gentle air-drying can rescue texture after damp storage; nothing rescues a lot that has grown mould.

Shelf life is a judgment rather than a date. The test is sensory: crush a little between the palms, smell it, and compare it with the memory of the lot when it arrived. A herb that has lost its anise middle is still usable as a green note in a utility blend, but it is no longer the material a fine formula was built around.

## A bench note: testing a new lot

> **Direct answer:** Test a new lot in a small trial blend, not in a production run. Compare it against a retained sample of the lot you already trust, and record the comparison in words.

The trial should be small enough to throw away. Build it from the new herb plus one resin and one wood you know well, so that any change in the opening can be attributed to the herb rather than to the base. Burn it beside a stick made from the retained sample, smell both at the start, the middle, and the end, and write down what changed: the anise note, the green edge, the speed of fade, the amount of smoke.

This habit catches the two failures that a visual inspection misses. Exhausted leaf, which has been distilled before sale, will read thin from the first minute. Over-dried leaf will read like hay and will not recover its top note in the blend. Neither problem is visible in a photograph, and both are obvious in a side-by-side burn.

## Safety

> **Direct answer:** The risks are the ordinary risks of burning plant material and handling dried leaf: smoke and particulate matter from combustion, dust from milling, and mould in badly stored herb. None of these is addressed by the herb's traditional reputation.

Incense smoke contains particulate matter and combustion products regardless of what is being burned. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, keep burning material away from children and pets, and never leave a burning stick unattended. A pleasant aroma is not a measure of air quality.

Dried leaf handled in quantity raises dust, and fine powder raises more. Milling and sieving belong in a ventilated area, ideally with a mask, and the work should be separated from food preparation. Leaf that smells musty, shows caking, or has visible mould should be discarded rather than burned; combustion does not sterilise a contaminated material, it aerosolises it.

A chemistry note belongs here rather than in a health section. The anise-sweet fraction of *A. rugosa* is associated with estragole, a compound that food-flavouring rules in several jurisdictions treat as a substance of concern and limit accordingly. That is a food-safety framework and not a finding about incense smoke, but it is a reason to treat the herb as a fragrance material with a defined use rather than as something to consume or to use therapeutically. This page makes no medical claims in either direction; see the [safety guide](/safety/) for the site's general position.

## FAQ

**Is 藿香 the same as patchouli?** No, and the confusion is the reason this page spends so long on names. 藿香 is the leaf of *Agastache rugosa*; 广藿香 names *Pogostemon cablin*, the plant behind patchouli. One is green and anise-sweet, the other earthy and woody. Different genera, different trade routes, no substitution. Our [patchouli](/ingredients/patchouli/) page covers the second plant.

**What does 藿香 smell like?** Green and minty at first, then sweet and anise-like, then dry and herbal. No resinous base sits underneath, so the scent lifts and fades instead of settling.

**Is 土藿香 a different species?** No. It is another name for *Agastache rugosa*, with 土 ("native") marking the local plant against imported 广藿香 — one species, one trade distinction.

**Can you burn 藿香 on its own?** You can, but it performs poorly. Loose dried leaf flares, smokes heavily, and struggles to hold an ember. Its classical role is as a component in blended incense or as a clothing sachet, and that is where it behaves best.

**Is Chinese giant hyssop the same as hyssop?** No. "Chinese giant hyssop" is an English common name for *Agastache rugosa*. True hyssop is *Hyssopus officinalis*, a different genus with a sharper, more camphoraceous scent. The shared word is a naming coincidence, not a botanical relationship.

## Summary

藿香 is the dried aerial herb of *Agastache rugosa*, a mint-family perennial from East Asia with a green, minty, anise-sweet scent and no resinous base. Its classical roles were blended incense, clothing sachets, and Buddhist liturgical naming; its materia-medica history is traditional rather than clinical. The practical problem is naming: 藿香 and 广藿香 are different plants, and a label that says only 藿香 identifies nothing. Buy by binomial, judge by freshness and dryness, use it as a lift rather than a foundation, and store it away from moisture, heat, and strong smells.

## Evidence status

Identity is settled by taxonomy and can be checked against the flora record. Historical use is documented in the Song and Ming incense texts, which is a different kind of evidence: it tells us what people did, not what the material does. Authenticity is a screening question, answered by a binomial and a sample. Health claims remain unestablished, and this page leaves them there.

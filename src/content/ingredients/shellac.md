> **Direct answer:** Shellac (紫草茸, zǐcǎoróng) is lac — a resin secreted by the lac insect *Kerria lacca*, a scale insect that encrusts the twigs it feeds on. It is an animal material, not a botanical one, and the classical Chinese record knew that. In incense it served as a colourant and a functional ingredient in formulas such as 宝金香, not as a fragrance in its own right.

## An animal material in a plant-based craft

> **Direct answer:** Nearly everything in an incense workshop is plant material — woods, resins, barks, roots. Lac is the exception. It is produced by an insect, and that single fact changes how it is sourced, how it is judged, and why it confuses people who expect a botanical.

Incense is usually described as a plant craft. Agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, myrrh, benzoin, clove, patchouli — the vocabulary is botanical from end to end. Two materials break the pattern, and both are insect products: beeswax and lac. The first is familiar enough that nobody thinks of it as animal. The second is often mistaken for a plant resin simply because it arrives looking like one.

The mistake is understandable. Lac comes as hard, brittle, reddish-brown flakes or lumps; it melts, it dissolves in alcohol, it behaves in every visible way like a resin. Nothing about the appearance announces an insect. The identification has to come from the record, and the record is unambiguous.

This is not a technicality to file away. Origin determines what the material is chemically, how it should be stored, what it can substitute for, and whether a given buyer wants it at all. A maker who avoids animal-derived ingredients needs to know that a red "resin" in a classical formula is not a plant. A maker who does not care still needs to know, because the supply chain, the grading, and the adulteration risks all follow from the insect origin.

> **Editorial note:** The common misconception is that "natural resin" means "plant resin." It does not. Lac is natural, it is a resin, and it is animal. Those three facts coexist, and any catalogue that files lac under botanicals is filing it wrongly.

## The insect behind the resin

> **Direct answer:** *Kerria lacca* is a scale insect of the family Kerriidae. The female settles on a young shoot, feeds on sap, and secretes a resinous covering that hardens into a crust around her body. Harvesting means cutting the encrusted twigs, not collecting a plant exudate.

The life cycle explains the material. A female lac insect settles on a tender shoot, inserts her mouthparts into the plant's sap stream, and begins to secrete resin from glands in her body wall. The secretion spreads and hardens into a continuous envelope. Inside it, the insect continues to feed and reproduce; the crust becomes a protective chamber against drying air and predators.

That is the whole mechanism of production. The resin is not a wound response by the tree, and it is not sap that has dried. It is manufactured by the insect and deposited on the surface of the twig, which is why the harvested material arrives as a crust that must be separated from wood and insect remains.

The host plant is incidental to the identity of the material. The insect uses a range of host species depending on region, and the resin is named for the insect rather than the tree. That is the opposite of how most incense materials are named, where the plant is the reference point and the processing is secondary. Here the animal is the constant and the plant is the variable.

A useful comparison is beeswax. Both materials are insect secretions harvested from structures the insects built. Both are named for the animal rather than for a plant. Both sit in the "functional" rather than the "fragrant" layer of a formula. And both are routinely described in botanical catalogues without any mention of their origin, which is how confusion propagates. The difference is that beeswax is a wax and lac is a polyester resin, which is why their behaviour in a blend diverges sharply once heat is applied.

## Four names, one material

> **Direct answer:** 紫草茸, 紫鉚, 紫矿, 赤胶, and 紫梗 are historical Chinese names for lac, and "shellac" is the modern English name for its purified form. The shared 紫 element records the red dye value that made the material worth trading.

The naming history is a record of use. 紫 ("purple" or "red-purple") appears in most of the classical names because the material's primary commercial value was colour. 赤胶 ("red glue") describes both the hue and the resinous character. 紫梗 ("purple stem") points at the form in which it was harvested — a resinous encrustation on a stem.

"Shellac" comes from a different direction entirely. The English word derives from the material's use as a finish, and the modern product is the resin melted, filtered, and formed into flakes or buttons. That is the same substance as the classical 紫矿, at a different stage of purification.

For a reader, the practical point is a mapping, not an etymology:

- 紫草茸, 紫鉚, 紫矿, 赤胶, 紫梗 — lac, the raw or semi-purified insect resin.
- Seedlac — the crushed, washed, unmelted grain.
- Shellac — the melted and filtered resin, formed into flakes or blocks.
- Lac dye — the red colourant extracted from the resin, historically a separate product.

None of those names refers to a plant. That is the sentence to remember when a supplier describes the material as a "natural plant resin."

## What 宝金香 actually specifies

> **Direct answer:** The *Xiangcheng* formula 宝金香 calls for 沉香一两, 檀香一两, 乳香一钱, 紫矿二钱, and 安息香一钱. Read as weights, that puts lac at roughly one twelfth of the blend — not a trace, and more than either of the two minor resins.

The recipe is short enough to analyse directly. Assuming the standard Ming ratio of ten 钱 to one 两, the formula resolves as follows.

| Ingredient | Chinese | Amount | Share of total (assuming 1 两 = 10 钱) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agarwood | 沉香 | 1 两 = 10 钱 | about 41.7% |
| Sandalwood | 檀香 | 1 两 = 10 钱 | about 41.7% |
| Frankincense | 乳香 | 1 钱 | about 4.2% |
| Lac | 紫矿 | 2 钱 | about 8.3% |
| Benzoin | 安息香 | 1 钱 | about 4.2% |
| **Total** | | **24 钱** | **100%** |

Two woods carry more than four-fifths of the blend. That is the aromatic core, and it tells us what kind of composition this is: a wood-dominant incense with resin modifiers. The remaining three ingredients occupy the last fifth, and lac takes the largest share of that remainder.

The arithmetic corrects a habit of speech. Lac is often called a "minor ingredient," which is true in the sense that it is not the fragrance, and misleading if it suggests a pinch. At 8.3% it outweighs frankincense and benzoin combined. Whatever the material was doing in that formula, it was doing it at a mass fraction large enough to matter.

What was it doing? The recipe does not say. A quantity list is not a job description, and the classical texts generally record proportions rather than rationales. Three roles are plausible from the material's properties — colour, binding, and fixative support — and they are not mutually exclusive. The colour role is the best evidenced, because lac's dye value is documented independently. The binding role is the most speculative, because a resin that melts at low temperature can act as a binder, but the text does not claim it does.

> **Editorial note:** Reading function into an old formula is inference, and it should be labelled as inference. The numbers are evidence; the explanation is ours. A page that presents "lac was used as a binder in 宝金香" as though the recipe said so has moved from citation to invention.

## The functional layer of a 合香 formula

> **Direct answer:** Classical 合香 built a composition in layers: an aromatic core, then materials that modified how it burned, bound, or looked. Lac belongs to that second layer, alongside bletilla as a binder and beeswax as a wax. The 君臣佐使 framework describes hierarchy of effect, not a shopping list.

The 君臣佐使 (chief, minister, assistant, guide) vocabulary is often applied to Chinese incense, and it is useful as long as it is not over-read. It describes a hierarchy of roles within a composition: what sets the character, what supports it, what modifies it, and what carries it. It does not assign each material to a fixed rank across all formulas.

In 宝金香, the two woods are the chief and the supporting minister in any reasonable reading. The three minor ingredients sit below them, and their contribution is functional rather than olfactory. A comparison across the functional materials makes the pattern clearer.

| Material | Chinese | Origin | Primary function in a formula | Behaviour under heat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lac | 紫草茸 / 紫矿 | Insect secretion | Colour; possible binding and fixative support | Softens early, then burns with a waxy note |
| Bletilla | 白及 | Plant tuber | Binder — holds a paste together | Does not volatilise; stays in the ash |
| Beeswax | 蜂蜡 | Insect wax | Texture, plasticity, slower burn | Melts, then burns as a wax |
| Dragon's blood | 血竭 | Plant fruit resin | Red colour; aromatic resin in its own right | Melts and fumes with a distinct resinous smell |

The table separates two things that are often conflated: materials that contribute to the smell and materials that contribute to the object. Lac and bletilla are mostly object materials. Dragon's blood is both, which is precisely why it is a poor substitute for lac when colour alone is wanted — it brings its own scent into the blend.

This is where the [合香 blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) becomes practical. A formula is not a list of aromatics; it is a structure with functional slots. Once the slots are visible, the question about any material changes from "what does it smell like" to "which slot does it fill, and what happens to the other slots if it is removed."

## From encrusted twig to flake

> **Direct answer:** Harvest cuts resin-coated twigs. The crude lac is crushed, washed to separate resin from wood and insect debris, then melted and strained into flakes or blocks. Each step removes something different, and each one changes what the material is good for.

The processing chain runs like this:

resin-encrusted twigs are cut → the crust is broken off and crushed → washing in water carries away much of the water-soluble red dye along with soluble impurities → the washed grain (seedlac) is dried → gentle melting softens the resin → straining removes twig fragments and insect remains → the melt is poured and cooled into flakes or button shapes.

Notice what the wash does. The dye and the resin behave differently in water: the laccaic acids dissolve, while the resin does not. That single difference explains a whole family of products. If the wash water is kept and processed, it yields lac dye. If the resin is kept and purified, it yields shellac — which is paler precisely because the colour has been partly removed.

This is why colour grade and purity are linked in a way that is easy to misread. A deep red lac is not necessarily "better" than a pale shellac; the two are different products aimed at different uses. For a dyer or a classical formula, colour is the point. For a varnish maker, pale and clear is the point, because colour in a finish is a defect.

Heat is the second lever. Lac softens at a low temperature — well below the temperature of boiling water — which is convenient for melting and dangerous for over-processing. Push the melt too hot and the resin darkens and takes on a cooked note. That is one reason the classical material and the modern varnish grade are not simply interchangeable: the modern product has been through a controlled melt that the raw material never saw.

| Form | What it is | Typical use | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crude lac / sticklac | Resin crust broken from twigs, with debris | Starting point for further processing | High debris load; variable dye content |
| Seedlac | Crushed, washed, dried grain | Intermediate; dye often removed here | Residual water content; storage |
| Shellac flakes | Melted, strained, solidified | Varnish, finish, modern crafting use | Melt temperature history; clarity |
| Button lac | Melt poured into disc shapes | Traditional varnish trade form | Same as flakes, different handling |
| Ground lac | Powdered resin | Classical formula use; colour work | Dust; static; uneven particle size |

The forms are stages, not varieties. A buyer who specifies "shellac" and receives seedlac has received an earlier stage of the same material, with more debris and more dye. That is not fraud, but it is a specification failure, and it is the kind of thing that only shows up when the material is melted.

## The dye that came off in the wash

> **Direct answer:** Lac's red colour comes from laccaic acids, which dissolve in water while the resin does not. Washing crude lac therefore splits one harvest into two products — a dye liquor and a resin grain — and that split explains why pale shellac and deep red lac are the same starting material.

Anyone who has washed a red textile knows how readily some natural reds run. Lac dye behaves in that tradition: it is water-soluble, and the traditional processing exploits the fact. Crude lac is steeped and agitated, the colour passes into the water, and the resin remains as a paler grain. The liquor can then be concentrated and dried into a dye, while the grain is melted into shellac.

This is a neat piece of process engineering, and it is worth pausing on because it is counter-intuitive. Most resin processing tries to keep everything in the resin. Lac processing deliberately lets part of the material go, because the part being let go is a product in its own right.

The dye itself takes readily to protein fibres and has a long history on silk and wool. Cellulose fibres generally need a mordant to fix it well, which is the ordinary chemistry of natural dyeing rather than a defect of lac. The shade sits in the red to crimson range, warmer and less blue than some cochineal reds, and it was valued for that warmth.

For an incense maker, the dye history matters in two ways. First, it explains the colour names: a material called 赤胶 ("red glue") was named for a property that could be washed away, which tells you how central the dye trade was. Second, it warns against expecting modern shellac to behave like the classical material. Varnish-grade shellac has usually been through a wash and a melt that removed much of the colour. If a formula's red depends on the lac, the pale flake on the shelf may not deliver it.

> **Editorial note:** This is a case where "the same material" is true and misleading at once. Chemically, dye and resin come from one insect secretion. Practically, they are sold as separate products with separate specifications, and a buyer who orders one expecting the other will be disappointed for reasons that have nothing to do with quality.

## Judging quality

> **Direct answer:** Three things decide lac quality: how clean the resin is, how much colour it carries, and how it behaves when melted. For incense use, cleanliness and correct identification matter more than fragrance, because the material was never a lead scent.

The checks, in the order that matters for a formula:

1. **Purity.** Twig fragments, insect remains, and added resins are the common contaminants. A clean melt that strains clear is the practical test; grit that survives straining is a sign of careless processing.
2. **Colour.** Deep red-brown suggests good dye content; pale amber suggests the dye has been washed out. Neither is inherently better — match the colour to the intended role.
3. **Melt behaviour.** The resin should soften gently and flow without smoking. A material that scorches before it flows has been over-heated in processing or is not what it claims to be.
4. **Smell.** Warm, waxy, faintly sweet. A sharp or acrid note points to contamination or heat damage.
5. **Solubility.** Shellac dissolves in alcohol and in alkaline water; it does not dissolve in plain water. A material that disperses in water is not shellac.

That last check is the most useful one for a buyer who suspects a substitution. Water behaviour is cheap to test and hard to fake. A resin that clouds plain water is behaving like a gum, not like lac.

> **Editorial note:** Colour grade and quality are not the same axis. A pale shellac can be perfectly clean and a deep red lac can be full of twig. Buyers who equate "darker" with "better" are judging the dye, not the material — and in a varnish context they are judging it backwards.

## Why shellac left the incense bench

> **Direct answer:** Shellac became an industrial material — a wood finish, a record substrate, an electrical insulator, a food glaze — and the modern supply is built around those uses. Classical incense formulas predate that industry, and the shellac available today is a varnish-grade product rather than a formula-grade one.

The history is a useful corrective to the assumption that an old material is still sold the way it was. For much of the twentieth century, shellac was a bulk industrial resin. It coated furniture and floors, it was moulded into gramophone records, it insulated electrical windings, and in food it appeared as a glaze. Those uses drove the specification: pale colour, consistent melting, low wax, predictable solubility.

Each of those priorities is the opposite of what a dyer or a classical formula wants. Pale colour means the dye has been removed. Low wax means a refining step that the raw material never had. Consistent melting means controlled heat history, which is exactly the variable that changes the aroma of a resin. The industrial product is better for industry and less representative of the historical material.

When synthetic resins displaced shellac in the mid-twentieth century, the surviving trade narrowed further. What remains is a specialty material sold mainly to varnish, restoration, and food-glaze users, with spec sheets written for those applications. An incense maker buying "shellac" today is buying into that supply chain, not into a Ming-dynasty workshop.

The practical consequence is that identity and provenance questions have to be asked explicitly. A supplier may sell a perfectly good varnish flake and have no idea whether it corresponds to the material a classical formula assumed. That is not a criticism of the supplier; it is a mismatch of categories, and it is the buyer's job to notice it.

For the historical recipe, the closer match is usually a less-refined grade — seedlac or a crude lac — precisely because it retains more dye and has been through less heat. The catch is that less-refined material is dirtier and more variable, so the maker trades consistency for authenticity. That trade-off should be made deliberately rather than by accident.

## If you cannot get lac

> **Direct answer:** There is no neutral substitute. Every replacement changes the colour, the burn, or the smell, and usually more than one of them. The useful question is which function the formula actually depends on, then which substitute preserves that function at the least cost to the others.

Substitution questions usually arrive as "what can I use instead of 紫草茸?" The honest answer is that the question is underspecified. Lac does three things at once — it colours, it adds a small amount of resinous mass, and it may help bind — and a substitute that matches one of those will usually miss the others.

| What the formula needs | Closest option | What changes | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red colour only, in a dry blend | Lac dye, or a mineral/synthetic red | No resinous mass, no binding effect | Colour strength varies by lot; test on the actual paste |
| Red colour plus resin character | Dragon's blood (血竭) | Adds a balsamic scent the formula did not have | The smell may dominate a quiet wood core |
| Binding in a paste | Bletilla (白及) | No colour, different burn residue | Gum binders behave differently from melting resins |
| A wax-like texture change | Beeswax (蜂蜡) | No colour; softer finished stick | Wax loading changes how the stick draws air |
| Historical fidelity | Seedlac or crude lac | More debris, more dye, more variability | Requires cleaning and testing before use |

The rows are ordered by how often the question actually comes up. Most people asking about a substitute want colour, and most of them are working in a medium where a dye will do the job. The trap is assuming that a colour match is a formula match: a dye that reproduces the shade does nothing about the resin mass that the original ingredient contributed.

For the historical case, the least disruptive substitute is usually another stage of the same material. Seedlac is closer to what a Ming workshop handled than a modern pale flake is, and it keeps the dye that a varnish-grade product has lost. The cost is cleanliness and consistency, which is a real cost in a production setting.

> **Editorial note:** "Natural substitute" is not a meaningful category on its own. Cochineal is natural, insect-derived, and red, and it is a dye rather than a resin — it will colour a paste and will not bind it. The categories that matter are chemical and functional, not marketing ones.

## The red-resin confusion: lac and dragon's blood

> **Direct answer:** One comes from an insect and one from a palm fruit. 紫草茸 is secreted by *Kerria lacca*; 血竭 (dragon's blood) is a resin from *Daemonorops* / *Calamus* palms. Their similarity is visual, and the historical record notes that the resemblance caused real confusion.

The comparison is worth setting out in full, because the two materials are the most commonly swapped pair in the red-resin category.

| | Lac (紫草茸 / 紫矿) | Dragon's blood (血竭) |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Insect secretion, *Kerria lacca* | Fruit resin of *Daemonorops* / *Calamus* palms |
| Colour | Red-brown to orange-brown; dye water-soluble | Deep red to dark brown |
| Solubility | Resin in alcohol; dye in water | Resin in alcohol; characteristic balsamic note |
| Aroma | Waxy, faintly sweet, subdued | Resinous, heavier, with a distinct balsamic character |
| Classical role | Dye; functional ingredient in formulas | Red resin with medicinal and colorant use |
| Behaviour in a blend | Functional; little aromatic contribution | Aromatic as well as colouring |
| Substitution consequence | — | Adds a scent the original formula did not have |

The last row is the practical reason to care. Substituting dragon's blood for lac does not simply change the colour source; it changes the smell, because dragon's blood is an aromatic resin in its own right. A formula built around a quiet wood core will read differently with a balsamic red resin in it.

The confusion is documented in the record itself: the *Bencao Gangmu* takes the trouble to separate them, which implies that earlier or concurrent practice did not always manage it. That pattern recurs across materia medica — a name covers a category until someone with better information divides it. Here the dividing line is origin, and origin is precisely what a glance cannot supply.

See [dragon's blood](/ingredients/dragons-blood/) for the plant-resin side of the comparison.

## What happens when it burns

> **Direct answer:** Shellac softens before it burns. At the coal front it melts, wicks into neighbouring powder, and can seal the surface of a stick — which is why a small percentage behaves very differently from a large one.

Follow the sequence at the burning edge:

the coal front advances → heat reaches the shellac particles ahead of it → the resin softens well below combustion temperature → it flows into the gaps between powder particles → the melt cools and resolidifies slightly behind the front → the local surface becomes less porous → air draw through that section drops.

That chain explains two observations that otherwise seem contradictory. At low dose, shellac behaves as a harmless functional ingredient: the melted resin fills small voids, the stick burns evenly, and the waxy note is barely perceptible. At high dose, the same mechanism works against the burn: a continuous film forms, the draw becomes uneven, and the stick can go out or tunnel.

The lesson is about percentage, not about the material's character. A resin that melts early is a poor choice for bulk loading and a good choice for small functional additions. That is consistent with the 宝金香 proportion — a share large enough to colour, small enough not to dominate the burn.

There is a second-order effect on aroma. Because shellac releases little scent of its own, its main sensory contribution at the coal is textural: a slight sweetness, a waxy roundness, and a marginally slower burn in the immediate vicinity. Anyone expecting a resinous incense note from it will be disappointed. Anyone using it to modify how a stick carries heat will find it does exactly that.

## The classical record

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* records lac under 紫鉚 in its insect section and describes its insect origin accurately. The Ming *Xiangcheng* lists 紫矿 among the ingredients of 宝金香. Both are historical records of use, not chemical analyses.

The *Bencao Gangmu* passage is worth quoting because it gets the biology right centuries before entomology formalised it: "蚁于海畔树藤皮中为之……正如蜂造蜜也" — insects make it in the bark of trees and vines by the sea, just as bees make honey. The analogy is exact in the important respect: the product is manufactured by the insect, not exuded by the plant.

The *Xiangcheng* entry is a formula, not a description. It gives quantities, and quantities are the most reliable thing a historical recipe can offer. We know lac was used at 二钱 against 一两 each of agarwood and sandalwood. We do not know, from the text, what role the compiler intended. That gap is where careful writing has to stop and say so.

What the record does establish:

- **Origin.** The insect source was understood, not discovered later.
- **Use.** Lac appears in a real incense formula, at a stated weight.
- **Value.** The 紫 names record a dye trade, which is the economic context for the material's presence in a workshop.
- **Terminology.** Several names coexisted, which is the root of the modern identification problem.

What it does not establish: any therapeutic effect, any quality grading system, or any specific technological role beyond colour. Those are later inferences, and they should be attributed as such.

## Evidence, and where it stops

> **Direct answer:** The insect identity, the classical citations, and the processing sequence are documented. The functional interpretation of the formula is inference. No health claim is made, and none should be inferred from the material's presence in a historical recipe.

| Layer | Status |
|---|---|
| Organism (*Kerria lacca*, Kerriidae) | Established |
| Material class (insect-derived resin) | Established |
| Classical citations (本草纲目, 香乘) | Historically documented |
| Dye use | Historically documented |
| Functional role in 宝金香 | Inferred from quantity and properties |
| Health or therapeutic effects | Not established; not claimed |

The distinction between the fourth and fifth rows is the one most often blurred. That lac was used as a dye is documented in the material's own naming history. That it acted as a binder in a specific formula is a reasonable inference from its melting behaviour — and still an inference. Keeping those apart is not academic caution; it is the difference between describing a historical practice and inventing one.

## A note on the ethics of an insect material

> **Direct answer:** Lac is an animal product, and some readers will want to avoid it on those grounds. That is a legitimate position, and it does not require the material to be misdescribed as a plant resin in order to be respected.

Incense sits in an awkward place for anyone thinking about animal materials. The craft is overwhelmingly plant-based, which creates an expectation that everything in the ingredient list is botanical. Lac breaks that expectation, and so does beeswax, and so does ambergris in a different category. Readers who keep a vegan or ahimsa-conscious practice need accurate information more than they need reassurance.

The accurate information is simple: lac is secreted by an insect, harvested by cutting the twigs the insect has colonised, and processed into resin. There is no way to describe that as plant-derived without misstating it. A page that softens the origin to make the material more palatable is not being kind to the reader; it is removing the fact the reader needs in order to decide.

What follows from that is a matter for the individual. Some makers will use lac because it is historically appropriate and functionally useful. Some will leave it out and accept that a formula is no longer the historical one. Some will substitute a plant resin and accept the aromatic change. All three are defensible, and none of them requires the material's origin to be blurred.

For this encyclopedia, the editorial position is that origin is a fact to state plainly, not a controversy to manage. The same standard applies to the smoke and particulate warnings elsewhere on the site: the reader is entitled to the full picture before making a choice.

## Handling, storage, and safety

> **Direct answer:** Keep lac dry, cool, and sealed; it softens with heat and can pick up smells. Handle powder with gloves, keep alcohol away from flames, and burn any finished incense in a ventilated space.

Practical points:

- **Heat.** The resin softens at a low temperature, so a warm storage room is worse than a cool one. A bag left in a hot vehicle can fuse into a single lump.
- **Moisture.** Dry resin is stable; damp resin can cloud and clump. Seal it, and let cold material come to room temperature before opening to avoid condensation.
- **Odour pickup.** A waxy resin absorbs surrounding smells. Store it away from strong aromatics, or the next batch will carry a hint of whatever was in the cupboard.
- **Dust.** Ground lac is a fine powder and behaves like one. A mask is sensible when weighing quantities.
- **Alcohol.** Shellac dissolves in alcohol, and alcohol is flammable. Anyone experimenting with a dissolved binder should do it away from flame and away from the burning bench.
- **Skin.** Some people report skin sensitivity to shellac; gloves solve it and cost nothing.
- **Combustion.** Incense smoke contains particulate matter. Burn in a ventilated space and never leave a burning stick unattended.

There is no basis for presenting lac as a health material. It is a historical dye and formula ingredient, and the safety notes above are the ordinary ones that apply to any resin powder and any burning incense.

## FAQ

**Is 紫草茸 a plant material?**
No. It is the resinous secretion of the lac insect *Kerria lacca*, a scale insect of the family Kerriidae. The host tree supplies sap, but the resin itself is manufactured by the insect. That is why the material is classified as animal-derived even though it is harvested from twigs.

**Why does the *Bencao Gangmu* call it 紫鉚?**
Names in classical materia medica were not standardised across regions and periods. 紫鉚, 紫矿, 赤胶, 紫梗, and 紫草茸 all refer to lac, with the 紫 element recording its value as a red dye. The variation is a record of trade and local usage rather than of different materials.

**How much lac is in 宝金香?**
Two 钱 against one 两 each of agarwood and sandalwood, plus one 钱 each of frankincense and benzoin. If the Ming ratio of ten 钱 to the 两 is assumed, that is about 8.3% of the blend by weight — a functional share rather than a trace.

**Is shellac the same as lac dye?**
No. Lac dye is the water-soluble red colourant washed out of the crude resin; shellac is the resin left behind, melted and purified. They come from the same insect and the same harvest, but they are different products with different solubilities and different uses.

**Does lac make incense smell better?**
Not really. Its aroma is waxy and faint, and at formula level it contributes texture more than scent. The reason it appears in classical recipes is functional — colour, and possibly binding — not aromatic. A maker looking for a red resin with a strong smell is looking for dragon's blood, not lac.

**Can shellac be used as a binder in pressed incense?**
It can be dissolved and used as one, and its low melting point makes it behave differently from a gum binder. The catch is that a resin film can reduce the porosity of a stick and interfere with the burn if the dose is high. Treat it as a small functional addition rather than a primary binder, and test the burn before scaling up.

## Summary

Shellac — 紫草茸, classically 紫鉚 and 紫矿 — is the resinous secretion of the lac insect *Kerria lacca*, and it belongs to the small group of animal materials used in an otherwise plant-based craft. The *Bencao Gangmu* described its insect origin accurately, and the Ming *Xiangcheng* lists it in the 宝金香 formula at 二钱 against 一两 each of agarwood and sandalwood. Read as weights, that is roughly one twelfth of the blend: a functional share large enough to colour a paste, small enough not to govern the burn. Its role in incense was colour and structural support rather than fragrance; its aroma is waxy, faint, and easily overlooked. The persistent confusion is with dragon's blood, a plant resin of similar redness but different origin and a much stronger scent. Buy it by identity and purity, keep it cool and dry, and treat its classical record as documentation of use rather than as evidence of effect.

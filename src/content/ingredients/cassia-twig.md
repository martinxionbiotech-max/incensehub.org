> **Direct answer:** 桂枝 (guìzhī), cassia twig, is the slender young branch of *Cinnamomum cassia* — the laurel-family tree whose mature bark is sold as 桂皮. It smells warm, sweet, and woody, with the cinnamon family's spice but a lighter, greener body than the bark. In Chinese practice it is a materia-medica part first and an aromatic accent second, and almost every question about it turns on one fact: it is a different part of the same tree, not a different grade of the same material.

The confusion this page exists to settle is not botanical. Anyone can see that a twig is not a piece of bark. The confusion is commercial and linguistic: two parts of one tree travel under related names, appear in the same catalogues, and get translated into English as though "cassia" were one thing. A buyer who orders "cassia" and receives a bundle of sticks has not been cheated — they have been given a real material with a real use — but they have not received what a cinnamon-bark specification describes, and the difference will show up in the censer.

## Twig and bark: the distinction that carries the page

**Direct answer:** 桂枝 is the young branch; 桂皮 is the mature bark. They come from the same species and behave like two different materials — one light, woody, and green-edged, the other dense, resinous, and sharply pungent. Neither is a better version of the other.

Think of the tree as a factory with two product lines. The bark is the outer casing, and it is where the pungent aromatic load concentrates: thick, oily, and heavy in the hand, it is the material that gives cassia its reputation for brute warmth. The twig is the young shoot, mostly wood with a thin skin of bark, and it carries the same aromatic family in a much lower concentration, wrapped in a green, sappy, woody matrix.

That structural difference produces a sensory difference you can demonstrate in ten seconds. Snap a piece of bark and the smell arrives before you have finished the motion — sharp, sweet, almost hot. Snap a twig and the smell is softer, slower, more like the inside of a pencil box than a spice rack. Both are recognisably cinnamon-family. Only one of them will dominate a room.

| | 桂枝 (twig) | 桂皮 (bark) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Young slender branch | Mature outer bark |
| Texture | Hard, fibrous, straight, with visible nodes | Curled quill, brittle, smooth outer surface |
| Aromatic load | Moderate; sits inside a woody matrix | High; concentrated in oily bark tissue |
| Scent shape | Light, green-woody, sweet-spicy | Dense, sweet-pungent, almost hot |
| Classical role | Materia medica | Spice, seasoning, aromatic material |
| Effect of over-dosing | Turns dry and twiggy | Turns harsh and burning |

The table's last row is the practical one. Both materials punish excess, but they punish it differently: too much twig reads as sawdust and a thin green bitterness, too much bark reads as heat and a scratch at the back of the throat. A blender who treats them as interchangeable will not get a milder or stronger version of the same effect; they will get a different failure.

## Reading the name: 桂, 桂皮, 肉桂, and the osmanthus trap

**Direct answer:** 桂 names the tree; 桂皮 is its bark; 肉桂 is also bark, usually the thicker medicinal grade; 桂枝 is the young twig. And in modern Chinese, 桂花 is osmanthus — an unrelated plant whose name shares the character. Context decides, and labels often fail to supply context.

This is where English-language sourcing goes wrong most often, and it goes wrong in three separate ways.

The first is the bark split. 桂皮 and 肉桂 both point at bark, and the distinction between them is a matter of grade and use rather than species: 肉桂 tends to name the thicker, more aromatic bark valued in pharmacy and cooking, while 桂皮 is the everyday spice name. A supplier may use either. Neither means the twig.

The second is the osmanthus collision. 桂花 — literally "桂 flower" — is *Osmanthus fragrans*, the sweet-scented ornamental whose flowers flavour tea and pastries. It has nothing to do with cinnamon. In a garden context, a tea context, or a place name, 桂 very often means osmanthus; in a spice or pharmacy context it means the laurel. A machine translation that renders every 桂 as "cinnamon" will produce nonsense in one direction or the other.

The third is the Western spice muddle. English "cinnamon" covers both *Cinnamomum verum* — true cinnamon, the thin, brittle, delicately scented bark — and *C. cassia*, the thicker, harsher Chinese cassia that dominates cheaper spice aisles. "Cassia" in a Western catalogue therefore usually means bark, not twig, which is exactly the mismatch that sends a buyer the wrong part.

| Term | Points to | Family | Common English rendering |
|---|---|---|---|
| 桂 | The laurel tree (in spice context) | Lauraceae | "cassia" / "cinnamon" |
| 桂花 | Osmanthus | Oleaceae | "osmanthus" — never cinnamon |
| 桂皮 | Cassia bark, everyday spice name | Lauraceae | "cassia bark" |
| 肉桂 | Cassia bark, thicker medicinal grade | Lauraceae | "cinnamon bark" |
| 桂枝 | Young twig | Lauraceae | "cassia twig" |

The lesson is narrow but worth internalising: in this corner of the materia medica, a single character is not a specification. The character after it does the work.

## The aroma, in the twig's own terms

**Direct answer:** Warm and sweet first, with a dry woody core and a green edge that the bark does not have. It reads like cinnamon heard from another room — the same family of notes at lower volume, with more wood and less heat.

The opening is sweetness rather than pungency. Where bark hits with a hot, almost peppery sweetness, the twig arrives softer, closer to the smell of a fresh-cut hedge with a cinnamon stick buried in it. Under that sits a dry woody body — the smell of the branch itself, which is most of what the material is — and at the end a faint green bitterness that reminds you this was a living shoot.

There is no camphor in this profile, and no resin. That distinguishes it from the other warm spices it gets grouped with: clove is sharper and more medicinal, ginger is brighter and more citrus-edged, and 白豆蔻 carries a cooling vapour that cassia never has. Cassia twig's contribution is weight without heat, which is a useful and slightly unusual thing in a blend.

I describe impressions, not chemistry. Anyone who tells you the exact oil content of a twig from a photograph is guessing; the honest method is to snap, smell, and compare against a retained sample. For how this register sits among the wider families, the [scent guide](/blog/scent-guide/) is the place to start.

## What the classical record says

**Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) treats 桂 in its wood section, separating the bark from the young twig, and quotes an older line — 桂枝之下無雜木, "no other wood grows beneath the cassia" — from the *Lüshi Chunqiu*. The record establishes identity and standing; it does not establish clinical outcomes.

Two things are being recorded there, and they are different in kind. One is a botanical and commercial observation: this tree yields several distinct articles, and the twig is one of them. The other is a claim about the tree's dominance over the ground it grows on — that nothing else thrives in its shade. The second is the kind of statement ancient texts make about impressive plants, and it should be read as repute rather than as ecology.

The twig's standing in the materia medica is not a minor footnote. It is the plant part named in one of the most studied classical formulas in the tradition, 桂枝汤, and that association is why the two characters are familiar to Chinese readers who have never opened a spice jar. Whether the formula does what the tradition says is a separate question, and not one this page answers.

Where the record stops matters as much as where it goes. It gives us a name, a part, a place in the pharmacy, and a literary reputation. It does not give us tested outcomes, doses in modern units, or a quality grade. Treating the first list as established and the second as absent is the only honest reading.

## Why the twig is not a lesser bark

**Direct answer:** It is not a downgrade, a by-product, or a cheap substitute. It is a different article with its own classical standing, its own aroma, and its own price logic. Calling it "cassia" and expecting bark behaviour is a category error, not a bargain.

There is a persistent assumption in Western sourcing that when one part of a plant is famous, the other parts are leftovers. Cassia twig is the counter-example. The bark is the famous spice; the twig is the part the pharmacy kept. Neither role is subordinate — they simply serve different purposes, and the trade prices them as separate commodities rather than as grades of one.

The practical significance is this: a buyer who wants bark's density cannot fix the problem by adding more twig. Volume does not convert one material into the other. Past a certain dose, more twig adds fibre and a sawdust note rather than more warmth. That is the boundary the marketing language hides. "Same tree" is true; "same material" is not.

Editorially, I would go further. The twig is the more interesting of the two for a blender precisely because it is quieter. A material that cannot shout has to be placed carefully, and placement is the craft. The bark forgives nothing and dominates everything; the twig asks you to think.

## From branch to bundle

**Direct answer:** Young branches are cut, cleaned, dried, and either kept whole or sawn into short lengths. There is no distillation and no extraction — the material ships as wood — and the two decisions that shape quality are how young the branch was and how evenly it dried.

The sequence is short enough to describe in a paragraph, and every step has a visible signature in the finished bundle:

1. **Cutting.** Shoots of the current or recent season are taken. Older, thicker branches still smell of cassia but the wood dominates and the aroma thins.
2. **Trimming and cleaning.** Side shoots and leaves come off; soil and grit are removed. Leaf left in the bundle is a sign of hurried work rather than a defect in itself.
3. **Drying.** The cut lengths are dried to a stable state, in sun or under cover depending on the producer. Slow drying in humid conditions is where mould and mustiness begin.
4. **Cutting to length.** Whole sticks are sawn or snapped into the lengths a buyer wants. Every cut exposes end-grain, and end-grain is where aroma leaves.

Two mechanism chains explain most of what goes wrong.

**The youth chain.** Young shoot → thin bark layer over a soft woody core → moderate aromatic load held in a small volume → drying removes water without driving off the volatiles → a light, sweet, green-woody stick. Take the same process to an older branch and the proportions shift: more wood, thicker bark, slower drying → a heavier stick that smells of the tree rather than of the spice.

**The cut-end chain.** Sawn bundle → dozens of exposed end faces → volatile loss concentrated at every cut → the bundle smells wonderful in the warehouse and flat in the workshop three weeks later → sealed storage slows it, but nothing reverses it. This is why a bundle that was cut long before shipping can arrive structurally perfect and aromatically tired.

## A grading frame for a material that has no grades

**Direct answer:** There is no formal ladder for cassia twig. What experienced buyers do is check five things in a fixed order — part, age, bark condition, dryness, aroma — and reject on the first failure rather than averaging across the list. The table below is our editorial version of that sequence.

I should be explicit about its status. This is a decision aid we assembled from the physical variables that predict behaviour, not a standard used by any authority. A supplier quoting "Grade A cassia twig" is using a house label.

| Band | What you see | What you smell | What it means in use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong | Slender uniform sticks, thin intact bark, pale tan, clean snap | Sweet-spicy, woody, arrives quickly on snapping | Reads clearly at low dose; behaves predictably |
| Serviceable | Mixed calibres, some bark rub-off, a few thicker pieces | Softer, sweeter, slower; needs crushing to open | Works as a background accent; expect dose drift |
| Thin | Branchy, knotty, much thicker wood, greyish surface | Dusty, faint, or green-sappy with little sweetness | Adds fibre more than aroma |
| Reject | Mould, dark patches, damp feel, insect holes, grit | Musty, sour, cellar-like | No formula adjustment rescues it |

The order matters more than the labels. Part first — if the bundle is bark quills rather than sticks, nothing else on the list applies. Age second, because a thick old branch cannot be rescued by good drying. Condition third, dryness fourth, aroma last, because aroma is the only check that requires you to destroy a piece, and you want to have eliminated the cheap failures before you start snapping sticks.

A boundary worth stating: the "strong" band is not automatically the right buy. If the twig is a background note at a low percentage, the difference between strong and serviceable disappears inside the base, and the premium buys nothing you can smell.

## Substitution: when the formula wants cassia and you have the wrong part

**Direct answer:** Bark can stand in for twig if you cut the dose hard, because it is far more concentrated. Twig cannot stand in for bark at any dose, because the missing intensity is not a quantity problem. True cinnamon bark sits between them — finer, thinner, less pungent, and a different species.

Substitution in this family is about direction, not equivalence. Each move changes what the blend says, and the useful question is what you are willing to lose.

| You have | Standing in for | What it brings | What it costs | How to adjust |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 桂皮 (cassia bark) | 桂枝 | The same spice family at several times the intensity | Heat, harshness, loss of the green-woody edge | Use a small fraction of the twig dose and build up slowly |
| *C. verum* bark (true cinnamon) | 桂枝 | Finer, more delicate sweetness, less brute pungency | A different species; thinner, quieter, less "cassia" | Increase slightly; expect a more floral finish |
| 桂枝 | 桂皮 | Light woody warmth, gentle sweetness | Not enough density to carry a lead role | Use only as a support note, never as the main warm material |
| [Clove](/ingredients/clove/) | 桂枝 | Stronger, sharper warmth | Loses sweetness; adds a numbing edge | A trace only; clove overwhelms fast |
| [Ginger](/ingredients/ginger/) | 桂枝 | Bright, citrus-leaning heat | No bark sweetness, no woody body | Use for lift, not for weight |

The pattern: everything in this family can add warmth, and almost nothing can add the specific combination of sweetness plus woody restraint that the twig supplies. That combination is why it works as a fixer rather than a feature. When a blend feels thin at the bottom but harsh at the top, a little twig can add body without adding heat — a narrow trick, and a genuinely useful one.

## What it can and cannot do in a blend

**Direct answer:** It can add woody-sweet weight to a base, soften a blend that has become too sharp, and lend a cinnamon-adjacent note without the bark's aggression. It cannot carry a formula, and it cannot supply the density that makes cassia recognisable.

Three chains describe its behaviour in a burning stick.

**The support chain.** Heavy resin base → blend reads flat and sweet → a small charge of twig adds dry woody structure → the base gains shape without gaining heat → the whole formula reads more expensive than its parts. This is the twig doing the job the classics assigned it: an assistant, not a chief.

**The scorch chain.** Fibrous wood → burns hotter at the surface than a resin-rich material → fine powder packed tight → the twig fraction chars before it volatilises → acrid, ashy note appears in the middle of the stick. Coarser grind and a looser pack fix it more reliably than lowering the dose.

**The dilution chain.** Thin aromatic load in a woody matrix → a high dose is needed to be smelled → the dose brings fibre and bulk → the stick's burn changes before its scent does → the blender keeps adding and the blend gets worse. This is the trap of the quiet material. If the twig is not reading, the answer is usually a different material, not more twig.

## Where it sits among the warm materials

**Direct answer:** It is the quietest member of the cinnamon group and the least resinous of the warm spices. Placed against bark, clove, ginger, and cardamom, its role is structural warmth — body without heat — which is a narrower job than any of the others do.

A cross-map is more useful than a ranking here, because these materials are not competing for the same slot. What follows is editorial: a working sense of each material's shape, not a measured comparison.

| Material | Heat | Sweetness | Wood / resin body | Typical slot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 桂枝 (cassia twig) | Low | Moderate | High, dry wood | Support, body, background warmth |
| 桂皮 (cassia bark) | High | High | Moderate, oily | Lead warm note, or a strong accent |
| True cinnamon bark | Low–moderate | High, fine | Low | Delicate warm top note |
| [Clove](/ingredients/clove/) | Very high | Low | Low | Sharp accent, tiny doses only |
| [Ginger](/ingredients/ginger/) | Moderate | Low | Very low | Bright lift, citrus-leaning |
| [Cardamom](/ingredients/cardamom/) | Moderate | Low | None | Pungent top note with a cool edge |

Read down the "body" column and the twig's position becomes clear: it is the only material in the group that supplies substance without supplying heat. That is why it works where the others fail — a blend that needs weight at the bottom but is already too hot at the top. It is also why it disappoints anyone who buys it expecting cinnamon.

## Grinding, sieving, and the fibre problem

**Direct answer:** Twig grinds to a fibrous, slightly stringy powder that sieves poorly compared with bark or resin. The fibres bridge across sieve openings, the fine fraction is dusty, and the coarse fraction is woody. Expect to lose more to the sieve than with a resin, and expect the powder to pack differently.

Practical consequences follow. A twig-heavy powder needs longer milling and a coarser screen; a very fine grind produces a dust that hangs in the air and coats the workshop. Because the material is wood, it also absorbs and releases moisture more slowly than a fine resin powder, so a blend containing a lot of twig dries at a different rate from one that does not.

For anyone building a formula around this material, the sensible approach is to treat it as a structural additive first and an aromatic second. Ask what it does to the burn and the pack; the scent contribution is small enough that it should not be the reason the material is in the recipe.

## Storage, and the cut-end problem

**Direct answer:** Keep the bundle dry, sealed, dark, and away from strong-smelling goods. Whole sticks hold their character far better than cut lengths, and cut lengths hold it better than powder. The enemy is air at the cut faces and moisture everywhere.

A bundle stored in paper in a humid room will grow mould before it loses its smell; a bundle stored in a sealed bin in a dry room will keep a workable aroma for a long time. Between those extremes, the variable that matters is the number of exposed ends. Buy whole sticks where you can, cut them when you need them, and keep the offcuts sealed rather than loose on a shelf.

The same logic applies to the working bench. A jar of cut twigs left open during a shift is not a problem; a jar left open for a month is a different material. Small containers, refilled from a sealed reserve, are the boring answer that works.

## The coumarin question

**Direct answer:** Coumarin is a documented constituent of cassia bark and a reason some markets cap cassia content in food. We have no measurement for twig material here and make no claim that the twig matches the bark on this point. If coumarin matters for your market, test the lot you are using.

This is a place where honesty costs something and is still the right call. The general concern about cassia and coumarin is real and well documented for bark; extrapolating it to the twig in either direction — "it must be safer" or "it must be the same" — is speculation. The material is a different part of the plant, and the only defensible statement is that the buyer should test what they buy if a regulatory limit applies.

For incense, which is burned rather than eaten, the relevant exposure route is smoke, and the safety section below covers that. For any use that involves ingestion, the answer belongs to a qualified professional and a laboratory, not to a page like this one.

## What to check before you buy

**Direct answer:** Confirm the part, then the age, then the condition. A clean, dry, slender, sweet-smelling young twig is the target; a thick, dull, musty stick is not made better by a good price.

- **Part.** Sticks with nodes and a woody core are twig. Curled quills are bark. Mixed bundles are a warning about the supplier's sorting, not a bargain.
- **Calibre.** Uniform, slender sticks indicate young shoots. Thick, irregular pieces mean older wood.
- **Bark condition.** A thin, intact, pale tan skin is what you want. Heavy rub-off means handling loss and a duller material.
- **Snap test.** A clean snap with a visible fibrous core is normal. A soft or spongy stick has taken up moisture.
- **Smell after breaking.** Sweet-spicy and woody, arriving within a second or two. Faint, dusty, or musty means the lot is old or badly stored.
- **Cleanliness.** Grit, leaf fragments, and insect holes are reject signals.
- **Paperwork.** Species name, part, and lot reference. "Cassia" alone does not say which part or which species.

A supplier who can tell you the part, the species, and the harvest window is describing a material. One who can only say "cinnamon sticks" is describing a shelf.

## Supply and the absence of a scarcity story

**Direct answer:** *Cinnamomum cassia* is cultivated across southern China and Vietnam and is not CITES-listed. There is no wild-harvest crisis attached to the twig, and the sourcing question is therefore specification and consistency rather than conservation.

That is worth saying plainly because the aromatic-materials world is full of scarcity narratives, some true and some commercial. Cassia is a plantation crop feeding a mass market in spice and pharmacy; the twig is a secondary article from the same trees. Nobody is stripping a forest to supply it.

The leverage a buyer has is unglamorous: agree the part and the calibre, keep a retained sample, and buy from someone who will hold the specification across repeat orders. That is the whole game with a material like this. The broader framework is in the [guide to buying at wholesale](/blog/wholesale-guide/).

## Safety

**Direct answer:** The hazard is smoke and hot material, not the twig itself. Burn in ventilated space, keep the burner clear of anything flammable, and do not treat a food spice as harmless when it is on fire.

Incense of any kind produces particulate matter, and a small room with a lit stick concentrates it. Ventilate, avoid inhaling from the source, and keep children and pets away from the burner. A twig-based incense tends to be dry and fibrous, which means it can burn a little hotter and faster than a resin-heavy stick — worth knowing when choosing a holder.

Nothing here is medical advice, and no therapeutic claim is made for burning this wood. The wider [safety notes](/safety/) cover smoke, ventilation, and household considerations in more detail.

## Summary

桂枝 is the young branch of *Cinnamomum cassia*, the same laurel-family tree that gives 桂皮. Its scent is warm, sweet, and woody rather than hot and resinous, because the aromatic load sits in a thin bark layer over a woody core. The classical record places it in the materia medica and separates it from the bark; the trade places it as a supporting note rather than a lead. Its whole specification is part-level precision — twig, not bark — and its practical value is the one thing the bark cannot do: add cinnamon-family weight without adding heat.

**Key takeaways**

- 桂枝 and 桂皮 are different parts of one tree, not grades of one material.
- 桂花 is osmanthus and has nothing to do with cinnamon; the shared character misleads translations.
- The twig is quieter, greener, and woodier than the bark, and volume cannot convert one into the other.
- There is no formal grade; part, age, condition, dryness, and aroma are the working checks.
- Coumarin is documented for cassia bark, not established here for twig — test the lot if a limit applies.

## FAQ

**Is 桂枝 the same as 肉桂?** No. 肉桂 is bark — the thick, aromatic inner bark of the cassia tree, the material sold as cinnamon in much of the world. 桂枝 is the young twig. They share a species and a name family, and they differ in part, texture, aromatic intensity, and classical use. A recipe or specification that says one and receives the other has a problem no amount of adjusting will fix.

**Does cassia twig smell like cinnamon?** It smells like the same family at lower volume: sweet and spicy at the front, woody and slightly green underneath, without the bark's heat. If you want the smell people mean by "cinnamon", you want bark. If you want a woody sweetness that sits behind other notes, the twig is the more usable material.

**Can I grind cassia twig for incense?** Yes, but expect a fibrous powder that sieves poorly and packs differently from a resin. Mill it longer, screen it coarser, and treat it as a structural additive that carries a little aroma rather than as an aromatic that happens to be wood. Very fine twig dust is unpleasant to work with and easy to over-disperse in the air.

**Why is the classical line about "no other wood beneath the cassia" quoted so often?** Because it is memorable and it flatters the tree. The line comes from the *Lüshi Chunqiu* and is repeated in the *Bencao Gangmu*; it expresses the plant's reputed dominance rather than a botanical observation. Read it as reputation, not ecology, and it is a useful window into how the tradition thought about the tree.

**Is cassia twig used in Chinese cooking?** The bark is the cooking spice. The twig belongs to the pharmacy and, occasionally, to aromatics. Some households do add a stick to a braise, but the material's reputation in the kitchen rests on 桂皮. Confusing the two in a recipe produces a weaker, woodier result rather than a wrong one.

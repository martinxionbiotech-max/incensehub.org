> **Direct answer:** Lavender (薰衣草, xūnyīcǎo) is the flowering tops of *Lavandula angustifolia*, English lavender — a woody mint-family shrub from the dry hills of the western Mediterranean. Its scent is a soft floral sweetness carried by linalool and linalyl acetate, cooled by a green, camphoraceous base. In incense it is a modern crossover material, not a classical Chinese 香材, and this page describes it on those terms.

## Begin with the shrub, not the perfume

> **Direct answer:** Lavender is a woody perennial of dry, limy hillsides, and its oil is a chemical defence against heat and insects rather than a decorative extra.

Lavender is a small woody perennial, not a soft garden annual. It builds a low mound of narrow, grey-green leaves and sends up bare stems that end in a spike of tiny florets — the part the trade wants. The plant grows where summer rain is scarce and the ground is thin and alkaline, and that habitat explains most of its character: small, tough, resinous leaves; a heavy load of volatile oil held in glands on the calyx and leaf surface; a short, intense flowering window in early summer.

The oil is not decoration. A plant that lives on hot, exposed slopes uses volatile terpenes as part of its chemical defense, against insects and against heat stress. Those same compounds are what a perfumer smells. The chain is straightforward:

dry, limy hillside → small leathery leaves and dense surface glands → a concentrated, low-molecular-weight volatile load → a scent that reads clean and cool rather than sweet and heavy.

That origin matters for anyone buying the material. Lavender is a crop of poor soils and dry summers; pushed into rich, wet ground it produces leaf and flower bulk with a thinner, greener oil. The finest "fine" lavender in the trade is associated with higher, drier sites, and the difference is detectable in the glass even when the species is identical.

## The name is a provenance record

> **Direct answer:** 薰衣草 means "the herb that scents clothes" — a functional description of a European household use, not a classical Chinese plant name.

薰 (xūn) means to scent or fumigate. 衣 (yī) means clothes. 草 (cǎo) means herb. Put together, 薰衣草 says "the herb that scents clothes" — a plain, functional description of a plant used to perfume linen, sachets and drawers. The name is not a classical plant name with a textual lineage behind it; it is a modern descriptive coinage that happens to preserve the plant's actual Western use.

This is worth dwelling on because Chinese material names usually carry history. 沉香 (agarwood) names a resin and a trade. 檀香 (sandalwood) names a wood and a ritual. 乳香 (frankincense) names a resin and a route. 薰衣草 names a household job. The naming pattern is a small piece of evidence about how the plant arrived: as a useful foreign herb, not as a material in the 香谱 (xiāngpǔ, incense manuals).

A descriptive name is not a pedigree. The name tells you what the plant did in a European household; it does not tell you that the plant appears in Tang or Song incense lists, and it does not. Nothing in the name supports a classical Chinese origin, and inventing one would be a fabrication rather than an interpretation.

## Why lavender reads as both herb and flower

> **Direct answer:** Soft floral esters sit over a cool camphoraceous base, so the same oil reads as flower at a distance and as herb up close.

Most aromatics sit clearly on one side of a line. Rose and jasmine are flowers: rich, sweet, heavy, built on alcohols and esters that persist. Rosemary, sage and mint are herbs: green, sharp, camphoraceous, built on oxides and ketones that cut through the air. Lavender sits across the line, and that is its commercial identity.

The chemistry explains the straddle. Linalool is a soft, sweet-floral alcohol with a faintly woody tail; linalyl acetate is sweeter and fruitier, the "clean laundry" impression in the opening. Against them sit camphor and 1,8-cineole, the cool, medicinal, eucalyptus-adjacent pair that gives lavender its green edge, plus smaller amounts of terpinen-4-ol, lavandulol and other minor constituents that round the whole thing out.

linalool and linalyl acetate → a soft, sweet floral top that reads "clean" rather than "perfumed"; camphor and 1,8-cineole → a cool green counterweight; the two together → a note that smells floral at arm's length and herbal up close.

That dual reading is why lavender is such a useful blending material and such a difficult solo material. In a formula it can face either direction: it softens a harsh citrus, it lifts a heavy resin, it gives a floral bouquet a green stem. Alone, the same duality can read as soap.

## Three species, three different materials

> **Direct answer:** True lavender, lavandin and spike lavender are three separate crops, and species is the first question to ask and the main price driver.

The single most consequential thing to understand about commercial lavender is that the word covers at least three materials with different chemistry, different yields and different prices. They are related, and they are not interchangeable.

| Material | Species | Plant and site | Oil character | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| True lavender | *Lavandula angustifolia* | Compact, less productive, higher and drier sites | Sweet, soft, floral, moderate camphor | Fine fragrance, quality aromatherapy, sachets |
| Lavandin | *Lavandula × intermedia* | Natural sterile hybrid of *angustifolia* and *latifolia*; large, vigorous, lower ground | Stronger, harsher, distinctly more camphor | Soap, detergent, household fragrance, value lines |
| Spike lavender | *Lavandula latifolia* | Broad-leaved, lower altitude, hotter sites | Sharp, camphoraceous, more cineole, less sweet | Technical and household fragrance, blending base |

Lavandin is not a counterfeit. It is a genuine crop with a real agronomic advantage: it is bigger, yields far more oil per hectare, and resists the conditions that thin out true lavender. Its oil is more camphoraceous and less refined, which is exactly why it belongs in a laundry detergent and not in a fine floral accord. The problem is not lavandin's existence; the problem is lavandin sold under a label that says "lavender oil" with no species named.

Spike lavender is the third material and the one most often left out of consumer explanations. It is higher in camphor and 1,8-cineole than either of the others, sharper and more "medicinal" in the old sense of the word. Historically it was the workhorse lavender of cheap household products, and it still fills that role in industrial fragrance.

## What the still does to the flower

> **Direct answer:** Steam distillation separates oil from flower, and the cut and the temperature decide whether the result reads fresh or jammy.

Two commercial routes turn lavender flowers into a fragrance material, and they produce different things.

Steam distillation is the main route. Flowering tops are loaded into a still, steam is passed through the charge, the volatile fraction vaporizes with the steam, and the condensate separates into oil and water. The water layer — the hydrosol, sold as lavender water — carries a light, water-soluble fraction of the scent and is a product in its own right. The oil layer is the concentrate.

The cut matters. The earliest fraction of a distillation is lighter and richer in the small, volatile esters; the later fraction carries more of the heavier, waxier and sometimes camphoraceous material. A distiller can blend the cuts or separate them, and that decision shapes whether the oil reads "fresh and floral" or "sweet and slightly jammy". A long cook at high temperature pushes the oil toward the second reading.

Solvent extraction is the second route. Flowers are washed with a volatile solvent, the solvent is removed, and the result is a concrete — a waxy, dark mass — which is then washed with alcohol to yield an absolute. Lavender absolute is deeper, rounder and less "fresh" than the steam-distilled oil, with a hay-like and slightly honeyed quality. It is a perfumery material, not the clear, clean oil most people picture.

fresh flowering tops → steam or solvent → volatiles separated from plant wax and water → the cut and the temperature decide which fraction dominates → the oil's "freshness" is a process outcome as much as a field outcome.

## Dried flower, oil, absolute, water: four forms

> **Direct answer:** Dried buds, essential oil, absolute and hydrosol are four different materials, not four strengths of the same one.

| Form | What it is | How it smells | Where it belongs | Its limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dried buds | Whole calyces and florets, air- or oven-dried | Soft, dusty, sweet, much weaker than oil | Sachets, linen, loose incense blends | Fades within months; dust and mess |
| Essential oil | Steam-distilled concentrate | Bright, clean, floral-camphor | Perfumery, aromatherapy, stick finishing | Volatile; oxidizes; easy to overload |
| Absolute | Solvent-extracted concentrate | Deep, hay-like, honeyed, less fresh | Fine fragrance, rounder florals | Dark, viscous, expensive, not "clean" |
| Hydrosol | Distillation water | Faint, watery, green | Room sprays, linen water, cosmetics | Very low intensity; spoils without preservative |

The practical point is that "lavender" in a formula is under-specified until the form is named. A dried bud and an absolute are not the same ingredient at different strengths; they are different materials with different scent shapes. A buyer who orders "lavender" and receives an absolute has not been upgraded — they have been sent something that will darken a clear floral accord.

## What happens when lavender meets a burning stick

> **Direct answer:** Lavender's light esters flash off before the ember reaches them, so a stick gives a brief opening and then smoke unless a resin base holds the flower.

This is where most lavender incense writing goes wrong. It describes the scent of the oil and assumes the stick delivers it. It often does not.

An incense stick works by holding a smouldering ember at the tip. Heat drives volatiles out of the material just behind the ember, and those volatiles are what you smell; some of the material also burns, and that combustion produces its own acrid, carbonaceous notes. A material survives this process well when it is resinous, woody, or chemically heavy. Lavender is the opposite: light esters and monoterpene alcohols with low boiling points and high vapor pressure.

The consequence is a predictable failure chain:

light esters and monoterpenes → driven off during drying, blending and extrusion → little fragrance left in the finished stick → the maker raises the oil load or adds a surface fragrance dip → the oil migrates into the paper or the box, the stick looks damp, and the burn turns acrid → the customer smells a brief floral opening followed by scorched paper.

There are legitimate ways around it. Lavender can be carried on a resin base — a small amount of benzoin, labdanum or a soft balsam holds the volatile fraction and slows its release. It can be used as a finishing note applied to a stick that is already dry, accepting that the top will be short-lived. It can be built into a masala where wood powder, resin and herb are ground together, so the herb is protected inside the matrix rather than sitting on the surface.

What lavender cannot do is behave like sandalwood. It has no heavy, slow-releasing fraction to fall back on, so a stick that smells strongly of lavender in the box is usually a stick with a lot of surface oil, and surface oil is exactly what burns harshly.

## How to read a lavender offer

> **Direct answer:** Species, harvest, chromatogram and storage are independent signals, and strength on one does not cover weakness on another.

Quality in lavender is not a single ladder. It is a set of independent signals, and a strong showing on one does not cover a weak showing on another.

| Signal | What it tells you | What it does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Species named as *L. angustifolia* | The offer is at least aiming at true lavender | Whether the oil is actually from that species |
| Harvest year stated | The seller tracks freshness | Whether the oil was stored well since harvest |
| GC report with linalool, linalyl acetate and camphor | The chemical profile is plausible and consistent | Whether the sample matches the drum you receive |
| Pale yellow to colourless, clear oil | Consistent with a fresh, well-kept distillate | Nothing about species; lavandin is also pale |
| Dark or cloudy oil | Oxidation, old stock, or a heavy absolute cut | — |
| Price far below the market | Something has been substituted or diluted | — |

The single most useful document is a gas-chromatography report, because it shows the ratio of the main constituents rather than a marketing claim. True lavender oil sits in a recognizable window: high linalool and linalyl acetate, modest camphor. Lavandin pushes camphor and cineole up and the ester pair down. A "lavender" oil with a camphor-heavy profile is lavandin or a blend, whatever the label says.

A GC report is a fingerprint, not a certificate of quality. It can tell you the oil is probably what it claims to be; it cannot tell you the flowers were harvested at the right moment, that the still was clean, or that the drum was kept cool and full. Those are supply-chain facts, and they are established by visiting or by a trusted intermediary, not by a PDF.

## Judging a sample on the blotter

> **Direct answer:** Dip, wait, dilute and compare against a known reference; the dry-down is where a true lavender separates from a lavandin.

A smelling strip is the cheapest instrument in the trade and the one most often used badly. The protocol that produces comparable results is short.

Write the sample code on the strip before you dip it, not after — unlabelled strips are how two oils get confused. Dip about a centimetre, shake off the excess, and smell the wet end immediately for the top note, then again after a few minutes for the heart. Put the strip down and come back to it later; lavender's fate is decided in the dry-down, where the esters leave and the camphor stays.

The important comparison is against a reference, not against memory. Keep a small vial of a known true lavender and a known lavandin, and smell the unknown between them. The question is not "is this nice" but "which side of the line is this on". Camphor rises at the back of the nose and slightly numbs the front of it; a high-camphor oil announces itself within seconds, while a true lavender opens sweet and only turns cool after a minute or two.

Dilute before judging strength. A neat oil overwhelms the nose and flattens every difference into "strong". Ten parts carrier to one part oil on a strip gives a reading closer to how the material behaves in a formula, which is where it will actually be used.

Smell the strip at the end of the day as well. What remains is the fixative fraction — the part that will still be there in a stick or a sachet after the opening has gone. Lavender leaves very little behind, and that absence is a fact about the material, not a defect in the sample.

## Reading the label: what a claim can and cannot mean

> **Direct answer:** Most lavender label phrases describe an intention rather than a specification, and only the species name and the batch report can be checked.

| Label claim | A plausible meaning | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| "Pure lavender oil" | Single botanical species, no carrier | Species name and a full GC report |
| "French lavender" | Grown or distilled in France | Which of the two; origin claims are often about the brand, not the drum |
| "High-altitude" | Slower growth, finer oil in some vintages | Altitude stated in metres, not implied by a photograph |
| "Therapeutic grade" | Nothing standardized; a marketing phrase | Constituent profile, batch number, safety documentation |
| "Lavender 40/42" | Oil standardized to a fixed ester range | Whether you wanted a natural single-harvest oil instead |
| "Organic" | Certified under a named scheme | The certificate body and the scope of the certificate |

None of these phrases is meaningless, and none of them is a quality grade on its own. "Therapeutic grade" is the weakest of the set, because no authority defines it; the phrase describes an intention rather than a specification. "40/42" is the most misunderstood, because it is a genuine trade specification that says nothing about the flower, the harvest or the distiller — it says the oil has been adjusted to hit a number.

A buyer who wants the character of a place and a season should ask for a single-origin, single-harvest oil with a batch-linked GC. A buyer who wants a soap that smells the same in March and September should ask for the standardized grade. Both are legitimate purchases; they are simply different products, and the label should say which one is in the drum.

## Adulteration and the lavandin drift

> **Direct answer:** Lavender is stretched by species substitution, synthetic topping, carrier dilution and standardized trade grades, and each leaves a different trace.

Lavender oil is expensive enough to invite substitution and cheap enough that substitution is rarely prosecuted. The common patterns are consistent across the trade.

The first is species substitution: lavandin sold as lavender, or a blend of the two sold as "pure". The tell is the camphor note, which is louder and more forward in lavandin, and the GC ratio.

The second is chemical topping. Synthetic linalool and linalyl acetate are inexpensive and can be added to lift a weak oil toward the numbers a buyer expects. A naturally balanced oil has a spread of minor constituents around the two headliners; a topped oil is heavy on the headline pair and thin on the rest. This is why a GC report should list more than two peaks.

The third is dilution. Oil cut with a carrier — fractionated coconut oil, propylene glycol, a cheaper terpene stream — still smells of lavender and still passes a casual sniff. It fails in a formula, where the carrier leaves a greasy trace and the projection collapses.

The fourth is standardized trade grades. Large volumes of lavender oil are sold to a specification in which the linalool and linalyl acetate levels are adjusted to a fixed range, so that a soap factory can buy the same material year after year. That is a legitimate industrial product. It is not the same thing as a single-origin, single-harvest oil, and the two should not be priced or described as if they were.

The professional response is boring: name the species, ask for the harvest, ask for a full GC, buy a sample before a drum, and smell for camphor. A lavender that smells strongly of camphor and only faintly of flowers is not a subtle lavender; it is probably not true lavender.

## Where lavender earns its place in a formula

> **Direct answer:** Lavender works as an opening over structure — coumarin, citrus, chamomile or resin — and turns soapy when asked to carry a formula alone.

Lavender's blending behaviour follows from its dual nature. It is a top-to-heart material with a soft floral face and a green edge, and it works best when something else supplies structure.

The classic pairing is the fougère idea: lavender over a sweet, hay-like coumarin and a mossy base. Lavender gives the accord its clean, barbershop opening; coumarin and oakmoss give it a body that lasts. The combination dominated masculine perfumery for a century and still reads as "clean" to most noses.

The second pairing is lavender with citrus, especially bergamot. Both are top notes with a bright opening, but bergamot is sharper and more bitter, lavender softer and more floral. Together they make a cologne-style opening that is clean without being thin, which is why the combination shows up in room sprays and linen waters.

The third is lavender with chamomile. Both are soft, both are slightly apple-sweet, and the combination is a rounded, comforting floral with almost no sharp edges. It is a difficult pair to use in incense, because neither material has weight; as a sachet or a diffuser blend it works well.

The fourth is lavender with a resin — benzoin, labdanum, or a light frankincense. Here lavender is the opening and the resin is the floor. This is the pairing that makes lavender usable in a burnable format, and it is also the pairing that most cheap lavender sticks skip.

| Accord | Partner materials | What lavender does | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fougère | Coumarin, oakmoss, a light wood | Clean barbershop opening | Too much lavender; the accord turns soapy |
| Cologne | Bergamot, neroli, a soft musk | Softens the citrus bite | Both materials are top notes; nothing holds the base |
| Soft floral | Chamomile, a faint rose | Rounds and lifts | Overdosing; the blend goes flat and sweet |
| Resin base | Benzoin, labdanum, frankincense | Provides a clean opening over weight | Under-dosing; the resin swallows the flower |

## Lavender and rosemary: same family, different job

> **Direct answer:** The two share a plant family and a camphor note but belong to different genera with different oils, and they do not substitute for each other.

Lavender and rosemary are both Lamiaceae, both woody, both carrying a camphoraceous edge, and both are routinely described as "herbal". That is where the similarity ends in any practical sense.

Lavender is *Lavandula angustifolia*. Rosemary is *Salvia rosmarinus*, a different genus with a different oil profile: more 1,8-cineole and camphor, more pine and eucalyptus, less linalool, almost no linalyl acetate. The result is a green, savory, penetrating herb that belongs with roast lamb and with woody incense, not with soft florals.

The confusion is usually linguistic rather than botanical — both are "the purple-flowered herb in the Mediterranean garden" — and it becomes a sourcing problem when a buyer asks for "the herbal one" without a species name. Specify the binomial. It is the only unambiguous instruction in a trade where common names drift.

Spike lavender is the third member of the confusion set, and the one most often missed. It is closer to rosemary in character than to true lavender: sharp, camphor-forward, low in sweetness. If a "lavender" oil smells more like a medicine cabinet than a flower bed, spike or lavandin is the likely explanation.

## What lavender is not

> **Direct answer:** There is no classical Chinese incense record for lavender, and that absence is a fact about documentation rather than a judgment on quality.

Lavender is not a classical Chinese incense material, and the honest way to say that is plainly. It does not appear in the classical 香材 canon; it has no place in the 沉香, 檀香 and 乳香 lineage; and its Chinese name is a modern description rather than a classical designation.

That is a statement about documentary history, not about quality. A material does not become better by acquiring a false pedigree, and lavender does not need one. It is a genuinely useful aromatic with a distinctive profile, a large modern supply chain and a well-understood chemistry. Those are sufficient virtues.

The failure mode to avoid is retrofitting. It takes several forms: translating a poetic classical plant name as "lavender" because the description sounds plausible; reading a modern Chinese cosmetic use as evidence of an old incense use; or citing "ancient aromatherapy" as if the category existed in the classical period. None of these survive a check of the source text, and each one costs the writer credibility on the materials where the classical record is real.

A reader should also be careful with the reverse move — dismissing lavender because it is not classical. Chinese incense practice has never been sealed. It absorbed materials along trade routes for two thousand years, and it continues to absorb them. The accurate position is that lavender is a modern addition whose origin is Western, not that it is an illegitimate material.

## The modern supply chain

> **Direct answer:** Chinese lavender is a modern essential-oil crop concentrated in the Ili valley, and origin claims are far easier to make than to verify.

Commercial lavender is grown across southern Europe and, at scale, in China. The Chinese crop is concentrated in the Ili valley in Xinjiang, where the dry summers, cold winters and high light levels suit the plant; the local industry is a modern development built around essential-oil production rather than a survival of an old incense tradition.

That geography matters to a buyer for two reasons. The first is quality variation: the same species grown in different climates gives oils with different ratios, and a Chinese *angustifolia* oil will not be identical to a French one from a higher, drier site. The second is documentation: origin claims are easy to make and hard to check, and the further a drum travels from the still, the more the paper trail matters.

The crop also has a tourism and cosmetic dimension that complicates sourcing. Lavender fields photographed for visitors are not necessarily the fields that supply the oil; a brand built on a landscape is not the same thing as a distiller with a harvest record. Ask where the oil was distilled, not where the photograph was taken.

## Harvest timing, and why it shows in the oil

> **Direct answer:** Quality is decided in the last days before cutting and the first hours after it, and no chromatogram can show either.

The scent of a lavender field is a moving target. Oil content builds through the flowering period and begins to change as the flowers age: the fresh, ester-rich phase is brief, and a crop left standing too long gives an oil that is heavier and more camphoraceous. Growers therefore cut at the point where the spike is fully open but the lowest florets have not yet faded, and the window is measured in days rather than weeks.

Time of day matters too. Cutting in the heat of the afternoon loses volatiles from the moment the stem is severed; cutting in the cooler part of the morning, after the dew has lifted, keeps more of the oil in the flower. The cut material is then either distilled wet or dried first, and the choice changes the result: distillation of fresh material gives a brighter, more complete oil, while drying before distillation loses some of the lightest fraction and can push the oil toward a hay-like tone.

A long, slow transport from field to still is therefore a quality event, not a logistics detail. Flower that sits in a trailer for a day has begun to ferment and to lose its top notes before the steam ever reaches it. This is one of the reasons small distilleries can outperform large ones on quality even when both are working with the same species: the distance between the row and the retort is short.

For a buyer, this is the part of the story that a GC report cannot show. A plausible chromatogram is compatible with flower that was cut late, hauled warm and distilled tired; the numbers describe the oil, not the harvest. When a supplier can describe the cut and the still without consulting a brochure, that is a better signal than any adjective on the label.

## Choosing a format

> **Direct answer:** A sachet, a stick and a diffuser deliver three different experiences, and the stick is where lavender performs least like itself.

| Format | How lavender is present | What you actually smell | Best for | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sachet | Dried buds in cloth | Soft, dusty, sweet | Linen, drawers, small rooms | Fades in months; becomes decorative |
| Loose herb on charcoal | Dried buds over a heat source | Sweet, slightly scorched, thin | Ritual or experimental burning | Scorch dominates; little oil survives |
| Incense stick | Oil or powdered flower in a combustible base | Brief floral opening, then base and smoke | Blended sticks with a resin floor | Surface oil burns acrid; weak heart |
| Cone or backflow | Higher oil load, dense matrix | Stronger, heavier, smokier | Visual effect, short sessions | Smoke volume overwhelms the scent |
| Diffuser | Essential oil in a carrier | Clean, bright, closest to the oil | Room scenting, steady background | Overdosing; plastic damage; pets |
| Candle | Oil in wax | Soft, rounded, weaker than the oil | Ambience | Fragrance burns off at the melt pool edge |

The table is a reminder that "lavender incense" is not one product. A sachet, a stick and a diffuser deliver three different scent experiences from the same plant, and the stick is the format where lavender performs least like itself.

## Storage and the oxidation clock

> **Direct answer:** Lavender oil ages by oxidation, and a full, dark, closed bottle is the only storage rule that changes the outcome.

Lavender oil begins changing the moment it is exposed to air. The esters hydrolyze slowly, the terpenes oxidize, and the bright opening flattens into something heavier and slightly waxy. The process is not dramatic and it is not reversible; an old oil does not smell wrong so much as tired.

Three habits slow it down. Keep the bottle full: the air in the headspace is the reactant, so a half-empty bottle ages faster than a full one, and decanting into a smaller container is worth the trouble. Keep it dark and cool: light drives the same reactions, and a shelf above a stove is the worst place in the house. Keep it closed: the cap is not a seal against a warm room over a period of years.

Dried flowers run a different clock. They lose volatiles steadily from the moment they are dry, and a sachet that smells wonderful in the first month is usually a bag of scented dust by the end of the year. Vacuum sealing slows the loss; grinding the buds accelerates it, because every cut surface releases oil. That is why whole buds keep their scent longer than powdered flower, and why a stick made with powdered lavender smells stronger in the box than it will after a month on the shelf.

## Safety and the limits of the aromatherapy frame

> **Direct answer:** Smoke irritates the airway and concentrated oil is a skin and pet concern; the relaxation association is a traditional belief, not a clinical finding.

Incense of any kind produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn it in a ventilated space, keep the source away from anything that can catch, do not inhale concentrated smoke, and keep children and pets out of the burn area. This page is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice. See our [safety guide](/safety/) for the general handling notes.

Lavender essential oil has its own handling cautions. The oil is not a toy: applied neat to skin it can irritate, and oxidized oil is a more potent sensitizer than fresh oil, which is why old, half-empty bottles are the ones that cause reactions. Keep the bottle closed, cool and full, and dilute before skin contact.

Concentrated oils are also a known concern around cats, which metabolize several of the monoterpene alcohols poorly; a diffuser running in a closed room is not the same as a sachet in a drawer. Dogs are generally more tolerant, but the sensible rule for a household with animals is the same: ventilate, keep the bottle out of reach, and do not leave a diffuser running unattended in a small closed room.

The relaxation and sleep association that sells lavender is a traditional belief and a modern marketing convention, not a clinical finding. It is a reasonable description of what the scent is used for; it is not a claim about what the scent does. We make no medical claims for lavender, and the documented facts are narrow: botanical identity, the Lamiaceae placement, the main constituents, and a modern role in perfumery and home fragrance.

## FAQ

**What is lavender (薰衣草)?** The flowering tops of *Lavandula angustifolia*, a woody shrub in the mint family native to the western Mediterranean. The flowers carry a soft floral scent with a green, camphoraceous edge, and they are distilled for essential oil or dried for sachets.

**Is lavender a classical Chinese incense ingredient?** No. It has no place in the classical 香材 canon, and its Chinese name 薰衣草 is a modern description rather than a classical designation. It entered incense through Western perfumery and modern aromatherapy. See the [lavender incense explainer](/blog/lavender-incense/).

**Why does my lavender incense smell weak or soapy?** Usually because the volatile esters flash off before the ember reaches them, leaving a thin floral trace over a scorched base. A heavy surface oil load makes it worse, not better: the oil burns rather than evaporates, and the result reads soapy and acrid.

**Can I burn dried lavender buds directly?** You can, but the result is closer to scorched hay than to the scent of the flower. The buds carry little resin and no wood, so the heat destroys most of the volatiles before they reach you. A resin base or a sachet gives a better account of the material.

**What is the difference between English lavender and lavandin?** English lavender is *Lavandula angustifolia*, the true lavender, with a sweeter and more refined oil. Lavandin is *Lavandula × intermedia*, a sterile hybrid with a much higher yield and a louder camphor note. Both are real crops; they are priced and used differently.

**Is lavender the same as rosemary?** No. Lavender is *Lavandula angustifolia*; rosemary is *Salvia rosmarinus*. They share a family and a camphoraceous edge, but lavender is soft and floral while rosemary is green, piney and savory. See [rosemary](/ingredients/rosemary/).

**What does the name 薰衣草 mean?** "The herb that scents clothes" — 薰 to scent, 衣 clothes, 草 herb. It describes the European use of lavender in sachets and linen, and it is the clearest evidence that the plant's Chinese identity is modern and functional.

## Summary

Lavender (薰衣草) is *Lavandula angustifolia*, a dry-hillside shrub in the mint family, and its value as a fragrance material comes from a chemical straddle: soft floral esters over a green, camphoraceous base. The commercial picture is a three-way split between true lavender, lavandin and spike lavender, and most quality problems reduce to which of the three is in the drum. Its Chinese name is a functional description of a European household use, not a classical designation, and the plant has no place in the 香材 canon. In incense it is a difficult material — volatile, short-lived, easily scorched — that works when a resin or wood base carries it and fails when it is simply loaded onto a stick.

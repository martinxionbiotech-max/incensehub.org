**Direct answer:** 辛夷 (xīnyí) is the dried, still-closed flower bud of a spring magnolia, classically *Magnolia biondii* — placed by Flora of China in the segregate genus as *Yulania biondii* — a small deciduous tree of central China. Incense makers use it as a green-spicy floral top note and as a scenting material for cloth and rooms. The word also covers buds of closely related magnolias, *Magnolia denudata* (玉兰) among them, so the trade name describes a plant part at a particular stage more than it names a single species.

A flower bud is an awkward thing to sell. It is small, seasonal, easily crushed, and it loses the quality that makes it worth buying — the closed, oil-rich stage — the moment it opens. That fragility explains most of the practical rules on this page, from the narrow harvest window to the advice to buy whole and grind small. It also explains why 辛夷 is one of the few incense materials whose specification is a *date* as much as a species.

What follows treats 辛夷 as a working material: what the bud is botanically, how the name behaves in the trade, how the aroma is built, where it sits in a blended formula, and which claims about it can be supported. The traditional record is included, but it is kept in its own section and labelled for what it is.

## What the bud actually is

> **Direct answer:** It is a complete flower, folded. The bud contains the whole perianth in miniature, held closed by overlapping tepal scales and a hairy outer bract, and the aromatic tissue is concentrated in those scales rather than in a separate oil sac or resin duct.

Flora of China describes *M. biondii* as a tree of roughly twelve metres, flowering on bare wood before the leaves expand. The flowers carry nine tepals in three whorls: the outer three are sepal-like and purplish-red, while the inner ones are white, often flushed purplish-red at the base. A bud therefore holds colour as well as scent, and the dried drug shows both — a purplish-brown outer surface and a paler interior where a bud has been broken open.

The outer bracts carry a fine, silky hairiness. Growers and graders read that surface as a stage marker: tight, dull, densely haired buds are young; buds that have begun to gloss and swell are close to opening. Nobody needs a microscope to sort a sack of 辛夷, but the eye is doing real work, and it is doing it on a feature that disappears within days.

Botanically the plant is a magnolia of the classic sort — primitive in structure, beetle-pollinated in its native range, and dependent on a short window in which flowers precede leaves. None of that matters to a blender directly. What matters is the consequence: the aromatic material is a *reproductive* organ built to attract, which means it is built to release scent into air, and it does so faster than wood or resin ever will.

## Naming: one word, several plants, four different parts

> **Direct answer:** 辛夷 is a category name for dried spring-magnolia buds. It is not a synonym for the garden flower 玉兰, not the same material as 白兰花 (champaca), and definitely not 厚朴, which is bark from a different magnolia.

Chinese material names often bundle a part, a season, and a use into one word, and 辛夷 is a textbook case. The word points at a bud, at the moment before opening, and at a pungent aromatic quality all at once. It says nothing about which magnolia the bud came from, and that silence is where buying mistakes begin.

| Name | Plant | Part | State | What it contributes | What it is not |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 辛夷 (xīnyí) | *Magnolia biondii*, also *M. denudata* and relatives | Flower bud | Dried, unopened | Green-spicy floral top note; scenting | Not the open bloom; not a single-species guarantee |
| 玉兰 (yùlán) | *Magnolia denudata* | Flower | Open bloom, fresh or dried | Fresher, lemon-green white floral | Not the bud material, though the same tree can supply both |
| 白兰花 (báilánhuā) | *Michelia alba* (syn. *Magnolia × alba*) | Flower | Open, usually fresh | Heavy, sweet, fruity white floral | Not a magnolia bud; a different genus and a much louder scent |
| 厚朴 (hòupò) | *Magnolia officinalis* | Bark | Dried bark | Bitter, woody, medicinal | Not a flower at all; a different part of a different species |
| 梅花 (méihuā) | *Prunus mume* | Flower | Open bloom | Cool, almond-tinged winter floral | Not Magnoliaceae; a different family entirely |

The 玉兰 pair is the one that catches people. The two names sit in the same family, and the bud of *M. denudata* can legitimately be sold as 辛夷, so a single tree can yield both a "bud" material and a "flower" material. The distinction is not botanical purity; it is part and stage. A buyer who wants the closed, oil-dense bud has to say so, because "magnolia flower" in a listing can mean either.

The 白兰花 confusion is a family resemblance problem. Champaca is a magnolia relative with white, heavy, fruity flowers, and it is far more powerful than 辛夷. Substituting one for the other at the same weight will not read as a subtle change; it will re-centre the blend. There is a separate entry for [champaca](/ingredients/champaca/) for that reason.

The 厚朴 confusion is the most consequential, because it is a *medicinal* bark with its own identity and its own cautions. A search for "magnolia" in a materia medica turns up both, and a buyer who orders on the English word alone can end up with the wrong part of the wrong species. The naming trap here is not a cosmetic one.

## Aroma: green at the top, spice in the middle, camphor at the edge

> **Direct answer:** 辛夷 smells like a magnolia bloom that has been pulled slightly towards citrus peel and camphor. The floral heart is soft and slightly waxy, the spice sits close behind it, and a cool, almost medicinal edge keeps the whole thing from turning creamy.

Three impressions arrive in sequence. First comes a fresh, green, slightly bitter-floral lift, the part that reads as "spring" rather than "perfume". Then the spicy-floral body, which is where the 辛 in the name earns its place. Last is the camphoraceous edge — not a blast of eucalyptus, more a cool shadow that makes the floral note seem thinner and brighter than a gardenia or a jasmine.

That profile places 辛夷 in a specific neighbourhood of the floral map. It is not a "white floral" in the heavy sense. It belongs with the green-floral materials, and its closest relatives in this encyclopedia are the open yulan flower and, at a distance, plum blossom. The table below sets out the registers so a blender can see what changes when one floral is swapped for another.

| Register | Materials in this encyclopedia | Behaviour in a blend | Common mismatch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green-spicy floral | 辛夷 (bud), 玉兰 (open flower) | Lifts the opening; adds air and a faint camphor coolness | Mistaken for a "light jasmine" and dosed too high |
| Creamy white floral | 栀子花 (gardenia), 白兰花 (champaca) | Fills the middle; sweet, waxy, persistent | Used as a substitute for 辛夷 and overwhelms the top |
| Cool winter floral | 梅花 (plum blossom) | Thin, almond-tinged, delicate | Assumed to be magnolia-family because it blooms in the same season |
| Warm spice | 丁香 (clove), 肉豆蔻 (nutmeg) | Pushes heat and sweetness into the base | Confused with the "spicy" in 辛夷, which is aromatic rather than hot |

The mechanism behind the top-note behaviour is simple volatility. A bud's aromatic tissue is thin-walled and close to the surface → the volatile fraction leaves the plant material as soon as it is warmed → the molecules reach the nose before the heavier, slower notes of woods and resins → the blend opens green and spicy → the base arrives a minute later and takes over. That sequence is why 辛夷 is described as a top note, and why a blend that smells strongly of 辛夷 in the tin can smell much less of it once lit.

The corollary is a common misconception worth stating plainly: a strong smell in the jar does not mean the material will survive a hot coal. Heat accelerates the loss. If anything, the loudest sample is often the one that has been ground recently and is spending its aroma fastest.

## Where it grows, and why the map matters less than the calendar

> **Direct answer:** *Magnolia biondii* is a mountain tree of central China, recorded from Shaanxi, Gansu, Henan and Sichuan, and its buds are collected in late winter or early spring before the flowers open. *Magnolia denudata* shares that central-Chinese range, which is why more than one species can reach the same market under one name.

The geography is compact. This is not a tropical plantation crop with a wide belt of origin; it is a temperate hill tree with a Chinese heartland, and the material has historically moved from those hills to the incense markets rather than the other way round. Origin labels exist, but for a material harvested from both wild and planted trees in overlapping provinces, origin is a weaker signal than stage and handling.

The calendar is the strong signal. Buds are picked in a narrow window at the end of winter, when the tree has finished its dormancy but has not yet committed to flowering. Pick too early and the bud is small, thin and short on aroma. Pick too late and the tepals have begun to separate; the bud looks full but is already spending its volatile oil on the flower it is about to become.

The chain runs like this: a warm spell in late winter → buds swell and the outer hairs part → pickers judge firmness by hand → buds are cut and dried whole → the closed shape survives drying → the aromatic tissue stays protected inside the scales. Delay the same picking by a week or two → the tepals loosen → the bud opens in the drying shed → the aromatic surface is exposed to air and heat → the finished material is flatter, browner and lighter in the hand. Same tree, same year, different grade. That is the whole commercial argument for harvest timing.

## Drying, storage, and what "fresh" means for a dried drug

> **Direct answer:** Freshness in 辛夷 is not softness or moisture; it is the amount of volatile oil still held inside an intact bud. Slow, gentle drying and a sealed, cool storage jar protect that; heat, light and crushing spend it.

A dried bud is a small package with a large internal surface. Drying has to remove enough water to prevent mould without cooking the aromatic fraction, and those two goals pull in opposite directions. Fast heat drives water out quickly and can leave the outer bracts hard while the centre is still damp; the bud then looks finished, seals itself, and develops mustiness weeks later in the jar. Slow shade drying takes longer but keeps the colour and the scent. This is a practical judgment rather than an official rule, and it is one of the places where a small producer can outperform a large one.

Storage follows the same logic as storage for any volatile aromatic. Glass or a well-sealed tin, out of sunlight, away from a warm stove, and filled as full as practical so that the headspace is small. A paper bag on a shelf is a slow leak. A plastic bag in a hot cupboard is a faster one.

The physical test is straightforward. Squeeze a bud gently: it should feel firm and slightly heavy for its size, not hollow or papery. Break one open: the interior should still carry the spicy-floral smell, not a hay or dust note. Colour should be a dull purplish-brown with pale tips, not uniform grey. None of these checks needs equipment, and together they catch most of what goes wrong between harvest and sale.

Powder changes the clock. Once the bud is ground, the same volatile fraction is exposed across a much larger surface, and the aroma that was locked into the scales begins to leave immediately. A sealed jar slows the loss; it does not stop it. This is why the whole bud is the storage form and the powder is the working form.

## Grinding and blending: the step that spends the aroma

> **Direct answer:** Grinding buys you an even blend and costs you aroma. The finer the powder and the longer the grind, the more heat and surface area are applied to a material whose value is volatile, so the practical rule is to grind small amounts, briefly, and use them soon.

A blade grinder or a mortar both work; a mortar is slower and heats less. The useful habit is to grind in short bursts and let the material cool between them, rather than running the machine until the powder is warm to the touch. Warm powder smells wonderful for an hour and flat for a month.

Particle size then shapes the burn. A very fine powder packs tightly, restricts air, and can smoulder or go out in a stick; a coarse powder leaves gaps, draws air through the stick, and burns hot and fast. The right middle depends on the binder and the rest of the formula, which is why a bench test on a small batch beats a rule of thumb. Roll one stick, dry it, light it, and watch the coal line: a steady crawl with pale ash suggests the pack is right, while a racing line with a dark, cracked cone suggests too much air.

There is a second, less obvious effect. 辛夷 powder is light and bulky, so it changes the bulk density of a mix. Adding two parts of bud powder to a formula can loosen a stick that was previously well behaved, and the fix is usually to adjust the binder or the proportion of a denser wood rather than to grind finer. Reading the mix as a physical system, not just a scent, saves a lot of failed batches.

## Sachet work: the use that suits the material best

> **Direct answer:** Whole 辛夷 buds are at their most convincing in a cloth sachet or a shallow dish, where they release aroma slowly at room temperature and never see a flame. No smoke, no scorched bracts, no binder — just a dry bud giving up its oil over weeks.

The Chinese 香囊 (xiāng náng) — a small cloth bag of aromatics — is an old form, and floral buds were made for it. The bag is porous enough to pass scent, dense enough to hold fragments, and small enough to live in a drawer, a wardrobe, a suitcase or beside a bed. The material works without heat because the volatile fraction leaves a dry bud at room temperature anyway; the sachet simply slows the loss and concentrates it in a small volume of air.

Building one is mostly a matter of restraint. Whole buds, lightly crushed between the fingers to break a few scales, fill the bag two-thirds full and leave room for the material to move. A tight pack crushes the buds into dust and releases everything at once; a loose pack lets air circulate and stretches the life of the fill. Some makers add a few whole cloves for warmth or a spoonful of a dry wood powder to slow the release, and both are reasonable as long as the floral note stays audible.

The physical cautions are mundane and worth stating. Plant material can mark pale fabric, particularly if the bag gets damp, so line the sachet or use a dark outer cloth. A sachet hung in a steamy bathroom will grow musty rather than fragrant. And a bag that has stopped smelling is not necessarily empty of oil — crush a bud and smell it before throwing the fill away; sometimes the surface has simply sealed itself and the interior is still good.

A shallow dish works the same way with less craft. Whole buds in an open bowl on a shelf scent a small room for a few weeks, and stirring them occasionally exposes fresh surface. This is the least wasteful way to use a grade-B lot whose buds are intact but whose aroma has faded at the edges: no heat to scorch them, no grinding to spend them, just slow release into still air.

## In the censer: how heat releases the bud

> **Direct answer:** 辛夷 gives its best reading under indirect heat, where the bud is warmed rather than set alight. On a live coal the outer bracts char before the interior warms, and the smoke from that charring covers the green-spicy note the material was bought for.

The traditional Chinese method for delicate aromatics is separated-fire heating — 隔火熏香 (gé huǒ xūn xiāng), literally "separated-fire incense" — in which a heat source is buried in ash and the material sits on a mica or ceramic plate above it. Nothing burns. The bud is warmed from below, its volatile fraction leaves in order, and the room receives the aroma without the combustion products that would otherwise sit on top of it.

The order of release is the interesting part. Gentle heat → the lightest, greenest molecules leave first → the opening reads fresh and slightly bitter → as the plate warms, the spicy-floral body arrives → the waxy, softer tail comes last and lingers on the plate. Raise the heat too quickly → the whole sequence compresses into a minute → the tail scorches → a burnt-sugar note appears that no amount of blending can hide.

Direct combustion is a different event. A coal reaches the bud's surface unevenly: the hairs and outer bracts are thin and dry, so they ignite first, while the dense centre is still cool. That gradient produces the bitter, papery edge that people describe as "cheap floral". It is not a defect in the material so much as a mismatch between the material and the method. If 辛夷 is going into a stick, the binder and the surrounding wood dust moderate the heat, which is one reason a well-built stick reads more cleanly than a crumbled bud dropped on a coal.

The practical conclusion is to choose the method before choosing the grade. For heating, a clean grade-A bud rewards the effort. For a stick, a solid grade-B lot blended with a denser wood will often outperform an expensive bud that is burned too hot.

## What a listing should tell you

> **Direct answer:** A usable 辛夷 listing names the species or species group, the part, the harvest year and the form. A listing that says only "magnolia flower" leaves the buyer to guess at all four, and the guess is usually wrong in at least one.

The four items are not bureaucracy; each one maps to a quality variable. Species sets the aromatic range and the legal trade name. Part separates buds from open flowers and from bark. Harvest year tells you how long the volatiles have been sitting in a warehouse. Form tells you whether you are buying a storage material or a working stock that is already spending its aroma.

What a listing often adds instead is atmosphere: mountain origin, wild harvest, hand-picked, ancient technique. Some of these may be true. None of them is checkable from a web page, and none of them substitutes for the four items above. A seller who can answer the plain questions is more informative than one who answers with a story, and a buyer who asks for a sample before committing learns more from one broken bud than from a page of description.

The same logic applies to substitutions. If a listing offers "magnolia flower" as a stand-in for 辛夷, the question to ask is not whether the family matches but whether the part and stage match. A bag of open 玉兰 blooms is a legitimate aromatic and a poor substitute for a closed bud, and the difference will show up in the first thirty seconds of a burn.

## Quality: a screening frame, not an official ladder

> **Direct answer:** There is no published commercial grading standard for 辛夷. What exists is a set of practical checks — bud integrity, stage, colour, aroma, cleanliness — that separate a usable lot from a tired one, and the frame below is editorial rather than a trade rule.

| Editorial grade | Bud condition | Colour | Aroma when broken | Foreign matter | Realistic use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Intact, closed, uniform, firm | Dull purplish-brown, pale interior | Clear green-spicy floral with camphor edge | Essentially none | Top-note work where the floral must read cleanly |
| B | Mostly closed; a few split or missing tips | Slightly faded or uneven | Present but softer; less camphor | Traces of stem or leaf | Blends where 辛夷 is a supporting accent |
| C | Open or shattered buds, many fragments | Grey-brown, dusty | Hay-like, weak, or musty | Visible twigs, leaf, grit | Scenting sachets, or reject for fine work |

Two notes on using the table. First, the grade names are ours; a supplier's "premium" means whatever that supplier decides it means. Second, grade is not the same as species. A clean, well-handled lot of *M. denudata* buds can be better material than a tired lot of *M. biondii*, and a label that names the more famous species does not upgrade the bud in the bag.

The checks that matter most, in order: is the bud closed, is it intact, does it smell when broken, and is it clean. Species comes fourth because it is a labelling question. A buyer who reverses that order — species first, condition last — is buying a story rather than a material.

## In a blended formula: assistant, not chief

> **Direct answer:** 辛夷 rarely leads a 合香 (hé xiāng, blended incense) formula. It works as an assistant or guide material — the 佐使 (zuǒ shǐ) role in the 君臣佐使 framework — where a small proportion lifts the opening and keeps a heavy blend from smelling flat.

The 君臣佐使 framework is a way of describing rank in a formula: the chief (君) sets the character, the minister (臣) supports it, the assistant (佐) modifies or balances it, and the guide (使) carries it through. It is a design vocabulary, not a laboratory assay, and it is most useful when a blender is deciding what a material is *for* rather than what it is made of.

In that vocabulary 辛夷 is a natural assistant. Its job is to add air at the start of the burn and a green edge to a sweet or resinous heart. Put too much in and the camphoraceous note stops being a shadow and becomes a smell of its own — the blend reads thin, medicinal, or vaguely soapy. Put too little in and it vanishes; the formula smells fine but loses the lift it was added for.

A useful way to think about proportion is by function rather than by number. Ask what the blend would lose if the material were removed. If the answer is "nothing", the dose is decorative. If the answer is "the opening goes flat and the sweetness becomes cloying", the dose is doing work. That test travels better between formulas than any fixed percentage, because the right level depends on the other florals, the binder, and whether the incense is a stick, a cone or loose material for a censer.

Floral buds are also used outside combustion. A cloth sachet or a shallow dish of whole buds scents a drawer or a small room slowly, without smoke, and this is the use that best matches the material's temperament. The aroma is quiet and continuous rather than bright and brief, and the buds can be refreshed by crushing them lightly in the hand.

## Substitution: match the register, not the name

> **Direct answer:** If 辛夷 is unavailable, the nearest substitute is another spring-magnolia bud; the next nearest is the open yulan flower, which is fresher and less spicy. Champaca and gardenia can cover the floral slot but will change the blend's weight, and plum blossom covers only the cool, thin quality.

| Substitute | Closeness | What shifts | When it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| Another *M. biondii* / *M. denudata* bud | Very close | Little; species differences are subtle at equal grade | Default replacement when the same part and stage are available |
| 玉兰 open flower | Close in family, different part | Fresher, more lemon-green, less camphor and less spice | Blends where the floral should read light rather than pungent |
| 白兰花 (champaca) | Distant | Much heavier, sweeter, fruitier; dominates the opening | Only where a creamy white floral is wanted anyway; reduce sharply |
| 栀子花 (gardenia) | Distant | Creamy, waxy, dense; pushes the blend towards the base | Where the formula needs a floral heart rather than a top note |
| 梅花 (plum blossom) | Distant | Cool, thin, almond-tinged; no camphor | Where the target is a winter-floral mood, not a magnolia one |

The rule underneath the table is that substitution is a register decision, not a name-matching exercise. Two materials can share a family and still behave differently in a burn because one is a bud and the other an open flower, or because one is volatile and the other is heavy. The blend does not care about the label; it cares about what evaporates first.

## What it will not do

> **Direct answer:** 辛夷 will scent a room and lift a blend. It will not treat a condition, replace medical care, or make smoke harmless, and this page makes no health claims for it.

Burning any plant material produces smoke and particulate matter, and the fact that the material is floral and pleasant does not change that. Ventilate the space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, keep burning incense away from children and pets, and do not leave a lit stick or cone unattended. The site's [safety guide](/safety/) covers the general case.

There is a second safety boundary specific to this material. 辛夷 appears in traditional Chinese medicine as a materia-medica drug, taken internally as a decoction. Incense use is not that use. Inhaling smoke from a dried bud is a different exposure from drinking a prepared decoction, and nothing about the aromatic history of the material should be read as advice to ingest it. The two contexts should be kept apart in the reader's mind, and in any buying decision that involves a medicinal claim.

Aromatherapy language is also worth resisting. A scent can be pleasant, memorable, or useful as a marker of a place and a mood without doing anything measurable to the body. Treating a pleasant smell as evidence of an effect is the most common error in writing about aromatic plants, and it is an error this encyclopedia tries not to make.

## The traditional record, read with the right lens

> **Direct answer:** 辛夷 is recorded in the 本草綱目 (Bencao Gangmu), in the wood section (木部), where it carries a traditional association with the nose and nasal passages. That association is traditional belief, not established clinical fact, and it does not transfer to incense.

The classical text is a record of what practitioners believed and prescribed, compiled from earlier sources. Reading it as a historical document is legitimate and useful: it tells you which materials were traded, how they were grouped, and what kinds of claims were made about them. Reading it as a source of tested efficacy is not legitimate, and the difference is the whole point of the evidence table below.

| Layer | Status | What it is based on |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical identity | Established | Flora of China and GBIF taxonomy for *M. biondii* / *Yulania biondii* |
| Aromatic use | Historically documented | Classical and trade records of 辛夷 as a scenting material |
| Traditional nasal association | Traditional belief | 本草綱目 木部, pre-modern materia medica |
| Clinical efficacy | Not established | No claim is made here; the page is not medical advice |
| Combustion safety | Established | General evidence on smoke and particulate matter from burning biomass |

The distinction matters for a reader who arrives from a search about 辛夷 as a remedy. This page is about the material as an aromatic, and its accuracy depends on not smuggling a therapeutic promise into a description of a smell. What can be said with confidence is narrower and more useful: the bud is a distinctive green-spicy floral, it has a long recorded history as a scenting material, and its quality is a matter of stage and handling rather than mystique.

## FAQ

**Is 辛夷 the same as the magnolia flower in a garden?** Only partly. Garden magnolias are usually grown for the open bloom — 玉兰 in the Chinese case — while 辛夷 is the dried bud picked before that bloom appears. The same tree can supply both, but the bud is a different material with a tighter, spicier, more camphoraceous scent. If a listing says "magnolia flower" without naming the part, ask which one is in the bag.

**Why does whole 辛夷 smell stronger than the powder I bought last month?** Because the whole bud protects its volatile oil inside intact scales, while powder exposes that oil across a large surface. Freshly ground bud smells loud for a few days and then flattens; a sealed bag of whole buds holds its character far longer. Buy whole, grind small amounts, and treat the powder as a working stock rather than a pantry staple.

**Can I burn the whole buds directly on a charcoal disc?** You can, and the result is uneven. The outer bracts char before the interior heats through, so the first minute is bitter and the middle of the bud may never release its oil cleanly. Whole buds do their best work in a sachet or a warm dish; for a censer, crush or grind them and blend with a material that carries heat evenly.

**Is 辛夷 the same as 厚朴 (magnolia bark)?** No. 厚朴 is the bark of *Magnolia officinalis*, a different species and a different plant part, and it belongs to a separate medicinal and commercial category. The two share a family and an English adjective, which is exactly how the confusion starts. An incense buyer who receives bark instead of buds has received the wrong material, not a variant of the right one.

**How much 辛夷 should go into a blend?** There is no universal figure, and any page that gives one is guessing. The workable approach is functional: start with a small proportion, burn a test stick, and ask whether removing the material would flatten the opening. If it would, the dose is doing work; if not, it is decoration. The right level depends on the other florals, the binder and the format.

## Summary

辛夷 is a dried, unopened spring-magnolia bud, classically *Magnolia biondii* and legitimately drawn from related species including *Magnolia denudata*. Its value is inseparable from its stage: closed buds hold a green-spicy, faintly camphoraceous floral oil that opens a blend and scents cloth and rooms, while opened or shattered buds are a lesser material under the same name. The trade name is a category rather than a species guarantee, which is why part, stage and condition deserve more attention than the label. Traditional records give the bud a long aromatic history and a nasal association that belongs to materia medica, not to incense; the verifiable claims are botanical, sensory and practical. Buy whole, keep it sealed and cool, grind small, and dose by function.

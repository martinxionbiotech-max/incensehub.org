> **Direct answer:** Aglaia is 米仔兰 (mǐzǎilán), *Aglaia odorata*, a small evergreen tree of the mahogany family grown across southern China and Southeast Asia for flowers no bigger than grains of rice. It is a garden plant rather than a censer material: the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) never lists it, and its perfume does not survive picking, drying or burning. That boundary is the reason it has a page here — a genuinely fragrant plant that is not a 香材, carrying a name that quietly misleads.

## Start with the name, because the name is the trap

> **Direct answer:** 米仔兰 translates roughly as "rice-grain orchid", and the plant is not an orchid at all. The 兰 in its name is a fragrance word applied across several unrelated plant families; every dependable identification of this material runs through the binomial *Aglaia odorata*, never through the common name.

The character 兰 does a lot of work in Chinese plant naming and almost none of it is taxonomic. It attaches to the orchids proper (兰花), to the yulan magnolia (玉兰), to champaca (白兰), to the small white-flowered shrub gardeners call 含笑, and to this plant in three or four regional variants. A reader who treats 兰 as a family label will conclude that all of these are relatives. They are not: the group spans Orchidaceae, Magnoliaceae, and — in this case — Meliaceae, three families that share a scent register and nothing else.

There is a historical reason the character is so loose. Commentators have argued for centuries about which plant the classical 兰 of the poetry actually names, and a respectable body of opinion reads it as a fragrant herb rather than an orchid. The disagreement is the useful part: 兰 was a word for something that smelled good, attached to plants by poets and gardeners long before botanists needed it to mean a particular flower. Treating it as a botanical claim is a modern misreading of an old aesthetic word.

The rest of the name is more literal. 米 is rice; 仔 is the southern diminutive suffix that turns a noun into "little thing"; 兰 supplies the fragrance. "Little rice flower" is an exact description of a shrub whose blossoms look like grains of polished rice scattered through dark green foliage. The name is precise about size and useless about identity, which is the usual bargain with garden names.

**Editorial judgment:** the naming trap here is not exotic. It is the ordinary one — a beautiful descriptive name outliving the need to be accurate. The correction is equally ordinary: ask for the binomial.

## What the plant actually is

> **Direct answer:** *Aglaia odorata* is a species in the genus *Aglaia*, family Meliaceae — the mahogany family, which also contains the timber mahoganies, neem and the chinaberry tree. It is a woody shrub or small tree with pinnate leaves and dense clusters of tiny yellow flowers, and it belongs nowhere near the orchids.

Meliaceae is a family most people meet through furniture rather than fragrance. Its commercial reputation rests on timber and on insecticidal seed compounds, not on perfume. Placing 米仔兰 inside it is a useful correction to the intuition that a sweet-smelling flower must come from a delicate herbaceous plant: this one comes from the same stock as cabinet woods. The family's botanical signature shows in the flowers, where the stamens fuse into a tube around the style — a structure that is characteristic of Meliaceae and quite unlike the column of an orchid.

The habit is woody and upright, eventually tree-like where winters are warm enough, and it holds glossy pinnate leaves through the year. The flowers are the entire point of the plant commercially and culturally; the wood is unremarkable, the fruit is small and ornamental at best, and nobody grows it for either. A mature specimen in flower reads as a green shrub dusted with yellow points, and the scent arrives before the eye finds the individual blossoms.

That combination — woody, evergreen, repeatedly flowering in warm weather, and fragrant at close range — is what made it a courtyard plant rather than a crop. A crop needs a harvestable part with enough mass and enough value per kilo to justify picking. A courtyard plant needs only to be pleasant where people sit. 米仔兰 was selected for the second job, and every later question about it follows from that selection.

## Where it grows and how it is kept

> **Direct answer:** The species is native to southern China and Southeast Asia and has been carried through the subtropical south as a garden and courtyard plant. It is not a scarce wild harvest; in cool-winter climates it lives in a container and is brought under cover for the cold months.

Its range runs through the warm, humid south — the sort of climate with mild winters, long summers and regular rain. In those places it is ordinary garden furniture: hedged, potted, planted by a gate, sometimes trained into a dense screen. The plant's tolerance of repeated warm-season flowering, rather than a single short bloom, is part of why gardeners keep it; the scent returns through the season instead of arriving once.

Move north or uphill into real frost and the relationship changes. The plant does not take hard cold, so it becomes a container specimen, summered outdoors and wintered under glass or in a sheltered room. That is a different horticultural proposition — more attention, slower growth, a smaller plant — but the reward is the same: a few weeks of a scent that fills the space immediately around the pot and nowhere else.

Two consequences follow for anyone reading this as a materials page. First, supply is nursery supply: the plant moves as rooted stock, not as sacks of dried flower. Second, the value sits in the specimen, not in a harvest. A large, well-grown, flowering shrub is worth more than the sum of its flowers, which is the reverse of how an aromatic crop is priced.

## The scent, and why it stays close to the flower

> **Direct answer:** The smell is sweet and floral with a soft fruity edge that many noses read as lemon peel or the aroma of steeped tea. It is lighter than jasmine and far less creamy than champaca, and it does not travel: you smell this plant from a step away, not from across a garden.

Describing it accurately means resisting two temptations. The first is to call it "delicate", which is true but vague. The second is to reach for food metaphors until the flower disappears into a dessert. What the nose actually meets is a clean sweet-floral centre with a thin citrus-fruit top and a faintly tea-like dryness underneath — closer to the smell of a hot glass of lightly scented tea than to a bouquet. There is very little of the heavy, animalic, indolic weight that gives jasmine its push.

The physics of the flower explains the character of the scent. Each blossom is only a few millimetres across, so the aromatic reservoir inside it is small. A single flower therefore releases very little, and a cluster reads as a scent only because there are many flowers open at once. Perception requires proximity because the source is small and the plume is narrow:

a flower a few millimetres wide → a correspondingly small charge of volatile oil → a scent plume measured in centimetres rather than metres → the plant is placed beside a path, a doorway or a seat → garden design follows the physics of the blossom.

Warm, still air holds the plume together; a breeze tears it apart and the plant seems to stop smelling. This is why visitors report the scent as a surprise at the gate and nothing at the far end of the lawn, and why the same shrub can seem potent on a humid evening and absent on a windy afternoon. The flower has not changed. The air has.

## The lemon in the description is an analogy

> **Direct answer:** Calling the scent lemon-adjacent names a resemblance, not an ingredient. Nothing in the flower is lemon oil, and a formulator who builds from the description will reproduce the comparison while losing the flower it was meant to point at.

Scent vocabulary is thin, so description proceeds by analogy: this smells like that. The habit is necessary — there is no other way to put a smell into words — and it carries a predictable failure. A reader who wants the smell takes the analogy as a recipe and reaches for the named material. Add lemon oil to a blend and you get lemon: bright, sharp, unmistakable, and nothing like the quiet fruit edge of a rice-grain flower.

The chain is worth watching for because it recurs across every aromatic family:

thin vocabulary for smell → description by comparison to something familiar → the comparison is read as a list of components → the familiar material is added directly → the blend converges on the familiar reference → the original target disappears behind its own description.

**Practical significance for a formulator:** use the analogy to identify the axis, then choose a material that carries that axis without taking over. "Lemon-adjacent" means the blend needs lift and a clean edge, not that it needs citrus. A small amount of neroli, or a trace of a green fruity note, moves in the right direction; a citrus oil moves in the direction of citrus.

**Editorial judgment:** descriptions are maps, not ingredients. The map is useful for navigation and useless as a meal. The same caution applies to every food metaphor in fragrance writing, including most of the vocabulary used for resins and woods.

## Judging the scent honestly

> **Direct answer:** A close-range flower has to be judged at close range, in the air and at the hour when you will actually meet it. Three simple conditions — distance, time of day, and still versus moving air — explain most disagreements about whether the plant smells strong.

The first condition is distance. Smelling a cluster held to the nose measures the flower; standing where you would sit measures the garden. Those are different questions and they give different answers, which is why a nursery description and a visitor's impression can both be honest and still disagree.

The second is the hour. Volatiles accumulate in still, warm air and disperse when the air moves, so the same shrub can seem generous at dusk and mute at noon. Judging a plant once, at the wrong hour, produces a conclusion about the weather rather than about the flower.

The third is condition. A cut branch is a depleting sample: the aromatic charge that made the living cluster smell begins to leave as soon as the tissue is severed, and it does not come back. A plant judged from a cut sprig will always seem weaker than the plant in the ground, and the gap widens with every hour the sprig spends in a vase.

**Practical significance for a buyer:** ask to see the plant in situ, at the hour you would use the space, and stand at the distance you would sit. If the seller can only offer a cut sample, treat the impression as a floor rather than a measurement. This is not fussiness — it is the only way to compare two plants fairly, and it costs nothing.

## Placing it among the sweet florals

> **Direct answer:** Within the crowded sweet-floral group, 米仔兰 occupies the lightest position: less creamy than champaca, less indolic than jasmine, less honeyed than osmanthus. The difference is not prestige but weight, and weight determines how each material is used.

Read the table down the projection column and the logic of the category appears. The plants that project — champaca, jasmine, osmanthus — are the ones that became extraction crops, because a flower that throws its scent into the air also gives it up to a solvent. The plants that whisper stay in gardens. 米仔兰 sits at the quiet end, and no amount of enthusiasm changes the arithmetic of yield per kilo of blossom.

**Editorial judgment:** this is not a ranking. A garden plant that scents one square metre perfectly is doing its job; a jasmine absolute that perfumes a room is doing a different job. The mistake is to assume the second job is the higher one.

## Is 米仔兰 a 香材? A category test

> **Direct answer:** Measured against the tests this encyclopedia applies to an incense material — storage stability, a workable delivery form, trade quantity, formulary record and a known burning practice — 米仔兰 fails every one. It is a garden fragrance, which is a category, not a consolation prize.

| Test | The question it asks | 米仔兰 |
|---|---|---|
| Storage stability | Does the scent survive drying and keep through a season? | No — the aromatic fraction leaves with the water |
| Delivery form | Can it be powdered, smouldered or distilled at useful yield? | No established form exists |
| Trade quantity | Does the aromatics trade move it in lots? | No — it moves as nursery stock |
| Formulary record | Does a classical incense text list it? | No entry in the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) |
| Burning practice | Is there a known, safe way to burn it? | None recorded |

A material does not need all five to be interesting, but it needs most of them to be a 香材. Resins pass on storage and delivery and often on record. Woods pass on storage and delivery. Roots and barks pass because their aromatics are locked into dense tissue. A fresh subtropical flower passes none, and the failure is not a judgment about beauty.

The test earns its keep in both directions. It stops the inflation that calls every fragrant plant an incense material, and it stops the opposite error — dismissing a garden plant as unimportant because it cannot be burned. Categories are tools for asking better questions, not medals.

## Why you will not find aglaia incense

> **Direct answer:** The flower's scent is a cloud of small, light molecules, and those are exactly the molecules that drying drives off and combustion destroys. Dry the blossom and little smell remains; burn what is left and the smoke dominates. The absence of an incense tradition is a chemical outcome, not a gap in taste.

The chain is short and it closes:

fresh blossom → volatile compounds with high vapour pressure → perceptible at body temperature → the same volatility lets them escape the picked flower within hours → drying carries the aromatic fraction away with the water → the dried blossom is close to odourless → a formulator has nothing left to blend → no incense tradition forms around the plant.

Compare the materials that do dry well. Resins keep their aromatics because the molecules are heavier and often locked in a hardened exudate. Woods keep theirs inside lignified tissue that releases them slowly when heated. Roots and barks do the same with denser structure. A thin-petalled flower is the opposite case: it is built for a few days of scent and then for seed, not for storage.

This also explains a disappointment that recurs in craft incense. Someone dries a scented flower, grinds it, burns the powder, and gets a brown acrid smoke with a ghost of the original smell. The ghost is real; the failure is not in the method. Most of the aromatic mass left the flower on the first day of drying, and what stayed behind is cellulose and mineral ash.

**What this means for a buyer:** any product sold as aglaia incense is a base material — charcoal, wood powder, binder — carrying an added fragrance, or it is a different flower wearing the name. There is no traditional aglaia burner to compare it against, so the label has nothing to be checked against except the ingredient list.

## The 珠兰 collision, and other name traps

> **Direct answer:** 珠兰 is the most dangerous of the plant's names, because in ordinary horticultural use it more often refers to *Chloranthus spicatus* (金粟兰), a plant in a different family with a different scent and a documented role in tea scenting. Buy by binomial.

| Name | Usually means | Family | Risk to a buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 米仔兰 | *Aglaia odorata* | Meliaceae | Low, if the binomial is confirmed |
| 米兰 | The same species, shortened | Meliaceae | Medium — used loosely in the nursery trade |
| 树兰 | The same species, garden usage | Meliaceae | Medium — regional and informal |
| 珠兰 | Often *Chloranthus spicatus* (金粟兰) | Chloranthaceae | High — a different plant entirely |
| 兰花 | Orchids generally | Orchidaceae | High — a whole family, not a species |
| 白兰 | *Michelia alba* (champaca) | Magnoliaceae | High — creamier, heavier, extractable |
| 玉兰 | Yulan magnolia | Magnoliaceae | High — a tree flower, not this shrub |

Common names travel by resemblance and by poetry, and both are unreliable carriers. A name that begins in one province's garden can end up on a different species in another province's nursery catalogue, and nobody is lying — the word simply drifted. 珠兰 is the clearest case in this group: the tea-scenting 珠兰 of the south is a small chloranthaceous plant, while garden writers sometimes use the same characters for 米仔兰. Two different plants, one label, no malice.

The screening rule is dull and it works. Ask for the binomial and the family. If a seller cannot supply both, the name on the label is doing marketing work rather than identification work. For *Aglaia odorata* the answer is Meliaceae; anyone who answers "orchid" has told you they are guessing.

## If a formula wants this smell

> **Direct answer:** You cannot buy the flower as an aromatic material, so the practical question becomes which available material carries the idea you are actually after. Match on the axis you want — tea-fruit, soft white floral, or citrus lift — rather than on the name.

| If you want | Reach for | Why it works | What you give up |
|---|---|---|---|
| The lemon-tea edge | Osmanthus absolute | Fruity-floral with a dry, tea-like finish | The green freshness of a living flower |
| Soft white-floral sweetness | Champaca absolute, used sparingly | Creamy and low-indolic at low dose | The small-scale intimacy; champaca is louder |
| A light citrus-floral lift | Neroli | Bright, fresh, top-note energy | The fruit and the tea |
| Creamy weight under a floral | Ylang-ylang, first grades | Dense sweet floral body | Subtlety, if overdosed |
| The actual living-plant effect | The plant itself | Only the plant carries it | Permanence — the scent is seasonal |

Substitution in this craft is axis-matching, not identity theft. You choose the property the formula needs — lift, body, fruit, dryness — and accept that the result is a new thing rather than a reconstruction. What no substitution delivers is the specific pleasure of a flower smelled at arm's length on a warm evening, because that pleasure depends on the flower being alive and the air being still.

**Editorial judgment:** burning anything adds combustion notes that the fresh flower never had. A formulator chasing the exact scent of 米仔兰 in a censer is chasing a target that the physics rules out. Better to borrow the idea and let the blend be its own composition.

## Traditional use and the evidence layers

> **Direct answer:** The documented use is horticultural and cultural — a fragrant courtyard and garden plant in the subtropical south. Botanical identity is settled; there is no classical incense record, and this page makes no clinical claim.

- **Documented use** — cultivation as an ornamental and fragrance plant in gardens, courtyards and containers. Aromatic and cultural, not medical.
- **Name history** — the 兰 element belongs to a broad fragrance vocabulary, and the 米 element describes the size of the blossom. The name is descriptive, not taxonomic.
- **Botanical record** — *Aglaia odorata* is placed in Meliaceae by standard floras, and the species is recorded in Flora of China and in global biodiversity databases.
- **What is not claimed** — no entry in the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘), no incense formulation, no medicinal use, no essential-oil industry.

The layers matter because they fail in different ways. A garden use can be documented by ordinary observation; a formulary absence can be checked against the text; a medicinal claim would need evidence this page does not have and will not invent. Keeping them separate is how a reader knows which sentence to trust and how far.

## Safety, and the one place smoke enters the story

> **Direct answer:** Growing 米仔兰 involves no combustion, so the usual incense smoke warning does not apply to the plant. It applies the moment someone sells you "aglaia incense", because what burns is a base material and the smoke belongs to that base.

Combustion of incense produces smoke and particulate matter regardless of what the label says the fragrance is. Burn in a ventilated space, keep the burner away from anything that can catch, and do not treat a pleasant smell as evidence of clean combustion. These are properties of burning, not of this flower.

For the plant itself the considerations are ordinary horticulture. A strongly scented shrub in a closed room can be oppressive to sit beside; the answer is air and distance, not a medical theory. Keep pots out of reach of children and pets that chew, as with any houseplant. And keep the label scepticism: "natural" describes an origin, not a safety profile.

## Summary

米仔兰 is *Aglaia odorata*, a meliaceous shrub of southern China and Southeast Asia, grown for a scent that lives at arm's length and dies at the picking. Its 兰 is an honorific from a fragrance vocabulary, not a claim about orchids; its 米 is a measurement of the blossom. It fails every test this encyclopedia uses for a 香材 — no storage stability, no delivery form, no trade lots, no formulary entry in the *Xiangcheng* (香乘), no burning practice — and that failure is the most interesting thing about it. A plant can be genuinely fragrant and still belong to the garden. Saying so plainly is more useful than borrowing a pedigree it never had.

## FAQ

**What is aglaia (米仔兰)?** A small evergreen tree or shrub, *Aglaia odorata*, in the mahogany family (Meliaceae), native to southern China and Southeast Asia. It is grown for clusters of minute yellow flowers whose sweet, faintly fruity scent carries a short distance.

**Is 米仔兰 an orchid?** No. The 兰 in its names is a fragrance word used across unrelated families — orchids, magnolias and this Meliaceae shrub all carry it. The binomial *Aglaia odorata* is the reliable identifier.

**What does it smell like?** Sweet and floral with a soft fruity edge that reads as lemon peel or steeped tea, and very little of the creamy or indolic weight found in champaca and jasmine. The scent is strongest in warm, still air close to the shrub.

**Why is there no aglaia incense?** Because the aromatic compounds that make the flower smell are light and volatile: they leave the blossom within hours of picking and are driven off entirely by drying. What remains has little scent, and burning it produces smoke rather than perfume. No classical formulary records it either.

**Is 珠兰 the same plant?** Often not. In everyday horticultural use 珠兰 more commonly names *Chloranthus spicatus* (金粟兰), a different family with a different scent and a tea-scenting role. Some garden writing applies the name to 米仔兰. Confirm the binomial before buying.

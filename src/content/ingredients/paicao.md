**Direct answer:** 排草 (páicǎo) is a dried aromatic herb of the primrose family, *Lysimachia capillipes*, gathered from the hills of southern and eastern China. It smells of sweet hay with a warm, seed-like edge and a faint woodiness — a supporting note in blended incense, never a lead. The material has two identities that do not quite line up: the modern whole herb, and the classical 排草香, which the Ming *Xiangcheng* describes as a white root from Jiaozhi that was often adulterated.

## Key facts

**Direct answer:** 排草 is a herbaceous Primulaceae aromatic, traded dried and used as a sweet-hay blender; its classical name points to a root, its modern trade to the whole plant.

Everything else in this entry follows from those two last rows. The material is easy to sell under a name and hard to verify under a name, and the part of the plant that the name refers to has changed at least once in the record.

## A herb, not a wood: the plant behind the name

**Direct answer:** The plant is a modest perennial, roughly knee-high, with angled stems, leaves spaced alternately along them, and small yellow flowers that sit alone where leaf meets stem. It is a hill species of the southern Chinese provinces, found from low valleys up to about two thousand metres.

Flora of China gives a precise picture, and the details are worth knowing because they tell you what a genuine sample should look like. The plant is a perennial herb, not a shrub and not a tree. Its stems are two to many, erect, angular or winged, branching from the middle. The leaves are alternate, ovate to lanceolate, 1.5–7 cm long and 1–3 cm wide, with entire or faintly undulate margins. The flowers are solitary and axillary, with a yellow corolla 6–8 mm across. The fruit is a globose capsule 3–4 mm in diameter.

Its Chinese range runs from Zhejiang and Fujian on the eastern side, west through Jiangxi, Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou and Sichuan to eastern Yunnan, with Taiwan offshore, a northward reach into southern Henan, and the Philippines at the southern edge. It appears in mixed forests, along streams, on acidic soils and on rocky limestone slopes, from low ground up to 2,000 m. Both the [Flora of China treatment](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200016984) and the [GBIF record](https://www.gbif.org/species/4005914) are worth opening before a purchase, because the photographs and the distribution map answer questions that a supplier's description will not.

Two consequences follow for anyone handling the dried material.

The first is that the plant is small and soft, so the dried herb is light and bulky. A kilogram of 排草 occupies far more space than a kilogram of sandalwood, and it compresses over time in storage. That bulk is one reason the material has always been a blender: it takes up volume in a formula without adding much weight.

The second is that the whole plant is aromatic, not just one organ. There is no resin duct to tap and no heartwood to separate. What you buy is what grew, dried — leaves, stems and all — which makes the leaf-to-stem ratio a real quality variable rather than a cosmetic one.

That last point is where the classical record diverges, and the divergence is worth taking seriously.

## The classical record: a white root from Jiaozhi

**Direct answer:** One Ming entry carries most of what the classical record says: a white root, fine as willow, coming from Jiaozhi (northern Vietnam) and later grown in Lingnan, with a market already full of imitations. That description does not line up exactly with the modern whole-herb trade, and the gap is the interesting part.

The passage is short and specific enough to quote:

> "排草出交址，今岭南亦或莳之，草根也，白色，状如细柳根，人多伪杂之。淮海志云：排草香状如白茅香，芬烈如茅香。白茅香、排草香、瓶香、耕香当是一类。"

Plainly: the plant came from Jiaozhi and was by then also grown in Lingnan; the part used was a root; the root was white and fine like willow; and the trade in it was already mixed with other things. A second source quoted inside the entry, the *Huaihai Zhi*, compares 排草香 to 白茅香 for appearance and to 茅香 for strength of scent, then places all four names — 白茅香, 排草香, 瓶香, 耕香 — in a single category.

Three things in that passage deserve attention.

*The part.* 草根也 — "it is a grass root". Not a leaf, not a whole herb. A buyer reading the classical text and then opening a modern sack of leafy 排草 is looking at two different commodities, close as the names sound.

*The geography.* Jiaozhi is northern Vietnam; Lingnan is the far south of China. Lingnan falls inside the modern species' range, while Jiaozhi sits beyond its southern edge — which is consistent with a trade good that travelled north before it was grown locally. The classical material was a southern commodity before it was a cultivated one.

*The class.* Grouping 排草香 with 白茅香, 瓶香 and 耕香 suggests the classical writers were describing a family of fragrant grasses and roots rather than a single botanic entity. That is a meaningful hint: the category may have been defined by use and aroma, not by species.

None of this disproves the modern identification. It does mean the identification is a working hypothesis rather than a settled equivalence, and a page that pretends otherwise is smoothing over the most interesting part of the record. Anyone who claims a given sack is "the classical 排草" is claiming more than the sources support.

## How the name drifted, and what a "class" means

**Direct answer:** The *Xiangcheng* groups 排草香 with 白茅香, 瓶香 and 耕香 as "one class", which suggests the classical name described a category of fragrant grasses and roots rather than a single species. That is a different kind of naming from the binomial system, and it changes how a classical formula should be read.

Classical Chinese aromatic writing often sorts materials by sensory family and use rather than by botany. A name could cover several plants that smelled alike, grew in similar places and were handled the same way, and the boundary between them was maintained by traders rather than by taxonomists. The grouping of 排草香 with 白茅香, 瓶香 and 耕香 reads exactly like that: four fragrant grass-and-root materials placed in one class because they belonged together in the nose and in the market.

This is not a failure of observation. It is a different purpose. A modern flora asks "which species is this?"; a Ming compendium asks "what is this material, where does it come from, and what is it good for?" The second question can be answered without the first, and often was.

The consequence for a reader is a discipline. When a classical formula calls for 排草香, the safest reading is "a fragrant root of the 排草 class, from the southern trade", not "the dried whole herb of *Lysimachia capillipes*". The two readings will usually point at similar material. They will not always point at the same sack.

Does that make the classical record useless? No — it makes it a record of a category. Categories are what formulators actually work with. A modern blender who says "I need a sweet hay note" is doing the same thing the Ming writer was doing, and the fact that the underlying species may have shifted does not invalidate the sensory instruction. It only warns against claims of exact continuity.

## Two names, one genus: 排草 and 零陵香

**Direct answer:** 排草 (*L. capillipes*) and 零陵香 (*L. foenum-graecum*) are different species of the same genus, with overlapping sweet-hay aromas and separate histories. Similar smell is not shared identity, and the two should not be substituted on aroma alone.

The confusion is easy to understand and hard to excuse. Same family, same genus, same curry note in the botanical description, same job in a blend — and two classical names that have been copied and re-copied for centuries, picking up regional variants along the way.

| Question | 排草 | 零陵香 |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Lysimachia capillipes* | *Lysimachia foenum-graecum* |
| Part in modern trade | Whole dried herb | Whole dried herb |
| Part in classical record | A white root (排草香) | A herb with a long name history |
| Aroma register | Sweet hay, warm seed, faint wood | Sweet hay, heavier, more coumarin-like |
| Classical standing | Recorded, with an adulteration warning | A long, well-attested incense history |
| Substitution risk | High — often sold as the other | High — the better-known name absorbs the lesser |

The table is not a grading. It is a reminder that the two columns answer different questions. A formulator who needs the rounder, heavier sweetness of 零陵香 will not get it from 排草, and a maker who wants the lighter, greener hay note will find 零陵香 too dense. The [linglingxiang entry](/ingredients/linglingxiang/) covers the other side of the pair.

There is a commercial dimension too. 零陵香 is the more famous name, which means the substitution pressure runs in one direction: lesser-known material sold under the better-known label. That is not a modern invention — the *Xiangcheng* was already complaining about adulteration in the Ming — but it does mean that a buyer who orders by reputation rather than by species is buying into an old problem.

## Why the smell reads as hay, and sometimes as curry

**Direct answer:** Hay is the dominant impression in the dried herb, with a warm seed-like undertone underneath it; the botanical literature calls the living plant strongly curry-scented when dry. Those are two readings of one aroma, taken at different strengths and different moisture levels.

"Curry-scented" is a botanical descriptor, not a recipe note. It points at the warm, spice-seed impression that many aromatic herbs develop when their volatiles concentrate during drying — a smell closer to a seed mixture than to a cooked dish. At low strength, the same material reads as hay: soft, dusty-sweet, green-gold. At higher strength, or when the herb is crushed, the warm edge comes forward and the "curry" comparison starts to make sense.

This has a practical consequence for judging a lot. A sample that smells only of hay may be perfectly good material at low aroma intensity, or it may be old. A sample that smells sharply of the warm seed note is not necessarily better — it may be fresher, or it may be a different species, or it may have been stored in a way that concentrated the heavier fraction while the light green notes escaped.

The way to tell is to warm a pinch in the palm and smell it twice: once immediately, once after thirty seconds. Fresh 排草 gives a green-sweet opening that softens into hay. Tired 排草 gives hay immediately and nothing after. Contaminated or substituted material often gives the warm note without the green opening, which is the sensory signature of a heavier relative.

That test is not a laboratory method. It is the method the trade actually uses, and it separates most good lots from most bad ones before any paperwork is exchanged.

## What 排草 does in a 合香 blend

**Direct answer:** In a 合香 blend 排草 is an assistant and guide: it fills the sweet-hay middle, softens the seam between resins and woods, and extends the body of a composition. It does not define a blend, and it cannot rescue one that has no structure.

The 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) frame is the clearest way to place it. 排草 is never 君. It has no defining note strong enough to carry a formula, and when it is given that role the result is a flat, hay-coloured smell with no shape. As 佐 it modifies texture and temperature; as 使 it can carry a blend and bind the parts at the middle. Both are useful, and both are modest.

The mechanism is straightforward:

sweet herb in the middle → fills the gap between a bright top and a heavy base → transitions stop feeling abrupt → the blend reads as continuous → the same herb overused → the middle becomes a wall → top and base are separated by hay → the composition loses contrast.

That chain explains the most common failure with 排草. It is a good material for smoothing, and smoothing is easy to overdo. A blend with too much of it does not smell wrong in any single moment; it smells undifferentiated, as though someone turned the contrast down.

The *Xiangcheng* record shows the material in actual use, and the formula is short enough to read closely: "甲香制过一钱五分，广排草须一两，花露一两" — processed murex at one-and-a-half qian, Guangdong paicao at one liang, flower dew at one liang. The proportions are worth noticing: the paicao is the largest single component by weight, the murex is a small and powerfully animalic fixative, and the flower dew is a liquid aromatic. That is a formula built around a soft, sweet, slightly animalic middle, with 排草 doing the bulk of the filling.

The name in that formula is 广排草 — "Guangdong paicao" — and the qualifier matters. It marks the material by origin, the same way 广零陵香 marks Lingnan-grown 零陵香. Origin qualifiers in classical formulas are not decoration; they are specifications, and their presence tells you that the classical trade already distinguished regional grades of the same name.

## Pairing: what 排草 flatters and what it smothers

**Direct answer:** 排草 flatters dry woods, soft resins and other herbs, and it smothers bright citrus, delicate florals and strongly defined spices. The difference is not a matter of taste; it follows from where the herb sits in the scent — a soft middle with no top and little base.

A pairing table is only useful if it states the cost, so this one does.

| Partner | What 排草 adds | What it costs | Use it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood | A dry hay middle under the cream | Can blur the wood's own top note | The blend is too sweet and needs drying |
| Agarwood | A softening layer in the middle | Flattens resin detail; can read as dust | The wood is plain and needs rounding |
| Patchouli | Dry-autumn earthiness | Two dry materials make a muddy middle | The blend needs weight without sweetness |
| Frankincense | A hay-and-resin contrast | The hay can read as staleness next to citrus | The resin is harsh and needs softening |
| Agastache | A meadow-green middle | Both are soft; the blend can go thin | The formula needs a green bridge |
| Osmanthus | A dried-flower effect | The floral top can be buried | The floral is already strong |
| Clove or cinnamon | A buffer for the spice | The spice loses definition | The spice is too sharp |
| 零陵香 | More hay, more weight | The two become indistinguishable | Never as a substitute — only as a deliberate pair |

Read the last row twice. 排草 and 零陵香 can be used together, and the result is a rounder, heavier hay note, but the pair stops being two materials and starts being one effect. That is fine if the effect is what you want. It is a problem if the formula was built to show the difference between them.

The general principle behind the table is a contrast budget. Every material in a blend either adds contrast or reduces it. 排草 reduces it: it fills, softens and connects. A blend can absorb a certain amount of that before it stops having shape.

enough 排草 to smooth the seams → the middle fills → the top and base are connected → the blend reads as continuous → more 排草 to smooth the remaining seams → the middle becomes a wall → the blend reads as flat → the maker adds a sharp top note → the top now sits on a wall instead of on a body.

That is the failure mode in slow motion, and it is worth recognising because each individual step feels like an improvement. The material is doing what it is good at; the composition is losing what made it interesting.

## Processing: the wine soak and the dry fry

**Direct answer:** A single line in the *Xiangcheng* gives the classical method — half a day in wine, then dry-fried — and it changes the herb in a specific direction: rounder, less green, more cooked. Modern handling is less interventionist, and for most blends it is the better default.

"排草酒浸半日，炒干" is a compact instruction with several effects folded into it.

*The soak.* Wine wets the herb thoroughly and dissolves part of the aromatic fraction. Some of that fraction stays in the herb, some moves into the liquid, and the herb's texture changes — it becomes pliable and easier to handle. The alcohol also acts as a solvent for components that water alone would not lift.

*The fry.* Dry-frying after the soak drives off the wine and the water, and it heats the leaf enough to brown it lightly. The result is a material that smells rounder and less green: the sharp top notes have gone, the hay note has deepened, and a toasted, almost nutty edge appears.

The trade is real. You gain smoothness and you lose lift. A wine-soaked, fried 排草 is easier to blend into a warm, resinous formula and harder to use where the composition needs a green or floral opening. The classical instruction is not a universal improvement; it is a technique with a direction.

There is also a practical warning hidden in the step. Alcohol extracts aroma, which means the liquid that runs off carries value with it. A maker repeating the process should decide in advance whether the wine is discarded or reused, because the answer changes the economics of the batch and the strength of the finished material.

Modern handling follows the same logic with fewer steps. Harvest or buy the herb, dry it in shade or gentle warmth so the leaf keeps its colour, cut it to a consistent size, and store it sealed and dark. Heat speeds drying and spends aroma; light bleaches colour and degrades the surface; air carries the volatiles away. The best 排草 is the least handled 排草 that is still dry enough to keep.

## Weight, volume and the bulk problem

**Direct answer:** 排草 is light for its volume, so a recipe written by volume and a recipe written by weight will disagree about it. The disagreement is largest for this material and smallest for resins and woods, which is why bulk density belongs in the notes of any blend that uses it.

A dried herb occupies several times the volume of the same weight of sandalwood powder. In a measuring-spoon formula, a spoon of 排草 is a small weight; in a formula written in grams, the same weight of 排草 looks like a large pile. Two makers following the "same" recipe can therefore produce blends that differ substantially in this one component.

The effect compounds with compression. Herb that has settled in a sack is denser than freshly cut herb; a scoop taken from the top of a full bag and a scoop taken from the bottom of a half-empty one can differ by a noticeable margin. The material is not changing; the packing is.

volume measure of loose herb → low mass per scoop → the herb is under-weighted → the blend loses its middle → the maker compensates by eye → the next batch uses a different scoop and a different fill → the formula drifts without anyone changing it.

The fix is unglamorous: weigh the herb, record the weight, and note whether the material was loose or settled when it was weighed. For a material sold in bulk and measured by the scoop, the temptation is to treat it casually. That is exactly why it drifts.

## Without fire: sachets, wardrobes and drawers

**Direct answer:** 排草 has a second life outside the censer, as a scenting herb for enclosed spaces and textiles. The mechanism is slow adsorption rather than combustion, and it rewards whole herb over powder.

The practice is old and plain: dried aromatic herb goes into a porous bag, the bag goes into a chest, a wardrobe or a drawer, and the enclosed air gradually takes on the smell. No heat is involved. The volatiles evaporate at room temperature, accumulate in the still air, and adsorb onto textile fibres, which then release them slowly each time the space is opened.

sealed space → volatiles evaporate into still air → concentration builds → fibres adsorb the aroma → the door opens → the stored smell releases → repeated openings deplete the reservoir → the herb is bruised to expose fresh surface → the cycle restarts, weaker each time.

Three practical consequences follow.

*Whole herb beats powder.* Powder releases faster and dusts everything it touches. In a drawer or a chest, the dust is a problem: it marks light fabric and it puts aromatic material directly against skin. Whole or coarsely cut herb in a lined bag gives the same effect with none of the mess.

*The bag is a dosage form.* A small bag in a large wardrobe is a weak dose; several bags distributed through the space work better than one heavy one, because the aroma needs to reach the fabric rather than sit in a corner.

*Refresh by bruising, not by adding.* Crushing the bag between the hands exposes new surface and buys another period of scent. Once the herb has given up its oil, bruising produces dust and no aroma — that is the signal to replace the contents rather than to add more.

There is a limit worth stating plainly. This is a scenting practice, not a pest-control method, and the historical association between aromatic herbs and stored cloth should not be read as a claim about insects or mould. The herb makes a chest smell good. That is the whole of the reliable claim.

## Why the note fades: volatility in a soft herb

**Direct answer:** 排草 has no resin and no dense wood to hold its aroma, so its volatiles leave whenever air moves past the leaf. Every handling decision — drying, cutting, bagging, storage — is therefore a decision about how much of the note survives to the censer.

A resin holds its aromatic compounds in a matrix that gives them up slowly. A wood holds them in oil-bearing cells behind a hard surface. A dried leaf holds them in thin tissue with a large surface-to-volume ratio, which is excellent for releasing scent and terrible for keeping it.

Three processes run at once once the herb is dry.

*Evaporation.* The lighter molecules leave the leaf continuously, faster when warm and faster again when air moves. This is why a sack left open in a warm room smells wonderful and then stops smelling of anything.

*Oxidation.* Oxygen reacts with part of the aromatic fraction, and the products are not the original smell. The green opening goes first; the hay note lasts longer; the residue is dusty.

*Moisture cycling.* A herb that takes up and loses water repeatedly, as happens in an unheated store through the seasons, suffers both effects faster. Each cycle loosens the tissue and exposes more surface.

dry herb → large surface area → light volatiles evaporate → green opening declines → oxygen attacks the remainder → hay note flattens → moisture cycling opens the tissue further → the lot reaches a stable, dull "old herb" smell.

This explains a pattern that puzzles buyers: two sacks from the same supplier, bought a year apart, can smell like different materials. They may be the same species, the same field and the same grade — with a year of storage between them.

The practical response is not complicated. Buy in quantities that will be used, not quantities that will be stored. Keep the herb whole until use, because cut herb has more surface. Seal it, darken it, cool it, and accept that the note has a shelf life even under good conditions. A maker who plans around that limit will produce more consistent blends than one who assumes the material is stable.

## Quality, purity and the oldest warning in the file

**Direct answer:** Judge 排草 on aroma, leaf-to-stem ratio, cleanliness and dryness — and treat purity as a first-order check, because the *Xiangcheng* already recorded that the material was 人多伪杂之, "often adulterated by many people".

That phrase is unusual in a classical text. Most entries describe what a material is; this one adds that the market was full of versions that were not it. The warning has aged well.

| Grade | Appearance | Aroma when warmed | Likely origin of the fault |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Even green-gold colour, leaf-dominant, clean cut, no dust | Green-sweet opening, hay middle, soft drydown | — |
| B | Some stem, slight colour variation, minor dust | Hay forward, less green, shorter | Later harvest, ordinary handling |
| C | Heavy stem, brown or bleached patches, visible foreign matter | Flat, dusty, occasionally musty | Poor drying, age, storage |
| Reject | Mould, insect damage, chemical or petrol smell | Musty, sour, or sharply unnatural | Moisture damage or contamination |

Four checks do most of the work.

*Leaf-to-stem ratio.* The leaf carries the aroma; the stem carries bulk. A sack that is mostly stem is a sack of volume. Break a handful apart and look at what fraction is leaf.

*Colour.* Fresh, well-dried 排草 is green-gold, not brown and not vivid green. Brown suggests age or heat damage; unnaturally bright green suggests either very fresh material or colour that has been helped along, and the second possibility is worth a question.

*Dust and foreign matter.* Fine dust is aroma that has already left the leaf. A lot that arrives dusty has lost part of what was paid for, and the dust itself is a sign of handling that was not gentle.

*Smell, twice.* As described above: warm a pinch, smell immediately, smell again. The opening tells you whether the light fraction survived; the tail tells you whether there is any body underneath.

Adulteration in a sweet herb usually takes one of three forms: a cheaper *Lysimachia* or another fragrant grass mixed in, spent material that has already been used for scenting and dried again, or inert bulk such as stems and dust. None of these is easy to see. All of them are easier to smell, which is why the warming test is worth more than a photograph.

## A bench protocol for a new lot

**Direct answer:** Test a new lot in four steps — look, break, warm, burn a pinch — and record what each step shows. The sequence costs a few minutes and catches the faults that photographs and certificates both miss.

Most sourcing advice stops at "check the aroma". That instruction is true and nearly useless, because it does not say what to do first or what a normal result looks like. A fixed sequence is more practical.

**Step one: look at the sack, not the sample.** Open the bag and look at the surface and at a handful taken from the middle. Surface material is usually the prettiest material. Look for colour banding, which suggests lots mixed after drying; for dust at the bottom, which suggests handling loss; and for any sign of damp — clumping, a darker patch, a smell that arrives before the hand does.

**Step two: break a stem.** A dry stem should snap. A stem that bends is holding moisture, and moisture in a sealed bag is the beginning of a mould problem. The break also shows the inside colour: bright and green-gold is fresh, dull brown is old.

**Step three: warm a pinch.** Rub a small amount between the palms for a few seconds and smell it twice, as described earlier. Record the opening and the tail separately. A lot with a good opening and no tail will fade in the blend; a lot with no opening and a heavy tail is probably older material or a different species.

**Step four: burn a pinch.** Put a small amount on a heat-safe surface, light it, let it burn a few seconds, and smell the smoke from a distance — never with the face in the plume. The burned smell should resemble the warmed smell, with more smoke. If the burned smell is harsh, acrid or chemical, the lot is not suitable for incense regardless of how good the raw herb seemed.

| Step | Pass | Fail | What the failure usually means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Look | Even colour, no clumps, little dust | Banding, clumping, heavy dust | Mixed lots, damp, rough handling |
| Break | Clean snap, green-gold interior | Bends, brown interior | Undried or aged material |
| Warm | Green opening, hay tail | Flat, musty, or sharp | Old stock, mould, substitution |
| Burn | Smoke resembles the warm smell | Acrid, chemical, choking | Contamination or wrong material |

The protocol is deliberately crude. It is meant to be repeatable by anyone in a warehouse with no instruments, and it is meant to produce a written record that can be compared across lots. A supplier who resists a four-step check is telling you something useful.

## Sourcing: what to ask before the first order

**Direct answer:** Ask for the species, the part, the harvest window and the drying method, and ask for a sample that can be warmed and smelled. A 排草 quote without those four answers is a quote for a name, not a material.

| Question | Good answer | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Which species? | *Lysimachia capillipes* | "排草" alone, or a shrug |
| Which part? | Whole herb, leaf-dominant | No answer, or a part that shifts between samples |
| When harvested? | A named season or recent window | "Current stock" with no date |
| How dried? | Shade or low-temperature, stated | Unknown, or "quick" |
| May I sample? | Yes, with a warmed-smell check | Photographs only |

The part-of-plant question is the one buyers forget, because the modern trade assumes whole herb while the classical text describes a root. If a supplier is offering root material labelled 排草, that is not necessarily fraud — it may be a deliberate classical interpretation — but it is a different commodity with different behaviour, and the price should reflect which one is in the bag.

Origin is a weaker signal than part. The species grows across a wide band of southern and eastern China, and the same province can produce good and poor material depending on the season and the drying. A named mountain is a story; a warm pinch in the hand is evidence.

For the wider sourcing logic — how to compare quotes across suppliers when the names are not standardised — the site's [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) and [bulk ingredient sourcing](/blog/bulk-incense-ingredient-sourcing/) guides cover the structure. The 排草 case adds one wrinkle of its own: the name itself is ambiguous, so species and part have to be fixed before price is even comparable.

## Safety, storage and the limits of a traditional claim

**Direct answer:** 排草 is burned as part of a blend, so the ordinary combustion precautions apply; stored badly it can mould; and its traditional medicinal language is not evidence of a clinical effect.

Burning any dried plant produces smoke and particulate matter. A sweet herb does not make the smoke gentler, and a hay-like note does not make it cleaner. Burn in moving air, avoid drawing the plume directly, and keep lit material out of reach of children and pets. The [safety guide](/safety/) covers ventilation and household precautions in more detail.

Storage is the quieter risk. Dried herb that picks up moisture can grow mould, and mouldy 排草 may smell musty before it looks wrong. Keep it sealed, dark and cool; inspect on a schedule; discard a musty lot rather than blending around it. Herbal material is more vulnerable than resin or wood because it has more surface and less natural protection.

The traditional claim needs the same kind of boundary. 排草 appears in classical materia medica and in household practice, and that history is real. It is not a clinical finding. This page describes what the material is and how it has been used; it does not assert that it treats anything, and readers should not read the historical record as a therapeutic recommendation.

## What the record supports

**Direct answer:** The botanical identity is established, the classical record is documented, the aromatic use is historically attested, and no clinical claim is supported. Keeping those four in separate columns prevents most overreach.

The ambiguity about the part is not a flaw in the page; it is a fact about the material's history. A confident answer would be tidier and less true.

Read the table as a set of separate claims rather than a ladder. A lot can be botanically correct and historically interesting while still being a poor incense material, because identity, provenance and performance are different questions. The columns are arranged to stop one answer from doing duty for another: "it is the right species" does not settle "it is the right part", and neither settles "it is good material". Keeping them apart is most of the discipline this entry is trying to teach.

## FAQ

**Is 排草 the same as 排草香 in old recipes?** Not necessarily. 排草香 is the classical name, and the *Xiangcheng* describes it as a white root from Jiaozhi. Modern 排草 is the whole herb of *Lysimachia capillipes*. The names are related; the materials may not be identical.

**Can 排草 be burned on its own?** It can, but it is not built for it. The herb gives a soft, sweet, hay-like smoke with little structure, and burning it alone produces a flat smell and a lot of ash. It is a blender, and its value appears when it is working under something else.

**Why does my 排草 smell of curry?** Because the dried herb carries a warm, seed-like note that Flora of China describes as curry-scented. At low intensity it reads as hay; crushed or warmed, the spice-seed edge comes forward. It is the same aroma, not a contaminant.

**Is 排草 a substitute for 零陵香?** No. They are different species with overlapping aromas. 零陵香 is heavier and rounder; 排草 is lighter and greener. Substituting one for the other changes the middle of a blend, which is exactly where the substitution is hardest to notice until the blend is finished.

**How do I know if 排草 is adulterated?** Warm a pinch and smell it twice, then look at the leaf-to-stem ratio and the dust. Adulteration usually shows as a missing green opening, an excess of stem, or a musty undertone. The *Xiangcheng* warning that the material was often adulterated still describes the market accurately.

## Summary

排草 (*Lysimachia capillipes*) is a soft perennial herb of the southern Chinese hills, dried whole and used as a sweet-hay blender in 合香. Its scent opens green, settles into hay, and leaves a light woody trace, with a warm seed note that Flora of China calls curry-scented. The Ming *Xiangcheng* records a different-looking material under the same name — a white root from Jiaozhi, already adulterated in the market, used in formulas with murex and flower dew. Both facts are worth carrying: the herb is a useful assistant, and the name is not a specification. Buy by species and part, test with a warmed pinch and a burned pinch, expect the note to fade, and let it support a blend rather than define one.

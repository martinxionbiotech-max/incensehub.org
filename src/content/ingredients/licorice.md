> **Direct answer:** 甘草 (gāncǎo), licorice, is the dried underground portion — root and rhizome together — of *Glycyrrhiza uralensis*, a perennial in the pea family whose native ground is the dry grassland belt running from northern China through Mongolia into Siberia. A saponin glycoside called glycyrrhizin gives the root a sweetness reported at many times that of sucrose. Chinese incense uses it for the opposite of a sweet note: small measured additions soften sharper materials and make a compound blend cohere.

Three rows carry warnings. "Licorice" covers more than one plant. "Root and rhizome" is one traded material, not two products. And "sweet" describes a taste on the tongue, not a smell in the room. A buyer who carries those three distinctions into a negotiation avoids most of the mistakes this page is about.

## What the plant is, and what the word covers

> **Direct answer:** *Glycyrrhiza uralensis* is a perennial legume whose woody root and rhizome are dug, dried, and sold as 甘草. The English word covers more than one species, so the binomial is the only useful specification.

*Glycyrrhiza uralensis* is an herbaceous perennial in the pea family, Fabaceae — the same family as beans, clover, and acacia. It is not a tree, not a resin, and not a grass. The plant dies back each year and rebuilds from a woody root system that reaches down into dry ground; the part harvested is that underground structure, root plus rhizome, dug rather than picked.

The root is woody and fibrous, yellow inside when cut, and it keeps its sweetness after drying. That persistence is the first practical fact: a chip from a well-stored lot still tastes sweet years later, which is why sweetness works as a field test.

Its native ground is the steppe and semi-desert belt that runs from northern China across Mongolia into Siberia. Cold, dry, continental conditions suit it, which separates the crop from the Mediterranean *Glycyrrhiza glabra* and its warmer agricultural system. The two are relatives, not the same article of trade, and the difference survives every step from field to powder.

Collection and cultivation both supply the trade. Wild stands are dug where the plant already grows, which puts the quality question on the collector's drying method as much as on the plant. Cultivated stands allow selection, spacing, and a predictable harvest age, which is why cultivated root tends to arrive in more uniform lots. Neither route is automatically better. A well-dried wild root beats a rushed cultivated one, and a cultivated lot with a clean harvest record is easier to specify than a wild lot whose origin is only a province.

The genus, though, holds more than those two. Chinese pharmacopoeial 甘草 is a drug category, and the category admits *G. uralensis*, *G. glabra*, and *G. inflata*. So a label reading "甘草" has named a drug class, not a species. A label reading "*Glycyrrhiza uralensis*" has named a plant. The gap between those two statements is where most sourcing confusion lives, and it is worth pinning down before price is discussed at all.

The species description is public and stable: see the [Flora of China treatment](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200012145) and the [GBIF taxon record](https://www.gbif.org/species/7861283). Those pages settle what the plant is. They say nothing about a shipment, which is a separate discipline covered below.

## The name is a job description

> **Direct answer:** 甘草 names a taste rather than a place or a scent, and that is the first clue to its function: the root enters a formula as a corrective, not as a fragrance.

甘草 is built from two ordinary characters: 甘, "sweet," and 草, "herb." Nothing is hidden in it. The name says what the material tastes like and, by extension, what it is for.

That plainness is unusual in this materia medica. 沉香, "sinking fragrance," names a behaviour — the wood that sinks in water and carries a heavy aroma. 川芎 names a place, Sichuan, attached to a root traded from there. 甘草 names a sensation. When a naming system opts for a taste instead of an origin or an aroma, it is telling you that the taste is the point of the material.

For an incense formulator, the name functions as a short brief. You are not buying a top note. You are buying a corrective. The root enters a compound incense the way salt enters a dough: not to be tasted on its own, but to make everything else read as it should.

The classical phrase attached to the material makes the same point in formula language: 调和诸药, literally "regulate and harmonise the many drugs." Read carefully, that phrase is about the relations among ingredients in a compound preparation. It is not a statement about a symptom, an organ, or a cure. The distinction sounds pedantic until you see how often the phrase is lifted out of formula theory and reused as if it were a therapeutic claim. It is not one, and this page does not treat it as one.

## The preparation list is a workflow, not a scent family

> **Direct answer:** When the *Xiangcheng* groups 甘草 with agastache (藿香) and 零陵香, it is grouping materials that share a handling instruction — pick clean, sun-dry, crumble — not materials that share an aroma. Reading that list as a fragrance family is one of the easiest mistakes to make with classical incense texts.

Look at what the three materials have in common physically. Agastache is a leafy herb; 零陵香 is a leafy aromatic; licorice is a woody root. They do not smell alike, they do not burn alike, and they occupy different places in a formula. What they share is that all three arrive from the field carrying stems, weeds, grit, and uneven moisture, and all three tolerate the same gentle preparation: clean, sun-dry, crumble.

That is a workflow grouping. The text is telling a workshop how to handle a class of incoming material, not telling a formulator which scents belong together. Once you see the list that way, its inclusion of licorice stops being puzzling. A root that will be powdered belongs in the same cleaning-and-drying instruction as the leafy herbs, because the same hands and the same drying floor handle all of them.

The misreading has practical consequences. A reader who treats the list as an aroma family may assume licorice, agastache, and 零陵香 are interchangeable or complementary by nature, and build a blend on that assumption. A reader who treats it as a workflow learns something more useful: which materials the workshop expected to process together, and therefore which ones shared a quality standard at intake.

Classical technical texts compress instruction this way throughout. A list can be a recipe, a procedure, or a category, and the same sentence pattern can carry any of the three. The way to tell them apart is to ask what the listed items have in common — if the commonality is physical or procedural rather than aromatic, the list is a workflow.

## Why a sweet root belongs in a smoky blend

> **Direct answer:** The sweetness sits in a large, non-volatile glycoside, so it barely reaches the smoke. What a blend gains is softness at the edges, not a sweet note in the air.

Set a pinch of licorice powder on a warm plate and the first impression is disappointing. There is a dry, woody, faintly earthy smell with a hay-like edge and a suggestion of bitterness underneath. Nothing like the intensity of the taste. A stick made of nothing but licorice root burns to a thin, flat smoke that no one would call perfume.

That gap between taste and smell is the key to understanding the material, and it is a chemical gap rather than a poetic one. The sweetness comes from glycyrrhizin, a large, polar saponin glycoside. Molecules of that size and polarity do not evaporate into a smoke stream the way small aromatic terpenes do. Heat lifts the volatile fraction of a botanical; it does not lift the glycoside. So the sweet taste stays in the ash and in the root, while the nose receives only the modest volatile fraction.

What the nose does receive is a soft, dry woodiness. In a blend, that softness is doing work out of proportion to its intensity. Sharp aromatic materials — camphoraceous roots, pungent spices, some resins — announce themselves at the front of the burn. Licorice's contribution arrives alongside them, and the contrast lowers the perceived sharpness of the whole. The sharp material has not changed. The way it is heard has.

There is a second, physical contribution that has nothing to do with scent. A fine root powder fills the small voids between coarser particles in a stick or cone. The burn front then advances through a more even matrix, and the smoke delivered from minute to minute varies less. This is a working model, not a measured result from our own bench: we have not run smoke chemistry on licorice-bearing sticks, and we would not present a guess as data. Treated as a model, it explains a common observation — that small amounts of fine powders make a stick behave more predictably.

The mechanism chain, stated plainly: non-volatile glycyrrhizin stays in the ash → only the root's mild woody volatiles reach the smoke → the blend gains softness rather than sweetness → the formulator perceives "rounding" instead of a sweet note. A second chain runs through the physical matrix: fine powder fills voids → the burn front advances evenly → smoke density varies less → the blend reads consistent from stick to stick. Neither chain requires licorice to be a fragrance in its own right. Both explain why it is dosed in grams rather than tens of grams.

## Dosing: the qian-against-liang signal

> **Direct answer:** The classical proportion — a few qian beside liang of the chief aromatics — encodes a supporting role. The tier is the useful information; the gram figure is not.

Section by section, the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) names the powdered root 甘草末, and the quantities it gives are small — a few *qian* set beside *liang* of the chief aromatics. As a recipe the passage is a historical curiosity; as a ratio it is a specification.

The units matter less than the proportion they encode. A formula that spends *liang* on its chief aromatic and *qian* on licorice is telling you, in the notation of its own time, that licorice is a minor addition. The pattern repeats across the classical material-preparation sections, which list 甘草 alongside agastache (藿香) and 零陵香 as a routine ingredient to be picked clean, sun-dried, and crumbled. Routine, minor, and measured — three words that describe a supporting material rather than a featured one.

What follows for a modern bench is not a number to copy. It is a way to read a formula. If a draft calls for licorice at the same weight as the chief aromatic, the draft is probably wrong, or it is making a different kind of incense than the classical record describes. The usual range in our reading sits far below the principal materials, and the right figure depends on how sharp the rest of the blend is, what binder carries it, and whether the format is a stick, a cone, or a loose powder.

The failure mode of overdosing is worth naming, because it is not what people expect. Too much licorice does not make a blend taste sweet. It makes the blend flat. Contrast collapses, the sharper materials lose their outline, and the smoke reads dull rather than rich. That is the practical argument for restraint: the material works by relation, and a relation needs two sides.

## Units, ratios, and why the grams do not travel

> **Direct answer:** Classical weights are ratios first and masses second. The *Xiangcheng* uses the Ming system, in which a *liang* divides into ten *qian*, but the absolute mass of a *liang* shifted across dynasties and regions — so the proportion inside one formula is the reliable information, not a converted gram figure.

A modern reader who converts a Ming *liang* to grams and then scales the recipe to a hundred sticks is performing arithmetic on a unit that was never fixed. The conversion looks precise and is not. Two rules keep the reading honest. Compare a material's weight only against the other materials in the same formula, never against a weight from a different text or a different century. And when a translation prints grams without naming the standard it used, treat the number as indicative of proportion rather than as a measurement.

A third rule concerns batch size. Classical incense formulas typically describe a batch, not a single stick, and the batch is then formed, cut, or pressed. The per-unit dose a modern maker cares about is therefore derived — divided out of a total — and any small error in the batch total is multiplied across every unit. This is why classical proportions are best read as a shape: a large weight for the chief, smaller weights for the supporters, a small weight for the harmonizer, and a trace for anything potent.

That shape is what makes the licorice figure legible. A few qian set beside liang is not a number to reproduce; it is a statement that the material belongs to the smallest tier of the formula. Get the tier right and the exact figure can be tuned on the bench. Get the tier wrong and no amount of precision will rescue the blend.

## Scaling a formula without breaking the trace tier

> **Direct answer:** Converting a formula to percentages and scaling by batch mass is straightforward for the chief and minister materials and treacherous for the trace tier. A harmonizer measured at a fraction of a percent can fall below the resolution of a bench scale in a small batch, and the resulting weighing error is large in relative terms even when it looks tiny in grams.

The arithmetic is simple. Weigh the whole formula, divide each material by the total, and you have percentages that travel across batch sizes. Multiply by the new batch mass and you have weights again. For materials at ten percent and above, the round-trip is safe.

The trouble starts at the bottom of the table. Take a material at half a percent of a two-hundred-gram batch: one gram, easy to weigh. Scale the same formula to a twenty-gram test batch and the same material is a tenth of a gram — inside the noise of many bench scales, and inside the range where a busy operator rounds by eye. The percentage did not change. The reliability did.

What follows is a chain worth memorizing: small batch → trace weight approaches scale resolution → the relative weighing error grows → the harmonizer's dose drifts between batches → the softening effect varies → the blend reads inconsistent even though the formula never changed. The fault looks like a material problem and is actually an arithmetic-and-equipment problem.

The standard fix is geometric pre-dilution. Blend the trace material thoroughly with a weighed portion of one of the major powders — typically the chief, since it will be in the batch anyway — to make a premix of known strength, then weigh the premix instead of the pure trace material. A premix turns a tenth-of-a-gram weighing into a ten-gram weighing, and the relative error collapses.

Two conditions make the technique honest. The premix must be recorded as its own lot, with the ratio and the date, or the batch record becomes unreadable. And it must be genuinely uniform: a coarse trace powder folded into a fine chief powder will separate in the jar, so sieve both to a comparable fraction first. A premix that separates is worse than no premix, because it looks like a solution.

For a workshop producing sample sets and full batches from one formula, the premix is usually the difference between a reproducible product and a product that drifts by a few percent each run — which, for a material whose whole job is relational balance, is exactly the kind of drift that shows.

## Where licorice sits in 君臣佐使

> **Direct answer:** Read through the four roles, 甘草 is an assistant or a guide: it does not carry the composition, it reconciles the materials that do.

Chinese formula theory reads a composition through four roles: 君 (chief), 臣 (minister), 佐 (assistant), and 使 (guide). The labels describe hierarchy and function, not price or prestige. A chief carries the composition's identity; a minister supports or extends it; an assistant corrects or modulates; a guide reconciles the parts and directs them.

甘草 is normally read as an assistant or guide — 佐 or 使 — and that reading fits everything the material does. It does not carry the blend. It does not extend the chief's aroma. It reconciles: it softens the edges where two strong materials meet, and it gives the composition a smoother surface. The classical phrase 调和诸药 is a formula-theory statement of exactly this role.

One caution about the frame. Calling a material a 使 does not mean it is a lesser material, and it does not mean it can be swapped for any other 使. The four roles describe different jobs, and a job is not a rank. A blend with no guide can still smell fine; it will simply be harder to keep balanced as the formula grows, because nothing is damping the friction between strong notes. That is a judgment about structure, not a claim about quality.

For the wider logic of how these roles interact across a formula, see [hexiang (合香) blending](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/). Licorice is a small, clean example of the assistant's work: it changes the relations among materials without adding a subject of its own.

## From root to powder

> **Direct answer:** Dig, wash, trim, dry, slice, grind, sieve. Most of the quality is settled in the drying and in the moisture at the mill, not in the grinding itself.

Processing licorice for incense is short, and most of the quality is decided before the grinder is switched on. Roots are dug, washed free of soil, trimmed of the above-ground stem, and dried. Drying is the step that fixes the material's future: root dried too fast case-hardens on the outside and stays damp within, and a damp centre is a mould risk that no later sieving will remove.

The *Xiangcheng* gives the classical preparation in one line: pick the material clean of stems and weeds, sun-dry it, and crumble it. That instruction is not decorative. Stems and weed fragments are the most common visible contaminants in a cheap lot, and they dilute both sweetness and aroma. Sun-drying is a slow, gentle method that suits a woody root; crumbling is the pre-industrial version of grinding.

Slicing usually comes before grinding. Cross-sections dry evenly, show the interior colour, and let a buyer inspect for rot or soil before committing to powder. A slicer also feeds the mill more uniformly, which matters because a mill grinds mixed sizes unevenly.

Grinding introduces the variables that a formulator actually feels. Particle size distribution sets how the powder packs, how much air the matrix holds, and how fast the stick takes up moisture from the air. A very fine powder increases surface area and speeds both aroma loss and moisture pickup; a coarse powder packs loosely and can leave a stick friable. Moisture content changes how the powder behaves in the mill, how it blends with the binder, and how the formed stick dries. There is no universal target — the correct figure depends on the formula, the binder, and the production line — which is why a specification copied from another factory is a starting point, not an answer.

For the wider mechanics of turning botanical material into a burnable powder, see [how incense powder is made](/blog/how-incense-powder-is-made/). Licorice is an easy material to grind and an easy one to ruin by storing the result badly.

## Forms, and what each one is for

> **Direct answer:** Powder is the blending form and the one the classical formulas name. Whole and sliced root exist to be inspected and stored before that step.

| Form | What it is | Best use | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole root | Dried, unsliced root and rhizome | Inspection, long storage, museum reference | Must be milled before use; interior hidden until cut |
| Sliced root | Cross-sections or long slices | Incoming inspection, sampling, small batches | More exposed surface, faster moisture pickup |
| Powder (甘草末) | Ground and sieved root | Blending into 合香 formulas | Loses aroma faster, cakes in humid storage |
| Extract or syrup | Concentrated preparations | Not a classical incense form | Sugar load, stickiness, unclear dose |

It is the powder that the formulas actually call for, and powder remains the operative form for blending. That is a statement about function, not about quality: whole root is not "better" than powder, it is simply earlier in the chain, and it preserves the evidence a buyer needs to judge the lot.

The extract row deserves suspicion in incense work. A concentrated preparation is made for ingestion and flavour; it carries sugars and water, it is sticky, and its strength is not expressed like a dry powder's. In a stick it changes the burn and the drying behaviour in ways the classical record never contemplated, and it is no shortcut to more licorice.

## Format matters: stick, cone, loose powder, sachet

> **Direct answer:** The same licorice powder behaves differently across formats because each format changes air flow, density, and burn temperature. A stick, a pressed cone, a trailed powder, and a sewn sachet are four different physical systems, and the harmonizer's contribution scales with the system rather than with the recipe alone.

In a stick, the powder is bound, extruded, and dried. The matrix is relatively open, air draws through it, and the burn front advances along the length. Licorice's fine fraction fills voids here, and the physical effect to watch is the paste: a root carrying both glycyrrhizin and polysaccharides can make a wet batch feel tackier, which changes how much water the mix takes and how long the extruded sticks need to dry. A batch that suddenly needs more water after a licorice lot change is telling you the powder's particle distribution moved.

In a pressed cone, the same powder is compacted. Density is higher, air flow lower, and the burn slower. The softening effect reads heavier in a cone, in our reading, because the smoke spends longer in the matrix and the sharper notes have more time to dominate before the softer fraction catches up. A formula tuned as a stick will often feel blunt as a cone at the same ratio.

Loose powder burned in a trail, the form behind 香篆, has no binder at all. Air supply is generous, the burn is fast and open, and residue behaviour becomes visible in a way a stick hides. Licorice leaves a relatively large mineral-rich ash for its weight, which is unremarkable in a stick and obvious in a trail.

A sachet is the boundary case, and it is worth stating plainly: licorice is a poor sachet material. Without combustion, the nose receives only the cold volatile fraction, and glycyrrhizin contributes nothing at room temperature. A sachet filled with licorice smells of dry root and little else. The material's whole argument depends on a burn.

## Three near-neighbours, and why "sweet" is not a family

> **Direct answer:** At least three unrelated mechanisms produce what incense vocabulary calls sweetness: a non-volatile glycoside you taste (licorice), a volatile compound you smell as sweet (anise and fennel, via anethole), and a heavy sesquiterpene alcohol that reads creamy (sandalwood, via santalols). Grouping them under one word hides the only difference that matters to a blender.

| Material | Where the sweetness comes from | Reaches the nose? | Behaviour in a stick |
|---|---|---|---|
| Licorice root | Glycyrrhizin, a large polar glycoside | Barely; it is not volatile | Softens and reconciles; adds little scent |
| Anise / fennel | Anethole, a small volatile | Strongly, at the top of the burn | Leads; sweet in the first minute, then fades |
| Sandalwood | Santalols, sesquiterpene alcohols | Yes, slowly and for a long time | Anchors a base; creamy rather than sugary |
| Spikenard root | A dense, valerian-like volatile profile | Yes, strongly and persistently | Dominates a middle register if overdosed |

Read the table down the second column and the point becomes obvious. Licorice's sweetness is chemistry of the tongue; anise's is chemistry of the air; sandalwood's is chemistry of time. A formulator who substitutes one for another because all three are described as sweet will get a blend that fails in three different ways.

The practical test is simple. Warm a pinch of the material and smell it. Anise answers immediately and loudly. Sandalwood answers slowly and stays. Licorice barely answers at all. That silence in the smelling jar is not a defect — it is the signature of a material whose work happens in relation to others rather than in its own right.

This is also where a common buying mistake lives. A buyer who evaluates licorice by aroma intensity, the way they would evaluate anise, will reject good lots and accept bad ones. The correct evaluation for this material is taste, cut surface, and consistency, with aroma used only to detect staleness or contamination.

## Grading: six checks, and what each one cannot tell you

> **Direct answer:** Six checks — species, taste, warmed smell, cut surface, moisture, and sieve behaviour — work as a set, because each one is blind to something another catches.

There is no formal grading scale for incense-grade licorice. What exists is a set of practical checks, each of which catches something and misses something else. Treating any single check as decisive is the common error.

| Check | What it catches | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| Species on the label | The *uralensis* / *glabra* / *inflata* question | Whether the label is true |
| Taste of a dry chip | Presence and persistence of sweetness | Whether sweetness comes from the root or from added material |
| Smell when warmed | Stale, musty, or scorched root | Subtle species differences, which are small |
| Cut surface | Rot, soil intrusion, insect damage | Internal decay not visible at the cut |
| Moisture by feel and behaviour | Damp root, damp powder, caking risk | Uniformity across a lot |
| Sieve and lot record | Particle consistency, traceability | Whether the mill ran clean between lots |

A buyer who runs only the first check is trusting paperwork. A buyer who runs only the second is trusting a tongue that can be fooled by a sweetened sample. The checks work as a set: species plus taste plus cut surface, then moisture and sieve behaviour on the powder, with a lot record tying the sample to the shipment.

Two boundaries are worth stating. First, sweetness is not a grade. A root can be intensely sweet and still be dirty, damp, or the wrong species. Second, aroma intensity is a weak signal for this material specifically: licorice is a low-intensity aromatic by design, so a lot that smells strong is not necessarily better — it may simply be a different species or a root with more of its volatile fraction intact.

## What a label can hide

> **Direct answer:** A label can be truthful and still unspecific. "Licorice root" names a common name rather than a plant, and 甘草 names a drug class that admits three species.

Licorice is not an expensive material, so outright fraud is less common than loose naming. The risk sits in words that are technically true and practically useless.

| What the label says | What it can actually mean | How to screen it |
|---|---|---|
| "Licorice root" | Any *Glycyrrhiza* species, any origin | Ask for the binomial and the country of harvest |
| "甘草" | The pharmacopoeial drug class, three species admitted | Ask which species the lot was drawn from |
| "Root and rhizome" | A normal, honest description of the traded material | Nothing to screen; the two are harvested together |
| "Pure powder" | No added excipients, but possibly stem and soil | Ask for the sieve fraction and a lot photo |
| "Wildcrafted" | Collected, not cultivated; says nothing about age or handling | Ask for harvest date and drying method |

The naming trap is the important row. Because 甘草 is a drug category and "licorice" is an English common name, a supplier can answer every question honestly and still leave the species unidentified. The only question that closes the gap is the binomial, followed by the origin. A Chinese-origin *G. uralensis* and a Mediterranean *G. glabra* are different crops with different sweetness profiles, and a formula tuned on one will not behave identically on the other.

There is a second trap in the word "pure." A powder can be free of added starch and still carry stem fragments, sand, or root that has been partly exhausted by an earlier extraction. None of those show up in a certificate that only tests for adulterants. They show up in a sieve, a water test, and a smell.

## Pairing: where licorice helps, and where it fights

> **Direct answer:** Licorice helps most beside materials with a hard or sharp edge — pungent roots, resinous notes, warm spices — and helps least beside materials that are already soft, sweet, or low-contrast. It is a corrective, so it has no useful relationship with a blend that needs no correction.

| Partner type | Example materials | What licorice changes | Risk if overdosed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pungent root | asarum (细辛), galangal | Takes the sting off the opening; the pungency reads rounder | The pungency loses its point and the blend turns vague |
| Resin | frankincense, myrrh | Softens the balsamic edge; smoke feels less jagged | Resin notes go sticky-sweet and lose their structure |
| Warm spice | cinnamon, clove | Blends the spice into the body instead of letting it sit on top | Spice disappears behind a flat wash |
| Sweet wood | sandalwood | Little to gain; both are soft | The blend loses contrast and smells like one material |
| Heavy wood or resin | agarwood | Slight smoothing of the top; the base is unchanged | The precious material's detail is muffled |
| Herbal root | spikenard (甘松), angelica | Adds a smooth floor under a herbaceous middle | Herbal character turns muddy |

Read the table as a set of hypotheses, not rules. The judgments in the third column are editorial, drawn from how these material classes behave in blends; they are not the result of a controlled trial, and a formulator should confirm them on their own bench.

The pattern behind the rows is simple. Licorice's contribution is contrast reduction, and contrast reduction only helps where contrast is a problem. Beside a soft sandalwood, there is no hard edge to soften, so the same fraction that rescues a pungent root will simply blur a material that was already smooth. That is why the material is best understood as situational rather than universal: it is not an ingredient you add to every formula, it is an ingredient you add to a formula that has a specific fault.

## A bench protocol for a new lot

> **Direct answer:** Judge a licorice lot in a fixed order — paperwork, cut surface, cold smell, warm smell, a sliver on the tongue, then a side-by-side burn against the lot you already trust. The order matters because each step can disqualify the lot before the next one costs you time.

Start with the paper. Species, origin, harvest year, and the name of the drying operation. If the species is not stated, the rest of the protocol still runs, but the lot cannot be approved as *G. uralensis* no matter how good it seems.

Cut a piece across the root and look. A sound root shows a clean, even interior with a yellow cast; dark streaks, soft patches, or a ring of damp tissue are reasons to stop. Smell the cut face cold, then warm the piece in your hand or on a warm plate for a minute and smell again. Cold, the root is faint; warmed, a clean lot releases dry wood and a hay-like sweetness. Mustiness, scorch, or a sour edge does not improve with heat.

Taste a sliver — a sliver, not a chew, and not repeated through the day. Sweetness should arrive late and stay; a sharp sugar hit at the front of the tongue suggests something added rather than something grown.

Then grind a small sample and feel it. Powder should run dry through the fingers, not coat them. Sieve it and note the fractions. Finally, burn a test stick of a formula you know well, made once with the trusted lot and once with the candidate, and compare them side by side rather than from memory. The difference you are listening for is not "more" or "less" but whether the blend's edges softened in the same places.

Record the result against the lot number. A protocol that lives in someone's memory is not a protocol; it is a habit, and habits drift when staff change.

## Storage and handling

> **Direct answer:** Whole root keeps for years; powder keeps for months, and only while it stays dry, dark, and sealed. Buy the form you will finish.

Whole root is the stable form. Kept dry, dark, and cool, it holds sweetness for years, and its slow moisture exchange with the air is buffered by its own density. Sliced root sits in the middle: still stable, but with more surface exposed. Powder is the fragile form, and it is the one most workshops buy.

A licorice powder that has taken up moisture shows it before it smells wrong. It clumps in the jar, it leaves a film on the sieve, and it feeds into the mill unevenly. In a stick, damp powder changes the drying curve and can produce a soft core that cracks later — a defect that appears days after the batch looked fine.

Storage rules follow from the material: sealed containers, out of light, away from warm equipment; lots sized to use within months rather than a year; labels carrying species, origin, lot, and receipt date; and no topping up of old stock with new, because mixed ages destroy the lot record. Whole and sliced root keep longer, but want the same dry, dark conditions.

## Safety, in two separate routes

> **Direct answer:** Two routes need two answers: smoke and dust are the workshop hazards, and the glycyrrhizin caution belongs to ingestion, not to burning.

Any burning aromatic produces smoke and airborne particulate, and that is the exposure that matters at the brazier. Work in moving air, keep your face out of the plume, and store both raw material and finished sticks where children and animals cannot reach them. Milling adds a second exposure: root dust hangs in the air long after the grinder stops, so a fitted mask and extraction at the mill belong to normal workshop practice rather than to exceptional caution. What follows is editorial knowledge content, offered as material guidance rather than medical advice; the site's [safety guide](/safety/) sets out the general case.

Licorice carries one caution that most incense botanicals do not, and it needs to be stated precisely because it is so often stated badly. Glycyrrhizin is the same compound that gives the root its sweetness, and large, sustained intake by mouth — confectionery, tea, chewed root, concentrated extracts — is associated with raised blood pressure and lowered potassium in some people. The mechanism is established pharmacology: glycyrrhetinic acid, the aglycone formed in the gut, inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 in the kidney, so cortisol acts on mineralocorticoid receptors unchecked, producing sodium retention and potassium loss.

Two boundaries keep that fact in its proper place. First, it is a finding about ingestion, not about smoke. Burning a stick is a different route of exposure, and transplanting an oral-dose caution onto incense would be inaccurate in both directions — it would overstate the risk of burning and understate the ordinary smoke hazard that applies to every incense. Second, the caution does not make licorice dangerous in a formula; it makes dose and route the questions that matter. For a workshop, the practical rule is simple: treat the root as a material, not a chew, and keep concentrated extracts out of the blending room.

What remains, for incense, is the smoke itself. Particulate exposure depends on ventilation, burn duration, and proximity, not on which sweet root is in the stick.

## The classical record and the modern claim

> **Direct answer:** The documented facts here are botanical and textual. The medicinal reputation is traditional, and nothing on this page converts it into a clinical claim.

The documented facts about licorice in incense are narrow and solid. It is a root and rhizome of a legume. It contains glycyrrhizin. The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) puts 甘草 in its routine preparation lists and calls for 甘草末 in small quantities inside formulas. Those are historical and botanical statements, and they can be checked.

The medicinal reputation of 甘草 is a separate body of material. It belongs to traditional materia-medica vocabulary, framed in formula theory, and it is not modern clinical evidence. This page does not present it as such, and no claim of treatment or prevention is made anywhere in this entry. The interesting question for a reader is not whether the old texts said something; it is what kind of statement they were making. 调和诸药 is a statement about how a compound preparation is put together. That is a coherent idea inside formula theory, and it is the idea this material's incense role actually borrows.

That borrowing is the honest bridge between the two literatures. Licorice was not drafted into incense because someone proved a medical effect. It was drafted in because the same relational property that made it useful in a compound decoction — the ability to soften and reconcile strong materials — is useful in a compound fragrance. The transfer is structural, not pharmacological.

## FAQ

**What is licorice (甘草) in incense terms?** The underground material of *Glycyrrhiza uralensis* — root and rhizome harvested as one — from the dry belt of northern Asia. Within a 合香 formula it is a low-intensity sweet-woody corrective, measured in small amounts to soften and reconcile stronger aromatics. It is not a material that leads a blend.

**Does licorice make incense smoke smell sweet?** Not in the way the root tastes. The sweetness comes from glycyrrhizin, a large, polar saponin glycoside that does not volatilize into smoke the way aromatic terpenes do. What reaches the nose is the root's mild woody-earthy fraction, and what the blend gains is softness rather than a sugar note.

**Why is licorice dosed so low in classical formulas?** Because the *Xiangcheng* files it among routine preparation items and measures it in qian beside liang of the chief aromatics. The ratio encodes a supporting role. Dosing it high does not sweeten a blend; it flattens the contrast that makes the blend interesting.

**Is Chinese licorice the same as Mediterranean licorice?** No. The Chinese plant is *G. uralensis*; the Mediterranean one is *G. glabra*. Different species, different native ranges — and the pharmacopoeial category 甘草 admits a third, *G. inflata*. All three carry glycyrrhizin, which is exactly why the binomial, not the common name, is the specification worth writing on a purchase order.

**Can I use licorice extract or syrup instead of powder?** Not as an equivalent. Extracts and syrups are made for ingestion and flavour work; they carry sugars and water, they are sticky, and their strength is not expressed like a dry powder's. The classical formulas list 甘草末 — powder — and that remains the form a blender can control.

**Can you burn licorice on its own?** It makes poor incense by itself — a stick of the pure root gives a thin, flat, faintly woody smoke. Its value appears in company, where a small fraction changes how the stronger materials are perceived.

**Is licorice a base note?** Not in the perfumery sense. A base note is a material with its own lasting aroma that anchors a composition; licorice has little aroma to anchor with. It behaves more like a modifier that works across the whole burn, softening the top and the middle without leaving a recognizable base of its own. Calling it a base note confuses a low position in the formula's weight table with a low register in the scent.

**Does the blood-pressure caution apply to burning incense?** The documented caution concerns sustained oral intake of glycyrrhizin, not smoke exposure. Keep the two routes separate: for burning, the relevant risk is smoke and particulate matter, which calls for ventilation and sensible distance rather than a change of herb.

## Summary

甘草, "the sweet herb," is the underground material of *Glycyrrhiza uralensis*, a legume of the northern steppes. Glycyrrhizin makes the root intensely sweet on the tongue while remaining largely non-volatile in smoke — which is why a material named for sweetness works in a blend as a softener rather than as a sweet note. The *Xiangcheng* files it among routine preparation items and measures it in qian beside liang of the chief aromatics, a proportion that defines a supporting role. The commercial essentials are species (*G. uralensis* versus *G. glabra* and *G. inflata*), cleanliness, dryness, and batch consistency. The safety essentials are smoke hygiene for the burn and a precise, route-specific reading of the glycyrrhizin caution, which concerns ingestion and not incense smoke.

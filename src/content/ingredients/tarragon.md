**Direct answer:** 龙蒿 (lónghāo) names a perennial herb of the daisy family, *Artemisia dracunculus*, whose leaf smells green and sweetly of anise. It shares a genus with mugwort and sweet wormwood without behaving like either. In a burner it reads as a modern accent, borrowed from cooking and perfumery rather than from the classical 香材 tradition.

## Key facts

**Direct answer:** A perennial Artemisia with a sweet-anise leaf, two cultivated forms that differ sharply, and no recorded place in the classical incense canon.

## The taste of anise in a leaf

**Direct answer:** Tarragon smells green first and anise second. The green is cut grass and herb; the anise is a soft licorice sweetness that arrives behind it. The combination is unusual enough that the plant is easier to recognize than to describe.

Crush a leaf between the fingers. The first release is green and slightly bitter, the smell of a stem rather than a flower. A second passes, and a sweet licorice note rises through it — not the sharp anise of a seed, but a softer, leafier version. The finish is faintly bitter again, a reminder that this is an Artemisia and not a mint.

The sweetness comes chiefly from estragole, also called methyl chavicol. It is a close relative of the anethole that drives star anise and fennel, and it produces a similar licorice impression with a greener, more herbal edge. That chemical kinship explains why tarragon is often paired with anise and fennel in the kitchen, and why it can stand in for them in a blend when a softer green note is wanted.

The bitterness comes from the rest of the leaf. Artemisia species carry bitter compounds alongside their aromatic ones, and tarragon is no exception. In small amounts the bitterness gives the sweetness a floor; in large amounts it turns the herb medicinal. That balance is the first thing a cook learns and the first thing a formulator has to manage.

## Three Artemisias, three jobs

**Direct answer:** Mugwort (艾草), sweet wormwood (青蒿), and tarragon (龙蒿) are three different species in the same genus. They share a green-herbal family resemblance and almost nothing else that matters.

Mugwort, *Artemisia argyi* and its relatives, is the moxibustion herb. Its smell is strong, slightly camphoraceous, and deeply associated with 艾 use in Chinese practice. It is a material with a long ritual and domestic record, and it behaves like a dense, slightly medicinal herb in a blend.

Sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*) grows as an annual and smells camphoraceous and sharp. It is a classical materia-medica herb, and its aromatic profile is closer to a medicinal green than to a culinary one. It is not a substitute for tarragon, and tarragon is not a substitute for it.

Tarragon, *Artemisia dracunculus*, is the anise-sweet one. Its leaf is softer, its bitterness lower, and its culinary identity stronger. It is the only one of the three that a French kitchen would recognize as a seasoning.

| Herb | Species | Smell | Classical role | Modern role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mugwort (艾草) | *Artemisia argyi* and relatives | Strong, camphoraceous, medicinal | Moxibustion, 艾 incense | Herbal and ritual use |
| Sweet wormwood (青蒿) | *A. annua* | Sharp, camphoraceous, green | Materia medica | Herbal and aromatic use |
| Tarragon (龙蒿) | *Artemisia dracunculus* | Green, anise-sweet, soft | None in the incense canon | Culinary and fragrance herb |

The table is a warning as much as a map. A supplier who sells "Artemisia herb" without a species is selling a category, and the category contains three materials that smell different, behave different, and belong to different traditions. The genus name is a starting point, not a specification.

## French and Russian: one species, two materials

**Direct answer:** The two tarragons sold in commerce belong to one species, but they are distinct cultivated forms. The French form is sterile, aromatic, and raised from cuttings; the Russian form is grown from seed, coarser, and much less sweet.

The distinction is not a marketing fiction. The French form seldom produces viable seed, so growers raise it from cuttings, division, or root pieces. That keeps the aromatic profile stable, and it makes the supply slower to scale. Russian tarragon sets seed, grows more vigorously, and produces a leaf that looks similar but smells weaker and greener.

For a buyer, the difference is the whole ballgame. A dried lot labelled "tarragon" may be either form, and the Russian form can carry only a fraction of the estragole that gives the herb its identity. The leaf looks right; the smell does not. That is why the form should be part of the specification, not an afterthought.

| Feature | French tarragon | Russian tarragon |
|---|---|---|
| Propagation | Cuttings or division | Seed |
| Fertility | Sterile; rarely sets seed | Fertile |
| Leaf | Narrow, dark green, soft | Broader, coarser, paler |
| Aroma | Strong anise-sweet | Weak, greener, grassier |
| Supply | Slower, more expensive | Faster, cheaper |
| Best use | Culinary and fragrance | Bulk herb, garden plant |

The two are not grades of one another. They are different cultivated realities under one binomial, and a supplier who does not distinguish them has not specified the material.

## Why it is not in the old manuals

**Direct answer:** The classical Chinese incense canon gives tarragon no recorded role. The plant grows in China and has a Chinese name, but the tradition used other Artemisia species. Botanical presence and classical standing are separate facts.

This is the distinction the page exists to make. Flora of China includes *Artemisia dracunculus* within the country's range; the plant is not foreign. 龙蒿 is its Chinese name. None of that makes it a classical 香材. The incense manuals selected certain materials and ignored others, and tarragon was not among the selected.

The reason is not a defect in the herb. It is a difference in tradition. French cooking made tarragon a signature flavour; Chinese incense made mugwort and sweet wormwood into working materials. The same genus produced different careers in different cultures, and the careers are not transferable.

A supplier who markets tarragon as a "traditional Chinese incense herb" is borrowing a pedigree the record does not support. A supplier who calls it a modern kitchen and fragrance herb is telling the truth. The second claim is less romantic and more useful.

## What tarragon can do in a blend

**Direct answer:** Tarragon is a top-note accent. It adds a green, anise-sweet lift that brightens a blend and then leaves. It does not anchor, it does not add weight, and it does not survive rough heat.

The first thing it does is open a composition. A green anise note at the top can make a heavy blend feel lighter and more modern, and it can bridge the gap between citrus and herbal materials. It is a useful tool for a formulator who wants freshness without adding another citrus.

The second thing it does is complicate a floral. A small amount beside rose or osmanthus can give the flowers a green stem quality, as if they had been cut rather than assembled. Too much and the anise takes over, turning the flowers into a liquorice sweet.

The third thing it does is disappear. Estragole is volatile, and the green note is the first thing to leave a warm blend. A stick that smells bright on the day it is made can smell dull a month later, not because the material was bad but because its character is front-loaded. That is why tarragon is usually delivered as an oil or an accord rather than as dried leaf, and why it is dosed for the opening rather than the base.

**Chain:** leaf warmed → estragole volatilizes first → green anise lift arrives → lighter fraction is spent → bitter-green base remains → the blend reads drier and less sweet.

## The green note problem

**Direct answer:** Green notes are fragile. Heat, air, and time remove them faster than they remove resin or wood, and tarragon's charm is almost entirely in its green top.

A green note is a promise of freshness. It says the material was alive recently, and it makes a blend feel open. The promise is chemically expensive: the compounds that create it are small, volatile, and quick to oxidize. A dried herb that smelled green in the jar can smell like hay in the burner.

Tarragon is a particularly clear case because its green and its anise are the same fraction. Estragole carries both the sweetness and the lift, and when it goes, both go. What remains is the bitter Artemisia base — a dry, herbal, slightly medicinal note that is useful in its own right but is not what the buyer paid for.

The formulator's response is to treat tarragon as a top note and to build the rest of the blend without it. A fixative — a resin, a wood, a heavier root — can slow the loss, but it cannot restore what has already gone. The practical rule is to dose for the first ten minutes and accept that the last hour will be different.

## Forms: fresh, dried, oil, accord

**Direct answer:** Fresh leaf is a kitchen ingredient, dried leaf is a shadow of it, oil is a concentrate, and an accord is a constructed version. Each has a different place in the work.

| Form | What it is | Best use | What it hides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh leaf | Cut herb, high water content | Cooking, garnish, tincture | Nothing; it is the reference |
| Dried leaf | Air- or freeze-dried herb | Tea, rubs, mild blends | Loss of green note; hay character |
| Essential oil | Steam-distilled concentrate | Perfumery, flavour, incense accords | Dilution, batch variation |
| Fragrance accord | A built mixture with a tarragon profile | Consistent commercial work | The fact that it may contain no tarragon |

Fresh leaf is the benchmark. It has the full green-anise profile, and everything else is measured against it. Dried leaf loses the top note quickly; a good dry keeps some sweetness, a poor one keeps only hay. Oil is the concentrated version, and it is far stronger than the leaf; a drop goes a long way.

An accord is the commercial answer to inconsistency. It reproduces the idea of tarragon with a mixture of materials, and it can be more stable than the natural oil. The catch is that an accord is a construction, not a plant. A label that says "tarragon accord" is telling the truth; a label that says "tarragon" and contains an accord is not.

## Quality: what to check

**Direct answer:** Good tarragon smells green and sweet, with a clean anise note and a faint bitter finish. Poor tarragon smells of hay, dust, or nothing much. The form matters as much as the grade.

Start with the smell. Crush a leaf or warm a drop of oil on a strip. The green note should arrive first, followed by the anise sweetness and a soft bitter edge. A hay smell means the leaf is old or badly dried; a flat smell means the oil has oxidized or the material is the wrong form.

Then look at the leaf. Fresh French tarragon is narrow, dark green, and soft. Dried leaf should keep a green-brown colour; a faded yellow-brown lot has lost most of its character. Stems and coarse pieces are a sign of Russian material or of a low-grade sort.

For oil, check the batch and the storage. Essential oil oxidizes in light and heat, and an old oil can smell sharp or resinous rather than green. A sealed, dark bottle in a cool place is the minimum.

| Marker | Good sign | Warning sign | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smell | Green then anise, clean | Hay, dust, flat | Which cultivated form |
| Leaf colour | Green to green-brown | Faded yellow-brown | That the species is correct |
| Leaf shape | Narrow, soft, dark | Broad, coarse, pale | That it is French tarragon |
| Oil clarity | Clear, mobile | Cloudy, thick, resinous | That it is unadulterated |
| Batch record | Form and origin named | "Tarragon" only | That the lot matches the sample |

The last row is the familiar lesson. A sensory check tells you whether the material is alive; a record tells you what it is. Both are needed, and neither replaces the other.

## Culinary use, and what transfers

**Direct answer:** Tarragon is a classic French culinary herb, used in fines herbes and béarnaise. Its cooking behaviour explains both why it is loved and why it is difficult in incense.

In the kitchen, tarragon is added late. The green note is fragile, and long cooking destroys it; a béarnaise gets its tarragon at the end, and a fines herbes mixture is added just before serving. The herb is valued for the freshness it brings to fat and eggs, not for the depth it adds to a stew.

That behaviour is a warning for incense. A material that must be added late in cooking is a material that will not survive a hot burner. The green note that makes béarnaise taste bright is the same note that disappears from a stick in the first minutes.

What transfers is the idea of contrast. Tarragon cuts richness in food; in a blend it can cut heaviness, brightening a dense base with a green edge. What does not transfer is the fat. Butter carries the herb and rounds its bitterness; incense has no butter, and the bitter edge is more exposed.

## Sourcing and storage

**Direct answer:** Buy by form and, where possible, by cultivated variety. Store dried leaf away from light and heat, and treat oil as a perishable concentrate.

Fresh tarragon is a market herb, not a commodity. It is grown in gardens and market gardens, sold in bunches, and used quickly. Dried tarragon is a processed herb, and its quality depends on how it was dried and how long it has sat. Essential oil is a distilled product with a batch and a shelf life.

The form should be named in any serious offer. "Tarragon" alone does not say whether the material is French or Russian, fresh or dried, leaf or oil. Each of those choices changes the aroma and the use.

Storage is straightforward. Dried leaf goes in a sealed container away from light and heat; oil goes in a dark bottle in a cool place. Neither benefits from the refrigerator, where condensation can do more harm than good. The [incense storage guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) covers the general principles for dried aromatics.

## The French kitchen's herb

**Direct answer:** Tarragon became a signature herb of French cooking because it brightens fat without overwhelming it. Its two classic homes — fines herbes and béarnaise — show what the leaf does best.

Fines herbes is a mixture, not a single herb. The classic quartet is parsley, chives, tarragon, and chervil, chopped fine and added at the end of cooking so that the green notes survive. Tarragon supplies the anise edge in that quartet; without it, the mixture is fresh but flat.

Béarnaise shows the same logic in a different form. Tarragon goes into the vinegar reduction that flavours the sauce, and the finished emulsion carries the herb through butter and egg yolk. The fat rounds the bitterness and holds the green note; the acid keeps the whole thing bright.

Both dishes are built around timing. The herb is added late because its character is volatile, and the fat is there to carry what remains. A cook who adds tarragon at the start of a long braise gets the bitterness and loses the anise. A cook who adds it at the end gets the full leaf.

For a formulator, the lesson is structural. Tarragon is a brightener, not a base. It works when something rich is underneath it and when it is added late enough to survive. Put it in a heavy blend without a plan and it will either vanish or turn bitter.

## A green note in a warm medium

**Direct answer:** Incense is a hostile medium for green notes. Heat drives off the volatile fraction first, and what remains is the bitter, herbal base. Tarragon has to be dosed and placed with that loss in mind.

There are three workable strategies. The first is to accept the loss and dose for the opening: the blend is bright for the first minutes and settles into a drier herbal base. The second is to pair the herb with a fixative — a resin, a wood, a root — that slows the whole composition and gives the green note something to cling to. The third is to use an accord, which is built to behave consistently even if it cannot reproduce the fresh leaf exactly.

None of these strategies restores what heat has taken. A fixative slows the fade; it does not reverse it. An accord can be more stable, but it is a construction rather than a plant. The honest formulator plans for a top-note material and builds the rest of the blend to work without it.

There is a misconception worth correcting here: a higher dose does not preserve a green note. It raises the initial intensity, but the volatile fraction still leaves at the same rate, and the extra material leaves behind more bitterness. The result is a louder opening and a harsher finish. The boundary is that tarragon is a seasoning, not a structural material; treating it as a base is the mistake.

## Reading a tarragon label

**Direct answer:** A useful label names the species, the cultivated form, the product form, and the batch. "Tarragon" alone is a category, not a specification.

Species is the first field. *Artemisia dracunculus* is the herb; other Artemisia species are not tarragon, no matter how green they smell. A label that names only the genus has left the important question open.

The cultivated form is the second. The French form is the aromatic one, raised from cuttings; the Russian form is seed-grown and weaker. A label that says "tarragon" without a form may be either, and the difference is large enough to change the dose and the effect.

The product form is the third. Fresh leaf, dried leaf, essential oil, and fragrance accord are different materials. Each has a different strength, storage life, and use. A buyer who orders "tarragon oil" and receives a fragrance accord has received a constructed product, not a distillate.

| Claim | What it may mean | What to ask | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| French tarragon | The aromatic, sterile form | How it was propagated | That the lot is fresh |
| Russian tarragon | The seed-grown, weaker form | Whether it was blended | That it is inferior for every use |
| Essential oil | A distillate of the herb | Batch, origin, distillation date | That it is unadulterated |
| Fragrance oil | A constructed scent | Whether it contains any herb | That it behaves like the plant |
| Organic | A certified growing system | Certifier and certificate | That the aroma is better |

For a buyer, the practical move is to ask for the form and the variety in writing, then to smell a sample. A supplier with a real product can answer both questions. A supplier with a category will answer in adjectives.

## What "fresh" means

**Direct answer:** Fresh tarragon is a perishable herb with a short window. Dried tarragon is a different material, and the word "fresh" on a dried-leaf label is a contradiction.

A cut leaf begins to lose its volatile fraction immediately. Cell damage releases enzymes, moisture evaporates, and the green note that defines the herb starts to go. In a refrigerator, a bunch may keep for a few days; on a counter, it fades faster. The change is not bacterial spoilage but aromatic loss, which is why a leaf can look fine and smell tired.

Drying slows the loss but does not stop it. A well-dried leaf keeps some sweetness and a memory of green; a poorly dried leaf turns to hay. The colour is a rough guide: green to green-brown is alive, faded yellow-brown is not. The smell is a better guide: sweet anise at the front, not dust.

**Chain:** leaf cut → cells damaged → estragole escapes → green note fades → bitter base becomes proportionally stronger → the herb reads as hay.

Freezing preserves colour better than air-drying in some cases, but it changes the texture and does not restore the fresh aroma. For a formulator, the practical answer is to buy the form that matches the work: fresh for the kitchen, oil or accord for a blend, dried only when the hay character is acceptable.

## The name on the packet

**Direct answer:** 龙蒿 is the Chinese name for the species; "tarragon" is the English trade name; "French" and "Russian" are cultivated forms. None of those words is a full specification.

The Chinese name is a modern botanical label rather than a classical 香材 name. That is not a demotion. It tells you what the plant is, which is what a binomial is for. A classical name such as 甘松 carries a history of use; 龙蒿 carries a botanical identification. The two kinds of name do different work.

The English name has travelled through European kitchens and acquired a culinary glow. "Tarragon" on a menu suggests béarnaise and fines herbes; "tarragon" on a fragrance label suggests a green anise note. The same word covers a herb, an oil, and an accord, and the buyer has to ask which one is in the packet.

The variety names are the most useful and the most neglected. French tarragon is the aromatic form; Russian tarragon is the coarse, weak one. A label that says only "tarragon" has not chosen between them. In practice, that silence usually means the cheaper form.

There is a naming trap here worth naming explicitly. "Artemisia" is a genus with hundreds of species, and a supplier who sells "Artemisia herb" is selling a category. Tarragon is one species inside that category. The genus name is not a guarantee, and the common name is not a specification.

## Tarragon among the anise herbs

**Direct answer:** Tarragon, anise, and fennel all carry a licorice-like sweetness, but they deliver it differently. Anise is a seed, fennel is a seed-herb, and tarragon is a leaf.

Anise is the cleanest of the three. Its anethole is direct, sweet, and almost one-dimensional, which is why it works in candies and liqueurs. Fennel is softer and greener, with a vegetable quality that comes from the rest of its chemistry. Tarragon is the leafiest: its anise sits inside a green, slightly bitter herb, and the bitterness keeps the sweetness from becoming candy.

In the kitchen, the three are not interchangeable. Anise goes into sweets and spirits; fennel into sausages, fish, and salads; tarragon into egg and butter sauces. The differences are partly chemical and partly cultural, and both matter.

| Herb | Part | Anise intensity | Green character | Best kitchen use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tarragon | Leaf | Medium, soft | Strong, slightly bitter | Egg, butter, chicken |
| Anise | Seed | High, clean | Low | Sweets, spirits, bread |
| Fennel | Seed and bulb | Medium, round | Medium, vegetal | Sausage, fish, salad |

In a blend, the same logic applies. Anise gives a sharp sweet top; fennel gives a softer green sweetness; tarragon gives a green top with a bitter tail. A formulator who substitutes one for another changes the shape of the blend, not just the flavour.

## A buyer's short list

**Direct answer:** Six questions cover most of the risk: species, variety, form, origin, batch, and smell.

1. **Which species?** The answer should be *Artemisia dracunculus*. "Artemisia" is not enough.
2. **Which variety?** French or Russian. If the supplier does not know, the material is probably Russian.
3. **Which form?** Fresh, dried, oil, or accord. Each has a different strength and use.
4. **Where was it grown?** A region is better than a country, and a farm is better than a region.
5. **Which batch?** A lot number lets you compare one delivery with the next.
6. **Can I smell it?** A sample is the only way to know whether the green note survived.

The order is deliberate. Species and variety decide what the material is; form and origin decide how it behaves; batch and sample decide whether this particular lot is worth buying. A supplier who can answer all six is selling a product. A supplier who answers in adjectives is selling a hope.

## The bitter edge

**Direct answer:** Tarragon is sweet and bitter at the same time. The sweetness gets the attention; the bitterness decides how much of the herb a blend can take.

The bitter note is an Artemisia signature. It sits under the anise, and it becomes more visible as the green top fades. In a fresh leaf it reads as a pleasant herbal grip; in an old leaf it reads as hay; in an overheated blend it reads as a dry, medicinal scratch at the back of the throat.

Cooks manage the bitterness with fat. Butter, cream, and egg yolk coat the tongue and soften the edge, which is why tarragon is so often paired with rich sauces. The herb cuts through the fat, and the fat rounds the herb. It is a mutually corrective pairing, and it is the reason béarnaise works.

Incense has no fat. The bitterness is exposed, and a dose that would be balanced in a sauce can be harsh in a stick. The formulator's options are to keep the dose low, to pair the herb with a sweet resin or a creamy wood, or to accept the bitter edge as part of the profile. All three are legitimate; none of them makes the bitterness disappear.

The boundary is worth stating: tarragon is not a "green" material in the same sense as a fresh leaf of basil or parsley. It is a green material with a bitter spine. A blend that treats it as a simple top note will eventually meet the spine.

## A green accord, built and understood

**Direct answer:** A tarragon accord is a constructed scent that reproduces the idea of the herb. It is more consistent than the natural oil and less alive, and the trade-off is the point.

Accords exist because natural materials vary. A crop changes with weather, a distillation changes with the still, and a buyer who needs the same smell every month cannot rely on the leaf alone. An accord replaces that variability with a formula: a fixed set of materials that together read as "tarragon."

The construction usually works from the outside in. A green top supplies the cut-stem freshness; an anise note supplies the licorice sweetness; a bitter herbal base supplies the Artemisia spine. The result can be convincing at a distance and thin up close, because it is an illustration rather than a plant.

For an incense maker, the accord has one clear advantage: it behaves predictably in heat. The formulator knows which part will leave first and which part will stay. The disadvantage is the same as the advantage: the accord cannot surprise, and it cannot supply the small irregularities that make a natural material interesting.

A label should say which one it is. "Tarragon oil" means a distillate; "tarragon accord" means a construction. Both can be good. Confusing them is the problem.

## The smell of a cut stem

**Direct answer:** Tarragon's green note is closer to a cut stem than to a leaf. It has a sap-like freshness, a faint bitterness, and a sweetness that arrives after the green.

That order matters. A perfumer who wants to reproduce tarragon has to build the green first and the anise second, or the result reads like candy. A cook who wants to use it has to add it late, or the green goes and the candy remains.

The comparison with other green herbs is useful. Parsley is greener and less sweet; chervil is softer and more anise-like; basil is sweeter and more floral. Tarragon sits between chervil and basil, with a bitterness that neither of them has. That position is why it works in fines herbes: it adds the anise edge that the others lack without taking over the mixture.

In a blend, the cut-stem quality can be used as a bridge. It connects citrus peel to herbal notes, and it connects green florals to resins. A small amount can make a composition feel more natural; a large amount makes it feel like a garden. The difference is dose, and the dose is smaller than most people expect.

## In the still

**Direct answer:** Steam distillation turns the leaf into a concentrated oil, and the oil is not simply "stronger tarragon." Heat changes the balance, and the green top is the first thing to shift.

A distilled oil carries the volatile fraction of the leaf. Estragole comes through strongly, because it is volatile and abundant; some of the heavier green and bitter compounds come through less completely. The result is an oil that smells sweeter and more one-dimensional than the fresh herb, with less of the cut-stem freshness.

Batch variation is normal. The crop, the harvest time, the still, and the distillation run all affect the balance, and two oils from the same species can smell noticeably different. That is not a defect; it is the nature of a natural material with a biological source. It is also why commercial work often uses an accord rather than a single oil.

Storage matters more for oil than for dried leaf. Essential oils oxidize in light and heat, and an oxidized tarragon oil smells sharp, resinous, or faintly metallic rather than green. A dark bottle, a cool cupboard, and a modest stock are the practical answer.

For an incense maker, the oil is a top-note material with a known direction and an unknown shelf life. Buy small, smell before each batch, and do not assume that last year's oil will behave like this year's.

## What the leaf does not tell you

**Direct answer:** The leaf can look right and be the wrong variety, the wrong species, or a blend. Appearance is a weak test for a herb whose value is in its volatile oil.

French and Russian tarragon look similar enough that a market buyer can be fooled, especially with dried leaf. The French form tends to be narrower and darker, but the difference is not reliable across growing conditions. The smell is a better guide, and even the smell can be muted by drying.

Species confusion is a separate risk. Other Artemisia species can be sold as "tarragon" by a supplier who is careless or by one who is stretching a lot. Mugwort and sweet wormwood are both green and herbal; neither has the anise sweetness, but a dried, blended lot can hide the difference.

Dilution is the third risk. An oil can be cut with a cheaper carrier or a synthetic anethole source, and the result still smells sweet. A buyer who relies on the label alone has no way to know. A batch record, a supplier with a known still, and a smell check against a reference are the practical defences.

The pattern is the same as in every aromatic trade: the visible material is not the whole specification. The species, the variety, the form, and the batch are what make the difference, and they are exactly what a casual label leaves out.

## A note on dose

**Direct answer:** Tarragon is dosed like a seasoning, not like a base. Start below the point where the anise is obvious, and add only until the blend lifts.

The reason is perceptual. A green top note is noticed at a much lower concentration than a resin or a wood, because the nose is tuned to fresh vegetation. A dose that seems invisible in the mixing jar can be clear in the burn, and a dose that seems right in the jar can be loud in a small room. The only reliable test is a burn trial at the intended dose.

A second reason is chemical. Estragole is volatile, so the top of the curve is sharp and the tail is short. Adding more does not extend the green note; it raises the peak and leaves more bitterness behind. The curve is steep at the start and flat at the end, which is the opposite of what a "more is stronger" intuition expects.

The practical sequence is to build the blend without tarragon, then add it in small steps, smelling at each step. Stop when the blend lifts. If the next step makes the anise obvious, back up. The boundary is not a fixed percentage; it is the point where the herb stops being a seasoning and starts being a flavour.


A practical way to find the dose is to make three small sticks at three levels — one clearly too low, one near the guess, one clearly too high — and burn them side by side. The comparison teaches more than a single trial, because the nose is better at ranking than at absolute judgment.
## The leaf in the hand

**Direct answer:** Fresh tarragon is soft, narrow, and dark green, with a smell that arrives before the leaf is crushed. Dried tarragon is brittle, duller, and quieter. The hand can tell the difference before the nose confirms it.

A fresh sprig bends without breaking. The leaves are long and narrow, with a slightly oily surface and a colour that is deeper than most culinary herbs. Crushed between the fingers, it leaves a green stain and a scent that lingers on the skin for a minute or two.

A dried leaf is brittle and papery. It crumbles easily, and the colour has usually moved from green toward olive or brown. The smell is sweeter and flatter, with the green note reduced and the hay note increased. It is still tarragon, but it is a memory of tarragon.

The hand test is not a substitute for a species check, but it is a useful first pass. A leaf that feels coarse and broad is more likely to be the Russian form; a leaf that is narrow and soft is more likely to be French. Neither observation is conclusive, and neither replaces the label.

## Vinegar, salad, and the green note

**Direct answer:** Tarragon vinegar is the herb preserved in acid. The acid draws the aromatic fraction out of the leaf and holds it in solution, which is why the vinegar smells green long after a dried leaf has gone flat.

The technique is old and simple. Fresh sprigs are steeped in vinegar for days or weeks, then removed; the liquid keeps the anise-green character and can be used in dressings, sauces, and pickles. The acid does two jobs: it extracts the volatile compounds and it slows the oxidation that would otherwise turn the leaf to hay.

That behaviour is a clue for anyone thinking about tarragon in a blend. The herb's character is soluble and mobile; it moves into fat, alcohol, and acid, and it leaves the leaf as it goes. A dried leaf that has lost its smell has not lost the compounds so much as surrendered them to the air. A vinegar or an oil can capture them; a open jar cannot.

For incense, the lesson is about volatility again. The same compounds that migrate into vinegar are the ones that leave a stick in the first minutes. There is no acid in a burner to hold them, and no fat to slow them. The green note is a guest that arrives early and leaves early, and the formulator plans around that.

## Safety

**Direct answer:** Any burning herb sends smoke and fine grit into the air, and tarragon is no exception. Essential oil is a concentrate and should be handled as one.

The safety story for tarragon is quieter than for star anise, but it is not empty. A dried herb burned as incense produces combustion products; a room needs air, and the burner needs a stable place away from children and pets. An essential oil is far stronger than the leaf and should not be treated as a food flavouring or applied undiluted to the skin without proper guidance.

This page makes no medical claims. Tarragon has a culinary and aromatic history, not a clinical one, and nothing here should be read as a treatment for any condition. General precautions for smoke, heat, and open flame are collected in the [safety guide](/safety/).

## Summary

龙蒿 (*Artemisia dracunculus*) is a daisy-family perennial whose narrow leaf carries a green, anise-sweet scent built on estragole. French and Russian forms are one species split into two commercial materials, and the gap decides whether a lot is worth buying. The classical incense canon does not include the herb; its place in a burner is modern, usually as an oil or an accord. The note is a top note: it lifts, then leaves, and the bitter Artemisia spine remains.

## FAQ

**Is dried tarragon worth using?** It is a different material, not a substitute for fresh. A good dry keeps some sweetness and a hint of green; a poor one tastes of hay. For a salad or a sauce that depends on freshness, use fresh leaf or oil. For a long-cooked dish where the herb is a background note, a decent dry can work.

**What is tarragon (龙蒿)?** It is *Artemisia dracunculus*, a perennial herb in the daisy family. The narrow leaves smell green and anise-like, and French cooking made the plant a classic. In this encyclopedia it is treated as a modern culinary and fragrance material, not a classical Chinese 香材.

**Is tarragon the same as mugwort?** No. They are different species in the same genus. Tarragon is *Artemisia dracunculus*, with a soft anise-sweet leaf; mugwort is *Artemisia argyi* and its relatives, with a stronger, more camphoraceous smell and a moxibustion tradition. The family resemblance is real; the materials are not interchangeable.

**What separates tarragon from sweet wormwood?** Sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*) is an annual whose green smell is camphoraceous and medicinal. Tarragon is a perennial with an anise-sweet leaf and a culinary role. They share a genus and a green-herbal family character, not a function.

**How do French and Russian tarragon differ?** The French form is sterile, raised from cuttings, and strongly anise-sweet. The Russian form is grown from seed, coarser, and far less aromatic. They are the same species, but they are different materials in the kitchen and in a blend.

**Does tarragon work in incense?** As a modern green accent, yes, usually as an essential oil or an accord rather than dried leaf. Its note is volatile and sits at the top of a blend, so it brightens rather than anchors. The classical Chinese incense record does not include it, and a formula that uses it should describe itself as modern.

**Can I grow tarragon at home?** Yes, if you start with the right form. French tarragon is raised from cuttings or divisions, because it rarely sets viable seed; Russian tarragon can be grown from seed. A garden plant labelled "tarragon" from seed is likely the Russian form, which is hardier and much less aromatic. For cooking, buy a named French plant.

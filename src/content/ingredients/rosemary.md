**Direct answer:** Rosemary (迷迭香, mídiéxiāng) is the aromatic shrub *Salvia rosmarinus*, long listed as *Rosmarinus officinalis*, a Mediterranean member of the mint family grown for its needle-like leaves. In incense it works as a dry, camphoraceous top note rather than a base, and its Chinese record is a single narrow thread: an imported court curiosity of the third century, not a workshop staple.

## The name records an arrival, not a description

**Direct answer:** 迷迭香 is a transcription of a foreign plant name, not a Chinese phrase describing the herb. The characters carry sound rather than sense, which is why the name reads oddly and why it is a reliable marker of an imported material.

Chinese has a long habit of taking plant names in by ear. 茉莉 for jasmine, 葡萄 for grape, 苜蓿 for alfalfa — all entered the language as approximations of foreign words, carried along trade routes from Central Asia and beyond. 迷迭香 belongs to that family of names. It does not tell you what the plant looks like, where it grows, or what it does. It tells you that someone, at some point, heard a word for it and wrote down the closest Chinese syllables.

That detail is more than etymology. A descriptive name such as 沉香 ("sinking wood") or 龙脑 ("dragon brain") encodes a physical property or a trade grade. A transcribed name encodes only contact. The plant arrives with the name and with a story about where it came from, and the story is usually about distance and rarity rather than about use.

The scientific name has a parallel history. *Rosmarinus officinalis* is the name most people still recognise, and it appears on labels, in pharmacopoeias, and in older gardening books. Molecular work on the mint family showed that the genus *Rosmarinus* sits inside *Salvia*, so the plant was moved and is now accepted as *Salvia rosmarinus*. Both names point at one species. A supplier who switches between them has changed a label, not a material.

## What the plant is, and why its genus disappeared

**Direct answer:** *Salvia rosmarinus* is an evergreen shrub with woody stems, narrow revolute leaves and blue-violet flowers, native to the dry hills of the Mediterranean basin. It is not a Chinese native, has no treatment in the Flora of China, and its identity rests on Mediterranean botany and on the international taxonomic backbone.

The leaf is the useful part, and its anatomy explains why. Rosemary leaves are tough, narrow, and rolled under at the margins — an adaptation to strong sun and dry summers. The oil glands sit mainly on the underside, protected in pits. A leaf handled roughly or ground while warm loses oil quickly, which is the first thing to understand about the material's economics.

The old genus *Rosmarinus* held three species. Molecular phylogenetics showed that this small group nested within *Salvia*, the sages, and the genus was merged. The change upset nobody in the trade, because the plant had always been sold by its common name and its smell. It did create a paperwork problem: a specification that names one synonym and a certificate that names the other can look like a mismatch to a clerk who does not know the taxonomy.

Rosemary's native range runs across the Mediterranean — southern Europe, North Africa, and the eastern Mediterranean coast. It grows on rocky slopes, in maquis scrub, and on dry coastal hills, often in thin soil where few other shrubs establish. The plant in a garden is usually larger and leafier than the plant on a hillside, and the oil from cultivated material is not identical to the oil from wild stands. That difference becomes important when a buyer asks what "rosemary oil" actually contains.

## The classical record: short, elite, and about clothing

**Direct answer:** 香乘 preserves two older quotations placing 迷迭香 in the Western Regions and in 大秦, and a note that it was transplanted to the court of 魏文帝, Cao Pi. The recorded use is an aromatic worn on the body to scent clothing — not a recipe, a dose, or a burning instruction.

The quotations are worth reading closely because they show what kind of object rosemary was in third-century China. 广志 says 出西域, "from the Western Regions". 魏略 says 出大秦国 — 大秦 being the Chinese name for the Roman sphere, the eastern Mediterranean world reached through Parthian and Central Asian intermediaries. Both statements are about origin, not about technique.

The plant was impressive enough to be moved. The record notes that it was transplanted to the imperial court in the 魏文帝 period, and the phrase attached to it — 可佩服，令人衣香 — describes something worn about the person so that the clothes carry its scent. That is a sachet use, or a sprig tucked into clothing, not a censer use. The Wei court also produced a rhapsody on the plant (迷迭香赋), which tells you it was treated as a cultivated curiosity worthy of literary attention.

What the record does not contain is just as informative. There is no proportion, no preparation method, no instruction to grind it with sandalwood or to seal it in a jar. A material that had entered the recipe canon would leave traces of that kind. Rosemary left a name, an origin story, and a court anecdote.

The honest reading, then, is that rosemary is **documented but not adopted**. Presence in a text is not the same as a place in practice. That distinction matters because modern marketing tends to convert any classical mention into heritage, and a single third-century note cannot carry the weight of a recipe tradition.

## Why it never entered the recipe canon

**Direct answer:** The classical Chinese incense canon is organised around materials that smoulder and hold heat — agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, myrrh, benzoin, borneol — with a supporting cast of roots and aromatics such as 甘松 and 藿香. Rosemary had no trade network, no religious use, and no processing tradition behind it, so it stayed a curiosity.

The canon's structure explains the exclusion. A classical blend is built like a small administration: a chief material that carries the composition, ministers that support it, an assistant that modifies a facet, and a guide that pushes the scent outward. That grammar works with materials that release slowly and predictably. A thin leaf that gives up its oil in one burst does not fit the role of a chief, and it is too loud to be a quiet assistant.

Materials that did enter the canon arrived with infrastructure. Frankincense and myrrh came with religious communities that burned them daily and with caravan routes that made them reliable. Sandalwood came with a shipping trade and a carving industry. 甘松 came from the steppe and had a long medicinal and aromatic record. Each had a supply chain, a set of handlers, and a body of practice that told a blender how it behaved.

Rosemary had a story and a plant. It arrived as a gift or a curiosity, was planted at court, and then largely disappeared from the record until modern commerce revived it. That is a thin basis for a tradition, and it is why the material's modern presence in Chinese incense is better described as a crossover than as a survival.

## One oil, four chemotypes

**Direct answer:** "Rosemary oil" names a distillation, not a fixed chemical composition. Different populations produce camphor-dominant, cineole-dominant, verbenone-dominant and bornyl-acetate-dominant oils, and those are different materials in scent, in blending, and in safety.

The plant adapts chemically as well as physically. Populations separated by climate and soil produce different ratios of the same handful of compounds — 1,8-cineole, camphor, alpha-pinene, camphene, borneol, bornyl acetate, limonene, verbenone. A distiller who sends a batch to market is sending one particular ratio, and the buyer who does not ask which one is buying an unknown.

| Chemotype | Marker compound | Aroma read | Often associated with | Best role | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Camphor type | Camphor | Sharp, medicinal, mothball-adjacent | Spain, Portugal | Industrial and functional fragrance; strong repellent note | Overwhelms florals; the most safety-relevant type |
| Cineole type | 1,8-Cineole | Clean, eucalyptus-fresh, penetrating | Tunisia, Morocco | Herbal top note, room sprays, stick accents | Stacks badly with eucalyptus; reads medicinal fast |
| Verbenone type | Verbenone | Softer, greener, less aggressive | Corsica, Sardinia | Fine fragrance, subtle herbal lifts | Scarce and costly; frequently mislabelled |
| Bornyl acetate type | Bornyl acetate | Balsamic, piney, gentler | Northern and garden populations | Sachets, soft herbal blends | Low projection in a burning stick |

The camphor type is the one that matters most for safety. Camphor is a volatile solid with a strong physiological effect, and a camphor-rich oil is a different proposition from a cineole-rich one. The cineole type is the one most people recognise as "rosemary" in a kitchen or a diffuser. The verbenone type is the perfumer's choice and the rarest on a commodity price list.

For an incense maker the practical consequence is simple. A specification that says "rosemary oil" without a chemotype, an origin, and a batch analysis is not a specification. It is a hope. The same weight of oil from two suppliers can push a blend in opposite directions.

## Aroma and pairing

**Direct answer:** Rosemary reads as a dry, green, camphoraceous top note with a piney edge and a faint resinous tail. It lifts a blend without sweetening it, which is why it pairs with other dry herbs, with citrus, and with resins that need opening up.

The effect is best understood as contrast. Most floral and resinous materials are heavy and sweet; rosemary is light and sharp. A small amount at the top of a blend makes the heart feel clearer, the way a squeeze of citrus makes a rich dish taste lighter. Too much and the whole composition turns medicinal, because camphor and cineole dominate everything they touch.

| Partner | What it contributes | Balance note |
|---|---|---|
| Bay leaf | Spicy-eucalypt heat | Reinforces the top; together they can read like a liniment |
| Eucalyptus | More cineole | Stacking two cineole materials flattens into "cold" and loses the herb |
| Mint | Cool sweetness | Softens rosemary's dryness; mint can take over if it leads |
| Lavender | Floral-herbal bridge | Shared linalool makes the transition smooth |
| Cedarwood | Dry woody base | Rosemary lifts the opening; cedar holds the tail |
| Labdanum or benzoin | Sweet resin | Rosemary cuts sweetness that would otherwise cloy |
| Black pepper | Terpene spice | Adds heat without sugar; good with resins |
| Lemon or bergamot | Citrus top | Extends the green freshness into the first minute |

Two rules follow from the table. First, do not stack materials that share the same dominant compound; the result is not twice the freshness but a single blunt note. Second, put rosemary where its volatility can be used — at the top of the blend, not buried in the base, where its sharpness will be cooked out before it can do anything.

## Rosemary is not 龙脑: a camphor confusion map

**Direct answer:** Rosemary smells camphoraceous, but it is not camphor and it is not 龙脑. Those are single crystalline substances with defined chemistry and strong physiological effects; rosemary is a leaf whose oil contains camphor as one component among many.

The confusion is easy to make and expensive to act on. A buyer smelling a sharp, cooling, medicinal top note may conclude that the material is "like camphor" and substitute accordingly. The substitution changes the formula, the safety profile, and the price logic all at once.

| Material | What it is | Dominant volatile | Aroma | Role in a classical blend | Safety note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rosemary | Leaf of *Salvia rosmarinus* | 1,8-Cineole, camphor, alpha-pinene | Green, piney, camphoraceous | Modern accent; not a canon material | Oil varies by chemotype; dilute for skin |
| 龙脑 (borneol) | Crystalline monoterpene alcohol | Borneol | Cool, camphoraceous, slightly sweet | Classical guide material, used in small amounts | Concentrated; not a casual additive |
| 樟脑 (camphor) | Crystalline monoterpene ketone | Camphor | Sharp, medicinal, mothball-adjacent | Limited use; strong and pervasive | Neurotoxic in excess, especially to children |
| Eucalyptus oil | Distillate of *Eucalyptus* leaves | 1,8-Cineole | Clean, penetrating, cooling | Modern top note | Not for ingestion; keep from infants |
| Mint | Leaf of *Mentha* species | Menthol | Cool, sweet, grassy | Minor accent | Dilute; avoid near the face of children |

The useful distinction is between a material and a compound. 龙脑 and 樟脑 are isolated solids that a blender weighs on a scale in fractions of a gram. Rosemary is a plant matrix in which camphor might be a few percent of the oil, alongside cineole, pinene and a dozen other constituents. The behaviour of the whole is not the behaviour of its loudest part.

A formulator who wants more "sharpness" can add camphor to a rosemary accord. That produces a note that smells stronger and behaves worse: the camphor dominates the top, the herbal character flattens, and the safety margin narrows. The better route is to choose a camphor-type rosemary oil and accept its natural balance, or to move to a cineole type if the goal is freshness rather than force.

## From leaf to stick

**Direct answer:** Rosemary reaches incense as whole dried leaf, as ground powder, and as essential oil. Leaf burns slowly and unevenly, powder packs into a binder, and oil volatilises fast; the choice of form is really a decision about where in the burn the note should appear.

Harvest timing matters more than most buyers expect. The oil content peaks around flowering, when the plant is putting energy into the tops, and material cut at that point is stronger than material cut from old growth. Drying is usually done in shade with air movement, because sun and heat drive off the lighter fraction and leave a flatter, hay-like leaf.

Grinding is where the aroma is quietly lost. Whole leaf keeps its glands intact; a mill fractures them and exposes the oil to air and to the heat of the grinding itself. The chain runs: fresh leaf → high moisture → slow shade drying → intact glands → milling fractures the epidermis → surface area multiplies → oxidation and evaporation accelerate → the powder smells weaker than the leaf it came from, within weeks.

Distillation follows the same logic at larger scale. Steam ruptures the glands, the vapour carries the volatiles to a condenser, and the oil separates from the water. The lighter compounds travel first, so the early fraction is sharper and more cineole-forward while the later fraction is heavier and more camphoraceous. Whether the distiller blends the whole run or keeps cuts is one of the hidden variables behind two oils that carry the same name.

For stick making, the powder is blended with a binder and a wood base, wetted, formed, and dried. The mechanics of that process sit in the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) guide; what matters here is that rosemary powder is a minor ingredient in a structure dominated by binder and base. It modifies the top of the burn rather than defining it.

## The kitchen herb and the censer herb

**Direct answer:** Culinary rosemary and aromatic rosemary come from the same species but not from the same supply chain. Cooking grade is judged on flavour, colour and food handling; aromatic grade is judged on oil content and chemotype, and the two do not automatically overlap.

A spice jar and a distiller's sack can hold material from the same harvest and still be different products. Culinary buyers want leaf that tastes right, looks clean, and meets food-safety expectations for handling, drying and storage. Aromatic buyers want oil content, a known chemotype, and material that will give up its volatiles in a still. A low-oil cultivar that tastes excellent in a roast is a poor distillation feedstock; a high-oil selection can be harsh on the palate.

This is a real boundary, and it cuts both ways. A kitchen herb is not automatically a good incense material, because it may be old, mild, or selected for flavour rather than aroma. An oil-grade leaf is not automatically food-safe, because the drying and handling that suit distillation may not meet food standards. The words "rosemary" on two packages do not promise the same thing.

For a small maker, the practical answer is to buy from a supplier who states the intended use. For a reader, the useful habit is to stop treating "natural" and "culinary" as quality guarantees. They describe a category, not a grade.

## What burning does to a herbal top note

**Direct answer:** A smouldering stick destroys most of rosemary's fresh top before it reaches the room. What survives is the heavier part of the oil plus the smoke of the leaf powder, which is why the burning material often smells more like a bonfire with a pine edge than like the herb on the chopping board.

The mechanism is the same one that governs every aromatic plant material, and it is worth stating plainly. A coal front runs hot, well above the boiling range of the oil's components. Material ahead of the front is heated by conduction and by the gas stream, so its volatiles evaporate before the fire arrives and rise with the smoke. Everything the fire reaches is pyrolysed into char and smoke compounds. The scent you get is a race between distillation and destruction.

- **Leaf powder in the matrix.** Ground leaf → cellulose-rich particles → the front crosses them in seconds → sugars caramelise and cellulose chars → the green top is replaced by a dry, smoky, resinous note.
- **Oil on the surface.** A dipped or sprayed stick → the oil sits as a film → the warm zone volatilises it early → rosemary peaks in the first minutes → the rest of the stick is base and smoke.
- **Oil in the core.** Oil mixed into the damp mass → binder and wood insulate it → release is slower and cooler → the note lasts longer but loses its sharpness.

A sachet avoids the problem entirely. At room temperature the oil evaporates slowly, and camphor — a solid volatile — sublimes gradually, so a linen bag keeps a clean herbal smell for months. The same chemistry that makes the leaf a poor solo burn makes it an excellent cold-air material.

There is a middle route, too. 隔火熏香, the indirect method in which material is warmed over a mica plate or a bed of ash rather than ignited, keeps the temperature below the pyrolysis range. Rosemary responds well to it: the cineole and camphor come off cleanly, and the harsh smoke note never develops.

## Quality signals

**Direct answer:** For leaf, quality is freshness and oil content: grey-green colour, intact needles, and a strong smell when rubbed. For oil, quality is chemotype and authenticity, not colour or the word "pure" on the label.

| Attribute | Good sign | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Colour | Even grey-green | Khaki, brown, or bleached patches |
| Leaf integrity | Whole needles, few broken pieces | Shattered, dusty, stem-heavy |
| Aroma when rubbed | Sharp, clean, pine-cineole | Hay, dust, mustiness |
| Moisture | Dry and brittle, no clumping | Soft, cool to the touch, clumps |
| Powder | Free-flowing, aromatic when disturbed | Damp, dull, smells of cardboard |
| Oil | Stated chemotype, batch analysis, clear origin | "Pure rosemary oil" with no further detail |

Rosemary has no formal grading ladder, and it does not need one. It is an abundant crop, not a scarce one, so the meaningful distinctions are freshness, cleanliness, and — for the oil — chemotype. A beautiful leaf with no smell is worse than a plain leaf that fills the room when crushed.

One confusion is worth clearing. The name change from *Rosmarinus* to *Salvia* is taxonomy, not quality. A supplier selling "Rosmarinus officinalis oil" is not selling an older or better material; they are using an older synonym. Judge the batch, not the label's vintage.

## Safety and dosing without a drop count

**Direct answer:** There is no single safe drop count for rosemary oil, because the material varies by chemotype and the route of exposure changes the risk entirely. For incense, the risk is smoke and soot; for skin, irritation and sensitisation; for ingestion, camphor toxicity. Each route needs its own answer.

The reason a number is misleading is that "rosemary oil" spans compositions from a mild cineole-forward oil to a camphor-rich one. Camphor is the compound that drives the serious concerns: swallowed in quantity it can cause seizures, and it is especially dangerous for young children. A drop count taken from one oil tells you nothing about another.

| Route | What "dose" means | What a specification should state | What no page should claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burning incense | Mass of material burned per room volume, plus ventilation | Material load, binder, burn rate, intended room size | That any burn is "clean" or risk-free |
| Sachet or passive inhalation | Duration and proximity, not drops | Filling weight and replacement interval | That a sachet is a treatment |
| Topical | Dilution in a carrier, plus patch test | Concentration, carrier, allergen declaration | That neat oil is safe on skin |
| Ingestion | Not a domestic dose at all | Nothing — this is outside incense use | Any internal-use instruction |

For skin, the aromatherapy literature usually cites a dilution range of one to two percent in a carrier oil for adult use, and that figure is a convention of practice rather than a universal safe dose. It assumes a healthy adult, intact skin, and a limited area. It does not cover children, pregnancy, broken skin, or a camphor-rich oil used over a large area. A patch test on the inner forearm is the minimum precaution.

For burning, forget drops and think about air. Any incense load adds fine particulate matter and carbon monoxide to a room, and an oil-rich stick adds more because the oil burns hot and can produce soot. A stick that has been dipped rather than mixed can flare when lit. Keep the burner stable, keep it away from curtains and shelves, burn one piece at a time, and ventilate. Our [safety guide](/safety/) covers the room-level rules.

Three cautions apply regardless of route. Essential oil is not a food and should never be ingested without qualified supervision. Keep it locked away from children, in its original container. Anyone pregnant, breastfeeding, epileptic, or responsible for a small child should get qualified advice before using a camphor-containing oil at all.

The point is not that rosemary is dangerous. It is that the word covers several materials, and a safety statement that does not name the chemotype is not a safety statement. Precision here is not pedantry; it is the difference between a useful caution and a false reassurance.

## A specification checklist

**Direct answer:** A workable rosemary specification names the plant, the part, the chemotype, the form, and the analysis. Anything less leaves the buyer unable to compare one lot with another.

| Field | What to record | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Salvia rosmarinus* (syn. *Rosmarinus officinalis*) | Prevents a synonym dispute at goods-in |
| Part | Leaf, or flowering tops for oil | Leaf and top material differ in oil content |
| Origin | Country and, where known, region | Origin is a rough proxy for chemotype |
| Chemotype | Camphor, cineole, verbenone, or bornyl acetate | Determines scent, blending behaviour, and caution |
| Form | Whole leaf, crushed, powder, or oil | Each behaves differently in a stick |
| Analysis | Batch GC, allergen declaration | Makes the claim checkable |
| Intended use | Stick, cone, sachet, or oil blend | Sets the relevant safety framing |

A buyer who fills this table for two suppliers can compare them. A buyer who cannot fill it is negotiating on price alone, which is exactly where a camphor-rich oil gets sold as a premium herb.

## Summary

Rosemary (迷迭香) is *Salvia rosmarinus*, the Mediterranean shrub long sold as *Rosmarinus officinalis*, used in incense as a dry camphoraceous top note rather than a base. Its Chinese record is genuine but thin — 香乘 quotes 广志 and 魏略, places the plant in the Western Regions and 大秦, and notes its transplanting to the court of 魏文帝, where it scented clothing rather than censers. The plant never entered the recipe canon because it arrived without a supply chain or a body of practice. Modern use is a crossover, and the material's real complexity is chemical: four chemotypes behave like four different oils, which is why "rosemary oil" is a category, not a specification.

## FAQ

**Is rosemary native to China?** No. It is a Mediterranean shrub, and it has no treatment in the Flora of China. The Chinese name 迷迭香 is a transcription, and the classical record places the plant in the Western Regions and 大秦 — the Roman sphere reached through Central Asia. Its appearance at the Wei court was an import, not a local harvest.

**Why does rosemary have two scientific names?** Because the genus was merged into *Salvia* after molecular work showed that *Rosmarinus* nested inside it. The earlier label is *Rosmarinus officinalis*; the accepted one is *Salvia rosmarinus*. Both refer to the same plant, and a label using either is not making a quality claim.

**Does rosemary incense smell like the fresh herb?** Less than people expect. The fresh top note is the first thing the coal front destroys, so what reaches the room is the heavier, resinous part of the leaf plus smoke. The result reads drier and smokier than the herb on the board, closer to a pine bonfire than to a kitchen.

**What is a chemotype, and why does it matter?** A chemotype is a population of one species that produces a distinct chemical profile. Rosemary has camphor-rich, cineole-rich, verbenone-rich and bornyl-acetate-rich types, and they differ in scent and in safety. A supplier who cannot name the chemotype cannot tell you what the oil will do in a blend.

**Can I add rosemary oil to a stick myself?** You can, but the result is uneven. Surface oil flashes off early, so the first minutes are strong and the rest of the stick is base. Oil also makes the material burn hotter and sootier. If you want the note to last, mix a small amount into the damp mass rather than coating the finished stick.

**Is rosemary related to sage?** Yes, closely. The genus merge put rosemary beside culinary sage (*Salvia officinalis*) in *Salvia*. They share a family and a set of aromatic compounds, but their profiles differ sharply: rosemary is camphoraceous and piney, while sage is savoury, warm and slightly bitter.

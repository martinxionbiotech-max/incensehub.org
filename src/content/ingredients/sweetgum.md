> **Direct answer:** Sweetgum (枫香, fēngxiāng) is the balsamic resin drawn from the Chinese sweetgum tree, *Liquidambar formosana* (family Altingiaceae). It contributes a sweet, warm, balsamic note to incense and perfumery, sits near but apart from the imported storax (苏合香), and carries a classical record as a material that could be passed off as frankincense.

## The Source Tree, in Botanical Detail

**Direct answer:** The ingredient comes from *Liquidambar formosana*, the Chinese sweetgum — a tree of the sweetgum family (Altingiaceae, which earlier taxonomies grouped under Hamamelidaceae) — and it is the balsamic resin 枫香脂 exuded by wounded bark, never the timber.

Flora of China sets out the tree's dimensions and habits. A mature specimen can climb to 30 m, with a trunk up to 1 m thick and sheathed in gray-brown bark. Its leaves are broadly ovate, split into three palmately arranged lobes carried on three veins, and bordered with a glandular-serrate edge. Male flowers group in short spikes, while each female inflorescence bears 24–43 blossoms that later ripen into a globose fruit head 3–4 cm wide. Flowering runs March to June and fruiting July to September. The tree favors sunny ground, village edges, and montane forest from 500 to 800 m, its natural range stretching across Anhui, Fujian, Guangdong, Guizhou, Hainan, Hubei, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Taiwan, and Zhejiang, and onward into Korea, Laos, and northern Vietnam. The fragrant part is the resin tapped from wounded bark, not the wood itself. See [Liquidambar formosana in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200010557) and [Liquidambar formosana on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/3702542).

## One Tree, Several Names

**Direct answer:** The single word 枫香 names both the tree and, loosely, its resin — but the resin has its own precise term, 枫香脂 (fēngxiāngzhī), and the classical *Xiangcheng* (香乘) preserves an even older label, 白胶香 ("white gum fragrance").

Three terms sit at different levels of precision:

- **枫香 (fēngxiāng)** — the tree, and loosely the whole material.
- **枫香脂 (fēngxiāngzhī)** — the resin itself, the fragrant substance with commercial value.
- **白胶香 (báijiāoxiāng)** — "white gum fragrance," an older name the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records for 枫香脂, which the *Golden Light Sutra* transliterates as 须萨析罗婆.

Because the tree and the resin share the word 枫香, a label can read ambiguously. In practice a label almost always points to the resin (枫香脂), since that is the part carrying fragrance value; the sweetgum's wood is not an incense material in the same sense. This naming split does not mean the wood is worthless — it simply means the two parts are sold and used for different purposes, and the point is to know which one a label names.

## How the Resin Forms and Is Collected

**Direct answer:** Sweetgum resin is a wound response, not a wood harvest — bark is cut → the tree exudes a balsamic resin to seal the injury → the exudate hardens into tears or a soft mass → it is collected and dried → the result is 枫香脂 ready for burning or blending.

That sequence dictates how the material behaves in the workshop. It shows up as resin, not chips or powder, so it is worked by melting and warming rather than by the grinding path used for woods and roots. The same logic applies to other tree resins on this site; see [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/) for the wider resin-handling picture.

## Aroma Profile: A Base Note, Not a Top

**Direct answer:** Sweetgum reads as a sweet, warm, balsamic base with resinous depth — soft and comforting, in the broad family of benzoin but with its own character — and it supports a blend rather than leading it.

The scent sits as a base or supporting note, never a bright top. It rounds out and warms heavier woods and resins instead of announcing itself first. The classical *Xiangcheng* (香乘) offers a sharp pointer to that character: 枫香 looks so close to frankincense (乳香) that Li Shizhen observed the two are told apart only by burning. That observation tells us the material reads as a warm, resinous, frankincense-adjacent note, not a sharp or floral one — a fact that matters for blending and for the substitution problem treated below.

## Sweetgum vs Storax vs Frankincense

**Direct answer:** Three resins sit side by side and get mixed up — sweetgum (枫香脂), storax (苏合香), and frankincense (乳香) — and the confusion is a species problem, not a name problem.

| Resin | Source tree | Origin | How it relates to the others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sweetgum (枫香脂) | *Liquidambar formosana* | native Chinese sweetgum | the native resin; recorded as an adulterant of frankincense |
| Storax (苏合香) | *Liquidambar orientalis* | Oriental sweetgum of Asia Minor | the imported balsam; shares the *Liquidambar* genus |
| Frankincense (乳香) | *Boswellia* species | Arabia and the Horn of Africa | the high-value target that 枫香 was used to fake |

The lesson of the table is subtle: two of these resins share a genus (*Liquidambar*) while the third belongs to an entirely different family, yet the one sharing the genus is not the one that got passed off. Sweetgum and storax are related resins from related trees, not the same product; sweetgum and frankincense are botanically unrelated but were made to pass for each other in trade. When a label says "sweetgum" or "storax," the species decides the exact aroma, origin, and price — see [styrax-resin](/ingredients/styrax-resin/) and [frankincense](/ingredients/frankincense/).

## Sweetgum Among the Sweet Resins

**Direct answer:** Within the family of sweet, balsamic resins, sweetgum holds a specific niche — warmer and more resinous than benzoin, more balsamic than pine resin, and softer than storax — which is exactly what makes it useful as a rounding agent.

A cross-map places the material against its aromatic neighbors:

| Resin | Character | Role sweetgum plays relative to it |
|---|---|---|
| Benzoin | sweet, vanilla-like, soft | sweetgum is warmer and more resinous, less confectionary |
| Storax (苏合香) | rich, cinnamon-balsamic, honeyed | sweetgum is softer and rounder, less spicy |
| Pine resin | sharp, terpenic, coniferous | sweetgum is smoother and more balsamic, less turpentine |
| Frankincense | citrus-woody, resinous, lemony | sweetgum is sweeter and less bright; the two are burn-distinguished |

This cross-map is a reasoning aid, not a grading system. Because sweetgum reads warm and balsamic without the sharp edges of pine resin or the brightness of frankincense, it settles naturally into the base of a blend, where it can soften and round heavier materials. ## A Field Guide to Grading Sweetgum Resin

**Direct answer:** Quality in 枫香脂 runs along a gradient from clear and clean to dark and woody — the clearer the tears, the better the grade — and there is no formal commercial ladder, only a durable visual and olfactory signal.

| Grade signal | What you see | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Clear tears ("通明") | translucent, pale, glassy lumps | the classical good grade; high resin, low debris |
| Soft balsamic mass | sticky, aromatic, pliable | sound material, harder to store cleanly |
| Dark or cloudy tears | opaque, brownish, mottled | age, oxidation, or contamination — test before use |
| Woody or debris-laden | bark fragments, grit, dull color | low resin content; the filler outweighs the fragrance |

The "通明枫香" (clear 枫香) that the *Xiangcheng* names as the good grade is not an abstract ideal — it is the top of this gradient. A buyer reads the same four rows when sorting a shipment: clarity, aroma, purity, and burn character. No standardized ladder exists, but "clear, clean, and aromatic" is the durable signal, and the table above is the practical way to apply it. The common misconception is that a darker, older-looking resin must be better; the opposite is often true, since darkening can just as easily mark oxidation or contamination.

## A 500-Year-Old Supply-Chain Warning

**Direct answer:** The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records a sharp practical fact — 枫香 was used to adulterate frankincense, and Li Shizhen noted the two are told apart only by burning — which reads today as a classical buyer-intelligence warning, not a historical curiosity.

The most useful classical observation about 枫香 is a supply-chain caution. The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records, quoting Li Shizhen: "乳香，今人多以枫香杂之，惟烧时可辨" — today people adulterate frankincense with 枫香, and the two are told apart only by burning. It adds that both 枫香 and pine resin could pass for frankincense — "枫香、松脂皆可乱乳香，但枫香微白黄色，烧之可见真" — but 枫香 is slightly whitish-yellow, and burning reveals the truth.

The principle cuts both ways. 枫香 is close enough to frankincense to serve as an adulterant, which means a buyer must separate 枫香 from frankincense on one side, and from the imported storax on the other. The burn test is the classical discriminator, and it still applies.

## Why Burning Is the Classical Test

**Direct answer:** Burning works as a test because 枫香 and frankincense look alike but burn differently — they share appearance → heat triggers each resin's own melt and smoke → the melt behavior, color, and smoke diverge → burning reveals which resin is in front of you → a buyer who skips the burn test cannot tell them apart.

The mechanics follow from what a resin is. A balsamic resin is a complex mixture that melts and smokes under heat, and each resin's mixture is its own. Because 枫香 and frankincense sit close in raw appearance — close enough for one to masquerade as the other — the differences only surface under heat. The classical "slightly whitish-yellow" note adds a visual cue, but the burn remains the decisive step, and it doubles as a purity check for any smoky or off material.

## From Tap to Blend: How It Is Processed

**Direct answer:** 枫香脂 is processed by tapping and, as the *Xiangcheng* shows, by warm melting — the resin is softened over fire and stirred into other aromatics rather than simply ground into powder.

Processing runs in two stages:

- **Tapping** — the bark is wounded, and the balsamic resin exudes and hardens into tears or a soft mass.
- **Warm melting** — the *Xiangcheng* records 枫香 being melted over fire and stirred into a blend with cassia ("通明枫香二两，火上溶，间桂末一两，入香内搅匀"), a warm-blending step suited to a meltable resin.

The key point is that 枫香脂 is a meltable balsamic resin, handled by tapping and warm blending, not by the grinding route of a wood or root. It acts as a fixative and supporting ingredient that rounds out other materials rather than carrying a blend alone.

## Where It Sits in a Blend

**Direct answer:** Sweetgum works as a supporting and fixative note — the material that warms and rounds the heavier woods and resins around it — and it enters a blend either as melted resin or as a resin torn small and warmed on a heater.

The *Xiangcheng* formula — two parts clear 枫香 melted over fire, then one part cassia powder stirred in — shows the intended role. The resin is not the star; it is the medium that carries and softens the cassia. That is the shape of its contribution generally: it lends a warm, sweet-balsamic undertone that makes woody and resinous blends read as rounder and less sharp. A blender who treats 枫香 as a top note has misplaced it; it belongs at the base, where its fixative character does its quiet work. ## Burning Sweetgum: Charcoal vs Electric Heater

**Direct answer:** Sweetgum resin burns two ways — fast and smoky on a lit charcoal disc, or slow and low-smoke on an electric heater — and the choice is a trade between intensity and cleanliness, not a fixed rule.

On a charcoal disc nested in sand or ash, a small piece of resin melts and smokes quickly, giving a strong but short burst with visible smoke and more particulate matter. On an electric heater, the same piece warms gradually and releases its balsamic note over a longer stretch with far less smoke. Both routes demand ventilation and small portions; the heater suits a longer, quieter session, while charcoal suits a brief, fuller release. The smoke-and-ventilation caveat is not optional — see the wider [safety guidance](/safety/).

## Storage and Handling

**Direct answer:** Sweetgum resin is forgiving to store but slow to work with — keep it cool, sealed, and away from flame, and expect a sticky, meltable material rather than a pourable powder.

Because 枫香脂 is a balsamic resin, heat is its main enemy in storage: warmth softens it into a sticky mass that gathers dust and loses its clean surface, so a cool, sealed container is the practical default. Keep it away from open flame and anything that could melt it by accident. In the workshop, a sticky resin is best portioned cold, then warmed only at blending time. These are handling notes, not a safety certification — burning any resin still produces smoke and particulate matter, so work in a ventilated space.

## What a Buyer Should Check

**Direct answer:** A sourcing buyer should pin down the species first — 枫香脂 (*Liquidambar formosana*), not storax (*L. orientalis*) or frankincense (*Boswellia*) — then check resin content and clarity, because identity is the first and most consequential check.

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer, the checklist runs:

- **Species** — 枫香脂 is *Liquidambar formosana*; distinguish it from storax (*L. orientalis*) and frankincense (*Boswellia*).
- **Resin content and clarity** — "通明" (clear) resin is the classical good grade; a pale, clean, aromatic resin beats a woody or debris-laden one.
- **Aroma** — sweet, balsamic, warm.
- **Burn test** — the classical discriminator; it separates 枫香 from frankincense and reveals a smoky or off material.

## Our Industry View

**Direct answer:** 枫香 is a native Chinese sweet-balsamic resin whose commercial story is about substitution — close enough to frankincense to serve as an adulterant and close enough to storax to be conflated by name, so the buyer's core job is to resolve the species and check the burn, not to trust the label.

From a manufacturing perspective, 枫香 is a material defined by its substitutions. Two points follow:

- **It is an adulterant and a substitute at once.** The *Xiangcheng* records 枫香 being used to adulterate frankincense ("乳香，今人多以枫香杂之"), which tells us two things at once: it is cheap enough and close enough to frankincense to be passed off as it, and a buyer receiving "frankincense" must be able to tell the two apart. The burn test is the classical discriminator, and it still applies.
- **Native resin, not imported.** Unlike storax (苏合香), which classical texts record as imported, 枫香 is the native Chinese sweetgum resin — more available and cheaper, which is exactly why it entered the substitution trade. A buyer who understands the native/imported distinction understands the price and the risk.

In our view, 枫香脂 is a fine sweet-balsamic resin for rounding a blend — buy it by species name, check that it is clear, clean, and aromatic, and use the burn test to keep it (and frankincense) honest.

## Summary

枫香 (fēngxiāng) is the sweet-balsamic resin 枫香脂 of the Chinese sweetgum, *Liquidambar formosana* (Altingiaceae), a native tree of central and southern China whose wounded bark yields the resin. It is distinct from the imported storax (*Liquidambar orientalis*, 苏合香) despite sharing the genus, and the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records — under the name 白胶香 — both its formula uses and its use to adulterate frankincense, a practice Li Shizhen said was revealed only by burning. Its medicinal claims are traditional language, not clinical evidence.

## FAQ

**Is sweetgum the same as storax?** They are close relatives rather than the same product. Sweetgum resin (枫香脂) is tapped from *Liquidambar formosana*, the native Chinese species, whereas storax (苏合香) is the product of *Liquidambar orientalis*, the Oriental sweetgum of Asia Minor. The shared genus is what causes the mix-up, but the two carry separate origins and, to a degree, separate aromatic profiles and price points. For the exact scent, the species is what matters when a label offers "sweetgum" or "storax."

**Is sweetgum a wood or a resin?** In an incense context, the name refers to the resin (枫香脂) tapped from the tree, not to the timber. The sweetgum (*Liquidambar formosana*) produces a balsamic resin that is the true incense and perfumery material, belonging to the same broad group as storax and benzoin. A label that lists "sweetgum" without spelling out resin or wood nearly always means the resin, because that is the portion with fragrance value.

**What does sweetgum smell like?** Warm, sweet, and balsamic, with a resinous body — a soft, comforting base that sits in the benzoin family. It behaves as a supporting or base note rather than a bright top, and its work is to round out and warm the heavier woods and resins around it. It shares benzoin's general family but keeps a distinct character of its own instead of reading as a straight copy.

**Was sweetgum used to fake frankincense?** Yes. The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘), quoting Li Shizhen, records that "乳香，今人多以枫香杂之，惟烧时可辨" — frankincense was routinely cut with 枫香, and the two could be separated only by burning. The same source notes that both 枫香 and pine resin could pass for frankincense, though 枫香 leans slightly whitish-yellow and burning exposes the difference. The warning is five centuries old and still applies today.

**What is sweetgum resin used for in incense?** It contributes a sweet-balsamic warmth and acts as a supporting and fixative note, rounding out woods and resins. The *Xiangcheng* shows it melted over fire and stirred into a cassia blend ("通明枫香二两，火上溶，间桂末一两，入香内搅匀"), evidence that it was treated as a meltable resin blended warm rather than ground like a wood.

**How do you tell a good grade of sweetgum resin?** Start with clarity — "通明" (clear or translucent) resin is the classical good grade — then move to aroma, purity, and burn character. A pale, clean, aromatic resin beats a dark, cloudy, or debris-laden one. When checking it against frankincense, burn a small piece; the way it melts and smokes reveals which resin you actually have.

**How should I store sweetgum resin?** Keep it cool, sealed, and clear of any flame. Heat turns the balsamic resin into a sticky mass that gathers dust and dulls its surface, so a cool container is the practical choice; portion it cold and warm it only when blending.

**What is the difference between sweetgum (枫香) and 白胶香?** None in substance — they are the same material under different names. 白胶香 ("white gum fragrance") is the older label for sweetgum resin (枫香脂) that the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records, alongside the *Golden Light Sutra* transliteration 须萨析罗婆. The tree is 枫香, and the resin is 枫香脂 or, in the older terminology, 白胶香.

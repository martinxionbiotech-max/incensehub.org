**Direct answer:** Every incense format needs a heatproof, stable holder matched to it — a stick holder with an ash tray, a backflow burner for backflow cones, a coil stand, a charcoal-safe burner for resin, and nothing at all for beads. The holder's job is simple but non-negotiable: catch the ash and keep the burn away from anything flammable.

**Key facts:** The holder is a safety item first and a decorative object second. The single rule is **heatproof and stable** — ceramic, glass, metal, or stone, on a level surface, away from drafts and flammables.

---

## Match the Holder to the Format

**Direct answer:** There is no universal "one holder" — the shape of the incense dictates the shape of what holds it, and using the wrong one fails the safety job before the burn even starts.

Each format drops ash differently and burns at a different temperature, so the holder's geometry follows the format's geometry. A stick sheds ash along its whole length → the holder must suspend the ember over a tray that catches the fall. A coil smolders in a flat spiral for hours → the stand must hold it clear of the surface the whole time. Resin on charcoal concentrates intense heat at one point → the burner needs an insulating bed. The table below maps the requirement to each format.

| Format | What you need |
|---|---|
| Stick | A stick holder (hole or clip) over an ash tray |
| Cone | A cone holder with a heatproof base |
| Backflow cone | A backflow burner (hole + catch tray) |
| Coil | A coil stand, or a heatproof plate over ash/sand |
| Resin / powder | A charcoal-safe burner on a bed of ash/sand, or an electric heater |
| Beads | None (worn, not burned) |

## Why "Heatproof and Stable" Is the Whole Rule

**Direct answer:** The holder fails in exactly two ways — it burns through, or it tips — and the words "heatproof" and "stable" name those two failures directly, which is why the rule collapses to four words.

Trace each failure to its cause. A holder that is not heatproof absorbs the ember's heat → the material chars, cracks, or transfers the heat to the table beneath → a scorch or a fire follows. A holder that is not stable tips when bumped or brushed → the burning end falls onto paper, cloth, or wood → ignition. So "heatproof" defeats the burn-through path, and "stable" defeats the tip-over path, and every other piece of holder advice is a footnote to those two. Ceramic, glass, metal, and stone spread and survive heat; plastic and bare wood fail the heatproof test outright. The whole guide is, in the end, four words.

## Thermal Mass: Why the Material Matters in One Direction

**Direct answer:** The reason ceramic and stone work where wood and plastic do not is thermal mass — a dense material soaks up heat slowly and spreads it, while a light one passes it straight through to whatever is underneath.

Think of the holder as a heat sink, not just a dish. A heavy ceramic or stone base has high thermal mass → it absorbs the ember's heat gradually and radiates it across a large area → the temperature at any one spot stays below scorching. Plastic has almost no thermal mass and, worse, melts; bare wood is an insulator that traps heat at the surface until it chars and can eventually smolder on its own. So the "heatproof" half of the rule is not about the material surviving — it is about the material refusing to pass concentrated heat to the table. A thin metal tray can also carry heat through fast if it is hollow, which is why the practical test is weight and thickness, not just the material's name.

| Your format | What you need | Upgrade path |

## The One Rule

**Direct answer:** Whatever the format, the holder must be **heatproof and stable**, set on a **level, non-flammable surface**, clear of drafts and anything that can catch.

Material matters in one direction only. Ceramic, glass, metal, and stone absorb and spread heat safely; plastic and bare wood do not — a hot ember resting on either scorches, and bare wood can smolder. A small dish of sand or ash under a cone or coil adds insulation and catches stray embers, which is why the ash bed is standard practice rather than an aesthetic choice. The full precautions are in [incense safety](/safety/).

## A Confusion Table: Which Burner Do You Actually Need?

**Direct answer:** Burners get mixed up by silhouette, and buying the wrong one fails the job before anything is lit — the table sorts the look-alikes by what each one actually does.

| What you are holding | What it is for | The wrong swap |
|---|---|---|

The table's value is before the purchase, not after. Matching the holder to the format is not a matter of taste — it is the difference between a burn that stays contained and one that ends on the table.

## When the Holder Matters Most

**Direct answer:** Three setups demand the holder be exactly right — resin on charcoal, backflow cones, and coils — because each one fails the safety job in a specific, predictable way if the holder is wrong.

- **Resin on charcoal** — the charcoal disc gets hot enough to crack a thin surface; a purpose-built burner on a sand/ash bed is the standard, because the bed spreads that concentrated heat before it reaches the table.
- **Backflow cones** — without the right burner (hole aligned, catch tray present) there is no waterfall effect, because the sinking smoke has nowhere to fall. See [backflow cones](/blog/backflow-cones/).
- **Coils** — a coil smolders for hours, so the base must survive that entire span without scorching.

The common thread: the holder is the one place where skimping converts directly into a scorch mark, a broken dish, or a dead visual effect. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## Reading a Holder Before You Buy It

**Direct answer:** Three quick checks — the material, the weight, and whether the ash catcher is actually big enough — predict whether a holder will do its job, before you ever set a match to it.

The checks map straight onto the one rule. Hold it and gauge the weight: a heavy base is stable; a feather-light trinket tips the first time it is bumped. Look at the material: ceramic, glass, metal, or stone pass; anything plastic or bare wood is out. Eyeball the ash tray: a stick sheds ash along its whole length, so a tiny tray overflows by the end of a single burn. The point is that a holder telegraphs its failure modes on the shelf, the same way a stick or cone telegraphs its own — the buyer who checks material, weight, and tray size has already caught most mistakes the shop can offer.

## A Judgment Boundary: Decorative Is Not the Same as Safe

**Direct answer:** A holder can be beautiful and still fail its one job, because prettiness lives on the surface while safety lives in the material and the mass — and the two do not always travel together.

The incense market is full of delicate, hand-painted burners that are lovely to look at and useless to burn on. A thin glazed tray that is too light tips; a wooden stand with no ash catcher scorches; a resin ornament melts at the first real ember. The boundary worth stating plainly: **"decorative" does not mean "functional," and "expensive" does not mean "heatproof."** Buy the safe, boring, heatproof holder first, and let the pretty one be a display piece that never sees a flame. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## Thermal Shock: The Crack You Did Not Cause

**Direct answer:** A holder that shatters under a fresh charcoal disc is rarely defective — it is a thermal-shock crack, the result of one spot heating much faster than the material around it.

Walk the failure as a chain. A dense ceramic or stone base sits at room temperature → a hot charcoal disc lands on one small spot → that spot's surface expands fast while the body beneath stays cool → the material pulls against itself along the boundary between hot and cold → a crack opens from the contact point outward. Glazed surfaces make it worse, because glaze and clay expand at different rates and the glaze is the first thing to pop.

This is why the instructions on a charcoal burner always say to seat the disc on a bed of ash or sand, never directly on bare ceramic. The ash layer does not just insulate the table — it spreads the heat so the temperature climbs evenly instead of spiking at a single point. A buyer who skips that bed and hears a sudden "tick" has just learned the difference between a material that can take heat and a material that can take *sudden* heat. The two are not the same thing, and the second is the one that matters for charcoal work.

## A Material Ladder: What Each Holder Body Actually Buys

**Direct answer:** "Ceramic, glass, metal, or stone" is a useful rule, but the four materials are not equal — this ladder sorts them by what each one absorbs, spreads, and survives.

| Body material | Absorbs heat | Spreads heat | Tip-over resistance | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solid stone | High | Excellent | High | Weight; rare |
| Thick ceramic | High | Good | Good | Glaze can crack under shock |
| Solid metal | Moderate | Good | High | Hollow trays pass heat through |
| Glass | Moderate | Moderate | Low | Shatters under thermal shock |
| Bare wood | Low | Poor | Varies | Chars, can smolder |
| Plastic | None | None | Low | Melts |

The table exposes the gap the four-word rule hides. Stone and thick ceramic soak heat and let it out slowly, so the spot under the ember never gets scorching. Metal is fine *only* when it is solid — a hollow stamped tray conducts the heat straight down to the table. Glass reads decorative but trades away both heat tolerance and stability. Read the ladder before you pay for looks.

## Heatproof Is Not the Same as Heat-Absorbing

**Direct answer:** A holder can be fireproof and still scorch the table underneath — surviving heat and stopping heat are two different jobs, and only the second one keeps the surface safe.

The misconception runs through cheap-burner shopping. A thin metal plate is technically "heatproof" — it will not melt or ignite. But metal is a conductor, not an insulator → the ember's heat races through the thin sheet → the wood or paper underneath reaches scorching temperature → a burn mark appears even though the plate itself is unharmed. The holder survived; the table did not. That is the failure the word "heatproof" never warns you about.

The judgment boundary for a buyer: **"heatproof" names the material's survival, and only mass plus insulation names the surface's safety.** A heavy stone or a thick ceramic base with an ash bed stops the heat in its tracks; a thin conductive sheet just relocates it. When you are buying for charcoal or for a coil that burns for hours, test for the second job — press the base, gauge its weight, and ask whether heat dies here or merely passes through. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Angle That Sets the Ash Path

**Direct answer:** Where the ash lands is decided before you light the stick — the holder's tilt, the hole's depth, and the tray's width together route the falling ember, and a mismatch spills it over the edge.

Follow the ash on its way down. A stick burns tip-first and sheds ash continuously → the ash falls straight only if the stick stands vertical → a stick poked into a shallow, angled hole leans → the lean pulls the falling ash off-center → ash drifts past the tray's rim onto the table. Two variables, not one, control it: the depth of the hole sets how far the stick leans, and the tray's width sets how much drift it can absorb. A deep, snug hole plus a wide tray is the combination that keeps the table clean; a shallow hole plus a narrow tray is the combination that sprinkles ash everywhere.

That mechanism is the real reason "a plate" is not always enough. A stick propped at a steep angle against a dish rim leans hard → the ember's ash column falls outside the dish → the surface below catches it. A purpose-made holder fixes the geometry, not just the heat. When you are buying, look at the hole depth and the tray span before you look at the decoration — those two numbers are the ash path, and the ash path is what you will actually live with.

## What the Holder Should Never Be Made Of, and Why

**Direct answer:** Three materials fail the holder's job outright — plastic, glass, and bare wood each fail in a specific way — and knowing the failure tells you what to skip at a glance.

| Material | How it fails | The moment it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Plastic | Melts and deforms | First contact with a hot ember |
| Thin glass | Shatters from thermal shock | When a hot disc or ember lands on it |
| Bare wood | Chars, then can smolder | After sustained heat from a coil |
| Lacquered wood | The finish scorches and off-gases | Under a lit cone |

The table is a negative checklist, and its value is speed: you can rule out a whole shelf without reading a label. Plastic is out because it cannot survive the temperature; thin glass is out because it survives heat unevenly and cracks; bare and lacquered wood are out because they are fuel, not insulation. The materials that pass — solid stone, thick ceramic, solid metal — are the ones that neither melt, crack, nor feed the fire. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Vessel's Shape as a Draft Shield

**Direct answer:** A censer's lid is not ornament — it is a draft shield, and the perforation pattern on that lid is a regulator that sets how fast the smoke escapes.

Walk the airflow. A burning coil or chip releases smoke that would be whipped sideways by any moving air → a lidded censer traps a still pocket above the ember → the smoke rises, cools, and leaks through the perforations at a controlled rate → fewer holes means a slower, softer stream; more holes means a faster, stronger one. The lid is doing two jobs at once: it blocks drafts from outside and throttles the smoke from inside.

That is why a lidded censer is not just a "fancy holder" — it is a different operating mode from an open tray. An open tray gives the burn no protection from air movement; a lidded censer wraps the burn in a still microclimate and hands the user a dial (the hole pattern) that an open tray cannot offer. For a long coil or a resin session in a breezy room, the lid is the difference between a steady scent and a smoke plume that wanders. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

| Setup | The arrangement | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Stick in a deep holder | Vertical stick over a wide tray | Ash drifting past the rim |
| Cone on a flat dish | Cone on a heatproof base | Wax or resin bleeding onto the surface |

| Resin on charcoal | Disc on ash inside a censer | The concentrated heat cracking the vessel |
| Backflow cone | Cone over an aligned hole | The smoke spilling the wrong way |

The table reads as five rows of the same sentence: the holder exists to neutralize one specific physical failure. Once you name the failure a setup is guarding against, the setup stops looking arbitrary and starts looking necessary. That is the whole guide in miniature — match the holder to the failure, not to the fashion. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Surface Under the Holder: The Second Failure Point

**Direct answer:** The holder can be perfect and still fail the job if the surface beneath it is wrong — a level, heatproof holder on a wobbly cloth is a tip-over waiting to happen.

Trace the second failure path. The ember is contained, but the whole assembly sits on something → a tablecloth bunches under one edge → the holder tilts → the burning end slides toward the edge → ash and ember spill onto the fabric. The holder did not fail; the *foundation* did. The same logic runs through every format: a coil stand on a wobbly stack of books, a censer on a plush rug, a stick holder on a sloped shelf — each is a stable object on an unstable base.

So the rule extends one layer down. "Heatproof and stable" describes the holder, but the holder must itself sit on a level, non-flammable, steady surface — a hard table, a stone shelf, a tray with a lip. The practical check is to nudge the whole assembly once it is set up: if the ember moves, the foundation is wrong even though the holder is fine. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## A Care-and-Maintenance Table

**Direct answer:** A holder lasts longer and stays safer with a small amount of upkeep, and this table maps each material to the care it actually needs.

| Material | What to do | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Ceramic / stone | Wipe ash with a dry cloth | Sudden temperature swings |
| Metal | Buff tarnish, empty the tray | Abrasive scrubbing that scratches |
| Glass | Rinse and dry fully | Thermal shock from hot-to-cold |
| Ash / sand beds | Sift and top up | Letting debris mix into the bed |

The table's rule is that most holder damage is not from the burn but from neglect between burns. A tray left full of old ash stains; a glass burner dropped into cold water while warm cracks; a metal holder left damp tarnishes. A minute of dry, correct care after each session extends the holder's life and keeps the next burn safe. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Ash Column's Weight: Why a Tall Holder Tips Less Than You Think

**Direct answer:** A holder's stability is a lever problem, not a weight problem — the tip-over force comes from the burn line's height above the base, and a wide footprint cancels a high stick.

Picture the physics. A lit stick stands vertical and the ember rides at the top → the whole assembly's center of mass sits low, but any sideways nudge applies its push at the top, where the lever arm is longest → the push times the height is the tipping torque → a wide, heavy base provides the counter-torque → a narrow base multiplied by a long stick produces a leverage ratio the base cannot win. A short stick in a deep, heavy dish never tips; a long stick in a lightweight tray flips with a finger-flick.

The practical rule folds into the four-word one: a holder for long sticks wants width and weight more than a holder for cones, because the stick's height *is* the lever. Judge the base diameter against the stick length before you buy, not after the first spill.

## A Base-Material Temperature Ladder: What Actually Touches the Table

**Direct answer:** The material that protects the table is the layer at the very bottom, and this ladder rates how each common base behaves when a full burn sits on it for an hour.

| Base layer | Heat reaches table? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thick stone slab | Barely | High mass soaks and spreads heat before it sinks |
| Sand or ash bed | Slowly | The grains insulate and the heat spreads through air gaps |
| Solid wood block | Eventually | Insulates well, but can char after sustained heat |
| Thin stamped metal | Quickly | Conducts heat straight down to the surface |
| Paper or cloth mat | Immediately | Flammable; no thermal barrier at all |

The table's lesson: the holder's *bottom* is the part doing the real safety work, and a beautiful ceramic dish on a paper doily has already lost the bottom layer. Stack heat-proof against heat-proof, down to the last surface.

## The First-Time Burner's Error, Named Plainly

**Direct answer:** The most common first purchase is the wrong one — a decorative backflow burner bought for ordinary sticks — and it is wrong because one format's holder cannot double as another's without a safety gap.

This is the misconception the whole guide corrects. A backflow burner has a hole for smoke to sink through and a catch tray below; put a stick in it and there is no seat for the ash to fall into, and the geometry that makes the waterfall work is irrelevant to a rod that sheds ash along its length. A holder is not a universal socket; it is a shape-matched seat, and the pretty one that "does everything" usually does no one thing safely.

The judgment boundary for a buyer: **match the holder to the format first, and let the looks be the second tiebreaker — a correct-but-plain holder beats a wrong-but-beautiful one every time.** Buy the seat that fits the burn you actually have. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Ventilation Loop: Why a Holder Cannot Fix a Stale Room

**Direct answer:** A holder keeps the ember safe, but it does not move air — and an hour of burning in a closed room accumulates smoke no holder design can undo.

Trace the smoke once it leaves the stick. The ember releases a thin plume → the plume rises and cools → in still air it spreads and hangs at ceiling level → each minute of burn adds more → the room's air becomes a reservoir of particulate that no ash tray catches. The holder's job ends at the ember; the room's job is airflow, and only a window, a fan, or a vent does that. A perfect holder in a sealed room is a contained fire feeding an unvented cloud.

The practical pairing: **match the holder to the format, and match the airflow to the burn length — the two are separate jobs, and the second one belongs to the room, not the shelf.** See [incense safety](/safety/) for the ventilation side.

## The Travel Question: What a Holder Must Survive

**Direct answer:** A holder that survives the shelf may not survive the journey to it — shipping is a thermal-shock and impact test, and the buyer should unwrap with the same eyes they used at the shelf.

Walk the transit chain. A ceramic holder is fired, glazed, and boxed → it rides in a truck and a plane, subject to vibration and sudden temperature swings → hairline cracks that were invisible at the factory can open under the stress → a piece that arrives whole may carry a hidden stress line that fails at the first hot charcoal. The buyer's check is the same three questions, repeated on arrival: is the material right, is it heavy enough, is the tray intact.

The rule: **inspect a holder when it arrives, not when it fails — a hairline crack under a fresh charcoal disc is a transit legacy, and it is caught in seconds by looking before you light.** *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Ornament Question: Why Some Holders Are Only for Show

**Direct answer:** A holder bought for its looks but never cleared for heat is a display object, not a burner — and the honest move is to sort the shelf into "can burn" and "can only sit."

Walk the sorting test. A holder's material, mass, and tray are its safety credentials → a thin, glazed, hand-painted piece that fails any one of them is out of the burning rotation → it earns a place on the shelf as decoration. The failure mode of mixing the two is a scorch mark on a table and a cracked heirloom. Sorting costs nothing and prevents both.

The practical rule: **mark, in your own head, which holders may see fire and which may not — a pretty piece on the shelf is honest, a pretty piece under a charcoal disc is a hazard.** The shelf and the burner are two different jobs, and the same object cannot always do both. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Setup Ritual: Ten Seconds That Prevent Most Fires

**Direct answer:** Most holder failures are prevented by a ten-second pre-burn check — level surface, clear zone, matched holder, and a look at what is underneath.

Run the ten-second pass. Set the holder on a hard, level surface → clear a radius of anything flammable → confirm the holder matches the format → glance at the material beneath the holder, not just the holder itself → light. The pass is the whole guide compressed, and it catches the two failure modes — tip-over and burn-through — before the match is struck. The fire that never starts is the one you never have to put out.

The habit: **make the ten-second check the ritual that precedes every burn — the holder does its job only if you do yours first, and yours is ten seconds long.** *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Wet-Dough Cousin: Why a Backflow Cone Needs Its Own Burner

**Direct answer:** A backflow cone is a shaped fuel with a hole through it, and its whole effect — smoke sinking downward — depends on an aligned hole and catch tray; on a flat dish the smoke has nowhere to go, so the effect simply does not happen.

Walk the backflow mechanism. The cone is formed with a hollow channel up its center → the smoke cools as it rises and, being denser than the surrounding air, wants to sink → the burner's hole lines up with the cone's channel → the smoke flows downward through it → the catch tray pools the sinking smoke so it spills outward like a waterfall. On a flat dish there is no hole to line up → the smoke rises instead → the signature effect vanishes. The burner is not decoration; it is the other half of the mechanism.

The practical rule: **a backflow cone without its burner is half a product — the aligned hole and catch tray are what turn the cone's smoke into the waterfall, and no flat dish can substitute for them.** See [backflow cones](/blog/backflow-cones/). *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Coal's Seat: Why Charcoal Never Touches Bare Ceramic

**Direct answer:** A lit charcoal disc must sit on a bed of ash or sand, never directly on bare ceramic, because the ash spreads the heat — and a disc on bare ceramic concentrates it, which is how thermal-shock cracks happen.

Trace the two heat paths. A disc on bare ceramic heats one small spot → that spot expands while the surrounding body stays cool → the material pulls against itself along the hot-cold boundary → a crack opens. A disc on an ash bed spreads the heat across a wider area → the temperature climbs evenly instead of spiking → the vessel survives. The ash is not a cushion; it is a heat spreader, and the difference between "spread" and "spiked" is the difference between a vessel that lasts and one that cracks on its first use.

The practical rule: **never seat a lit charcoal disc on bare ceramic — the ash or sand bed is the standard practice for a reason, and skipping it is the single fastest way to break a burner.** *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Draft's Reach: Why a Breeze Breaks a Backflow Cascade

**Direct answer:** A backflow cone's downward smoke is fragile — a draft or a breath of moving air disrupts the sinking column, and the waterfall breaks apart into ordinary rising smoke.

Walk the fragility. The sinking smoke is dense and slow → it depends on still air to keep its downward path → any moving air pushes against the column → the smoke stalls, wavers, or breaks upward → the waterfall becomes a ragged plume. The effect is not robust; it is a delicate, still-air phenomenon, and the same ceiling fan that shortens a stick's burn will also destroy a backflow cone's whole point.

The practical rule: **burn a backflow cone in still air, away from fans, vents, and open windows — the waterfall is the one incense effect that a moving draft can switch off entirely, and the room is the difference between a cascade and nothing.** *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## FAQ

### Q: What is the best incense holder?
There is no single "best" holder, because each format needs something different. Match the holder to what you are burning: a stick holder over an ash tray, a cone holder with a heatproof base, a backflow burner with a hole and catch tray, a coil stand, or a charcoal-safe burner on a bed of ash or sand. The rule that never changes is material and stability — ceramic, glass, metal, or stone, on a level surface away from drafts and flammables. Heatproof and stable beats decorative every time.

### Q: Can I burn incense without any holder?
Not safely. A holder does two essential jobs: it catches the falling ash for the whole burn, and it keeps the smoldering ember on a heatproof surface instead of against wood, plastic, paper, or fabric. Burning a stick or cone directly on a bare surface — or propped against something flammable — risks scorching and fire. Even a simple ceramic dish or a small plate of sand is far better than nothing, but a purpose-matched holder is the safest choice. The full set of precautions is in [incense safety](/safety/).

### Q: Do backflow cones require a special burner?
Yes. A backflow cone only produces its waterfall effect on a purpose-built backflow burner, which has a hole aligned to the cone's base and a catch tray or baffle that collects the falling smoke and lets it spill outward. On a flat, ordinary dish the smoke has nowhere to go, so it simply rises and the effect does not happen. Seat the cone upright so the hole lines up, and keep the setup away from drafts, which can break the downward flow. The details are in [backflow cones](/blog/backflow-cones/).

### Q: What should go underneath a charcoal burner?
Put a bed of sand or ash inside a heatproof burner, beneath the charcoal disc. The bed does two things: it insulates the intense heat of the charcoal so the burner and the surface under it do not crack or scorch, and it gives the hot disc a stable, non-flammable seat. Never place a lit charcoal disc directly on a thin dish, wood, or plastic. This is the standard setup for heating resin and wood chips. The full method is in [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

### Q: Can I improvise a holder from things at home?
You can improvise a basic one, with limits. A shallow ceramic dish or a small bowl of sand catches ash and gives an ember a heatproof seat, which is far safer than nothing. What you cannot improvise away is the format-specific part: a backflow cone needs its aligned hole and catch tray, and a coil needs a stand or plate that keeps the spiral clear of the surface. Improvising works for a stick or cone in a pinch; for backflow, coils, and charcoal, buy the matched holder instead of forcing a substitute.

### Q: Why did my ceramic holder crack the first time I used charcoal?
That is almost always thermal shock, not a factory defect. A hot charcoal disc heats one small spot of the ceramic much faster than the surrounding body → the hot zone expands while the cool body stays put → the two pull against each other → a crack runs out from the contact point. Glazed pieces crack first because the glaze expands at a different rate than the clay. The fix is the ash or sand bed: it spreads the heat so the temperature rises evenly instead of spiking at one point. Never set a lit charcoal disc directly on bare ceramic.

### Q: What is the ideal depth of ash under a charcoal disc?
About a finger's width — roughly two to three centimeters. The bed must be deep enough that the disc's underside never touches the vessel floor, and that the falling ash from the burning charcoal has somewhere to settle without smothering the airflow. Too shallow a bed and the heat reaches the ceramic fast enough to crack it; too deep and you waste charcoal as it sinks. The depth is a spacing decision, not a ritual nicety — it sets the distance between the hottest point in the room and the surface you are trying to protect.

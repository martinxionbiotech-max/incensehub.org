**Direct answer:** The distinction is about **who does the product work**. Under OEM, a factory turns a brief you wrote into a product: your scent direction, your dimensions, your packaging, your brand. Under private label, the factory's product already exists and you buy the right to put your name on it. Cost, timeline, minimums, and the ownership of the recipe all follow from that split.

## Key facts

The test is counterfactual. If the product would exist whether or not you ever contacted the factory, you are buying private label. If it exists because you briefed it, you are buying OEM. Neither route publishes prices or minimums, because both are quoted against a specific product, quantity, packaging set, and destination. Neither is superior in the abstract; they answer different questions.

## Key Takeaways

- **OEM is development plus branding. Private label is branding alone.**
- The counterfactual test — would this product exist without me? — settles the category faster than any price comparison.
- OEM buys differentiation and a recipe you can defend; it costs a development loop and deeper involvement.
- Private label buys speed and a known quantity; it costs distinctiveness and leaves the formula with the factory.
- In both routes the dangerous terms are the ones nobody puts in the quotation: ownership, tooling, master samples, and what happens when the relationship ends.
- Ask for the minimum **for your product**, never "your MOQ" in the abstract. An abstract question earns an abstract answer.

## Two Contracts, One Question

The incense trade uses "OEM" and "private label" loosely, sometimes for the same transaction. That looseness is expensive, because the two names describe contracts with different obligations, different risks, and different exit costs.

**OEM** — original equipment manufacturing — is a build-to-specification arrangement. You arrive with a brief: a scent family, a burn behaviour, a stick dimension, a packaging concept, a price target. The factory translates that into a formula, produces samples, and iterates with you until the sample matches the brief closely enough to scale. You direct the development; the factory supplies process knowledge and capacity. Whether you end up owning the formula is a separate question, settled in writing, and not implied by the letters OEM.

**Private label** is a selection arrangement. The factory has a catalogue, or at least a set of proven products. You choose one, specify the label and the box, and take delivery. No formulation happens, because the formulation already exists. You are buying a product plus a branding service.

The counterfactual test is the cleanest way to tell them apart in a conversation where a supplier is using the words interchangeably. Ask: *if I had never approached this factory, would this exact product still be in its catalogue?* A yes means private label. A no — because the product is being built around your brief — means OEM, whatever the invoice calls it.

The commercial consequence is worth stating plainly. In private label, your brand is the only thing that distinguishes the offer, because the product behind it is available to anyone else who asks. In OEM, the product itself can carry some of the distinction, if the formula is genuinely yours or genuinely exclusive.

## The Four Burdens, and Who Carries Each

Both routes distribute the same four burdens — development, tooling, inventory, and documentation — but they distribute them differently. Seeing where each burden lands explains most of the day-to-day difference between the two experiences.

| Burden | OEM | Private label |
|---|---|---|
| Development | Yours to direct: brief, sample review, iteration decisions | None; the product is already developed |
| Tooling and setup | Custom dies, moulds, or cutting jigs may be needed for your format | Shared with every other buyer of the same product |
| Inventory | You commit to a run sized around your specification | You buy from existing stock or a scheduled run |
| Documentation | Specification sheet written for your product; testing scoped to your claims | Standard sheet for the catalogue item, which may not answer your buyer's questions |
| Brand risk | Product performance is partly your design decision | Product performance is the factory's standard, visible to your competitors too |
| Exit cost | Higher: new factory must relearn your specification | Lower: any factory with the same catalogue item can supply it |

The table is the real cost comparison. A private-label quote often looks cheaper because development and tooling are invisible — they were paid for by someone else, long before you arrived. An OEM quote looks expensive because you are paying for those burdens directly, and because you will pay again every time you change your mind.

## What Actually Drives the Quote

There is no price list in this industry, and a supplier who offers one is either quoting a single standard item or guessing. A quotation is assembled from a handful of cost drivers, and knowing them tells you which levers are worth pulling.

**Material grade.** The aromatic load is the largest variable in a stick. A stick built around a natural resin or a wood powder behaves and costs differently from one built on a fragrance compound. Grade is not a moral category — a well-made compound stick can be excellent — but the quotation should say which it is.

**Format and dimension.** Diameter, length, core type, and coating thickness all change the machine settings and the cycle time. A bamboo-core stick and a coreless extruded stick are different production lines in miniature.

**Packaging.** A tube, a box, a blister, a printed sleeve, and a gift set are five different cost structures. Packaging often rivals the product itself in a retail-oriented order, and it is the line most often under-specified in a first brief.

**Quantity.** Setup cost is spread across the run. Doubling quantity rarely doubles price, which is why the per-unit figure falls and why minimums exist at all.

**Destination and compliance.** Different markets ask for different documentation, labeling languages, and restricted-substance declarations. A quotation that ignores the destination is a quotation that will be renegotiated at shipment.

**Freight and terms.** Incoterms decide who pays for what between the factory gate and your warehouse. Two quotes for the same product can differ by a third purely because one is ex-works and the other is delivered.

When a buyer says "OEM is more expensive," what they usually mean is "OEM includes development, tooling, and a smaller run." Those are three separate costs, and each can be negotiated or deferred. Naming them separately is the beginning of a sane comparison.

## The Sample Invoice, Decoded

Sample charges confuse first-time buyers because a sample is not a small order; it is a separate transaction with its own economics. The lines usually include tooling or setup, a hand-making charge, the material cost of a short run, and courier freight. Some factories credit the sample charge against the first production order, and that credit is worth asking about explicitly.

Two habits make the sample stage cheaper. First, ask for the sample in the final packaging, not only the bare stick, because packaging problems surface early and are cheap to fix. Second, ask for two samples from different production days, which reveals batch-to-batch variation that a single perfect sample hides.

## The Development Loop, as a Mechanism

OEM's timeline is not production time. It is the loop before production, and the loop is where projects die.

a brief is written → the factory selects a formula direction → a bench sample is made → you burn it in the intended setting → the gap between sample and brief is described in sensory terms → the formula is adjusted → a second sample is made → the loop repeats until the sample is close enough → a production sample is approved → the run is scheduled

Two things follow from that chain. First, most of the elapsed time sits in the middle, in the sample-and-revise cycles, not at the end. Second, the loop converges faster when the buyer can describe the gap precisely. "It doesn't smell right" costs a round. "The opening is correct but the base turns soapy after ten minutes, and the smoke is harsher than the reference" costs nothing and saves a round.

The loop also has a natural stopping point that buyers often miss. A sample is approved, the run is produced, and the delivered goods differ slightly because natural materials vary and because a bench sample is not a production sample. That gap is normal. The defence is a retained master sample, sealed and dated, against which production can be compared — a small piece of discipline that prevents most quality arguments later.

## The Quote Is Not the Cost

A quoted unit price is the beginning of a landed-cost calculation, not the end of one. Buyers who compare quotations as if they were final prices routinely choose the more expensive supplier and discover it two months later at the customs desk.

The components that sit between the quotation and the warehouse shelf:

- **Incoterm.** Ex-works, FOB, CIF, and delivered-duty-paid place different obligations on each side. An ex-works quote excludes everything after the factory gate; a delivered quote includes it and prices the risk into the number.
- **Freight mode.** Sea freight is cheap per kilogram and slow; air freight is the reverse. Incense is light but bulky, so volumetric weight often decides the bill rather than actual weight.
- **Duty and tax.** Rates depend on the tariff classification of the product and the destination, not on the supplier's description.
- **Customs brokerage and clearance.** A fee that appears only once a shipment is already moving.
- **Inland transport and warehousing.** The last mile is frequently the most expensive per unit.
- **Quality failure cost.** Rejected batches, rework, and the margin lost while stock is unavailable.

Two suppliers quoting the same unit price can differ by a wide margin once these lines are added. The discipline is to ask each supplier to quote on the same Incoterm and the same destination, then compare. A supplier who cannot quote on a named Incoterm is a supplier who has not shipped to your market before.

## Agents, Trading Companies, and Who You Are Actually Talking To

The entity that answers your enquiry is not always the entity that makes the product. Three arrangements are common, and they carry different risks.

A **direct factory** manufactures and sells its own output. Communication is shortest, technical answers are usually accurate, and the relationship is with the people who control the process.

A **trading company** buys from several factories and sells to you. It offers range and convenience, absorbs some documentation work, and adds a margin. The risk is that the factory behind your product stays invisible, which matters when a defect needs a process fix rather than a credit note.

A **sourcing agent** represents your interest for a fee or commission, and may work with several factories on your behalf. The value depends entirely on what the agent is paid for: an inspection-and-coordination fee buys attention, while a commission from the factory buys a different kind of loyalty.

The practical question is not which arrangement is best but which one you can verify. Ask for the production address, the name of the workshop that forms the sticks, and permission for a third-party inspection at that address. An intermediary who cannot answer those three questions is adding cost without adding control.

## What You Own After the Invoice

Ownership is the part of the deal that feels abstract until it matters. Four assets are in play, and each has a different default.

| Asset | Default in private label | Default in OEM | How to secure it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formula or recipe | Factory's | Ambiguous; often factory's unless assigned | Written assignment or exclusive licence, with territory and term |
| Brand and artwork | Yours | Yours | Trademark registration; written licence to the factory for production only |
| Tooling and moulds | Factory's | Factory's unless paid for and specified | Name the tooling in the contract, with a buy-out clause |
| Master sample and specification | None | Often none | Require a sealed retention sample and a written spec sheet |

The pattern is consistent: the things a buyer assumes are included are exactly the things that are not. A brand is not a formula. Paying for a run is not buying the mould. Approving a sample is not owning the standard.

For an OEM buyer, the formula question is the one that decides whether the investment is real. If the factory owns the recipe and merely licenses it to you, you have bought a private label with extra steps and a longer lead time. If you own it, you can move production, defend the product against copies, and treat the formula as an asset on a balance sheet. The [合香 blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) is useful here because it gives a vocabulary for what "the formula" actually consists of — base, supporting materials, accents, and the proportions between them — which makes the assignment clause specific instead of vague.

## Where Private Label Turns Into a Trap

Private label is a legitimate route, and for many sellers it is the correct one. The trap is not the route; it is entering it without noticing what it cannot do.

The first limit is sameness. If the product is in the factory's catalogue, it is available to every other buyer who asks. Your differentiation has to come from brand, packaging, channel, and service, because it cannot come from the product. That is a viable business — many successful house brands are built exactly this way — but it is a marketing business, not a product business.

The second limit is price compression. When several sellers offer the same item, the market converges on price, and the seller with the lowest overhead wins. A brand that has invested in positioning can hold a premium for a while; a brand that has invested only in a logo cannot.

The third limit is the delisting risk. If the factory stops making the item, changes the formula, or reallocates capacity, the seller has no recipe to take elsewhere. The product simply disappears, and the brand has to start again.

There is a fourth, quieter limit: the specification sheet you inherit may not answer the questions your own buyers ask. A retailer sourcing from you will want to know materials, burn behaviour, and compliance declarations. If the factory's standard sheet is thin, that thinness becomes your problem at the negotiation table.

## Where OEM Turns Into a Trap

OEM's failure mode is the mirror image: a project that consumes time and money without producing an asset.

The first trap is scope drift. A brief for "a clean sandalwood stick" becomes a discussion about three scent directions, two formats, and a gift box, and each addition restarts the sample loop. The project is not failing; it is simply expanding faster than it converges.

The second is the sunk-cost lock. After several rounds, the buyer has paid for samples and tooling and feels committed. That feeling is precisely when a weak formula gets approved, because stopping looks more expensive than continuing. The rational question at every round is not "how much have I spent" but "is this sample closer to the brief than the last one, and by how much."

The third is second-source lockout. A custom format with custom tooling can be very hard to move to another factory, because the new factory must rebuild the tooling and relearn the specification. That is not an argument against OEM; it is an argument for owning the tooling and keeping the specification documented.

The fourth is the ownership illusion described above — a custom product whose recipe belongs to the factory. The buyer has paid for development and received a dependency.

## A Sourcing Sequence for Either Route

The sequence below works for both routes, with the development steps skipped in private label. It is ordered so that the cheapest checks come first and the expensive commitments come last.

1. **Define the job the product does.** A gift SKU, a daily-use stick, a travel format, and a ritual set are different products with different cost structures. Write the job down before contacting anyone.
2. **Screen factories on capability, not on price.** Ask what formats they run, what materials they keep in stock, and which markets they already ship to. A factory that does not run your format will quote it anyway and subcontract it.
3. **Ask for the specification sheet and a sample of an existing product.** The sheet tells you how the factory thinks; the sample tells you how it executes.
4. **Request a quotation against your written brief**, with the cost drivers separated: material, format, packaging, tooling, testing, freight.
5. **Settle ownership before sampling.** Formula, artwork, tooling, master sample. Doing this first is cheaper than renegotiating after the product exists.
6. **Run the sample loop with written feedback.** Each round gets a dated note describing the gap in sensory and physical terms.
7. **Approve a production sample and retain a sealed copy.** This is the reference for every later dispute.
8. **Inspect before shipment, not after arrival.** A pre-shipment check on count, weight, packing, and labelling catches the errors that are cheap to fix at the factory and expensive to fix at the warehouse.

The [incense manufacturing quality control](/blog/incense-manufacturing-quality-control/) page covers the inspection points in detail, and [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) explains why certain defects are inherent to certain processes rather than signs of a bad factory.

## Reading a Factory's Answers

A factory's replies to a first enquiry are a sample of its operating discipline. Vague answers are not necessarily dishonest; they are often the sign of a sales office answering questions the production floor never sees. What matters is whether the answers become specific when you ask specifically.

| What you ask | A capable answer sounds like | A warning answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| "Which formats do you run in-house?" | Named formats, with the process for each | "All kinds" with no detail |
| "What is the minimum for this specification?" | A figure tied to your material and packaging, with the reason | A figure with no reference to the product |
| "Can you send a production sample?" | Yes, with a timeline and a cost | "The sample is the same as the photo" |
| "Who owns the formula if we develop one?" | A clear default, plus the terms under which it can be assigned | Silence, or "don't worry about that" |
| "What documentation comes with the shipment?" | A list: spec sheet, material declaration, inspection record | "Whatever you need" |
| "Which markets do you already ship to?" | Named markets and the compliance regimes involved | "All over the world" |

None of these answers proves quality. They establish whether the counterpart can hold a technical conversation, which is the precondition for everything else.

## The Same Order, Three Years Later

Route decisions look similar at launch and diverge over time. Seeing the second and third year is what separates a considered choice from a fast one.

| Point in time | An OEM programme | A private-label programme |
|---|---|---|
| Month one | Development begins; no saleable stock yet | Stock arrives; listings go live |
| Year one | Product is on shelf, formula documented, feedback flows into refinements | Sales data accumulates; the product is still identical to competitors' |
| Year two | Formula is refined; packaging can evolve without a new supplier | Price pressure appears as other sellers carry the same item |
| Year three | The recipe and the brand are both assets; a second source is possible | The brand must carry the entire premium; switching means starting over |

The timeline is not an argument that OEM always wins. A private-label seller who builds a strong channel, a loyal audience, and excellent service can outperform a mediocre OEM brand indefinitely. What the timeline shows is where the leverage sits: private label front-loads revenue and back-loads risk, while OEM does the reverse.

A useful way to decide is to ask which year matters most. A seller testing whether a market exists should optimise for month one. A seller who already knows the market exists and expects to be in it for five years should optimise for year three. Most bad route decisions come from optimising for the wrong year.

## What to Look At on the Floor

A factory's process explains its defects, and a buyer who understands the sequence can predict which complaints will recur. The questions below are the ones that separate a real audit from a tour.

| Stage | What to check | A defect it predicts |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material store | Sealed bins, pest control, aromatic powders kept apart from charcoal bulk | Cross-contamination; musty base note |
| Milling and sieving | Screen size and sieve condition | Gritty texture; uneven burn; blocked extruder |
| Mixing | Binder ratio, water content, mixing time | Brittle sticks; cracking at the bend |
| Forming | Extruder die wear; bamboo splint quality; rolling table hygiene | Diameter drift; core showing through the coat |
| Drying | Rack spacing, airflow, humidity control, dwell time | Warping; internal cracks that appear after packing |
| Finishing | Coating uniformity, cutting blade sharpness | Dust in the tube; inconsistent burn at the tip |
| Packing | Desiccant, batch code, carton compression strength | Damp sticks on arrival; no traceability for a bad batch |

Two of these stages cause most of the complaints buyers blame on "quality" in general. Drying explains warping and post-packing cracks, because a stick that dries unevenly keeps moving after it is boxed. Milling explains harsh smoke, because an inconsistent particle size burns unevenly and produces a hotter, dirtier ember in places.

A video walk-through that follows the material from store to carton tells you more than a certificate. Certificates describe a system; the floor shows whether the system is operating on the day you looked. Ask for the walk-through unedited, with the batch code of your production run visible at the packing stage.

## What a Defect Costs at Each Stage

Quality failures are cheap at the bench and expensive in the market. Mapping the cost curve explains why experienced buyers spend money on sampling and inspection rather than on discounts.

| Stage where the defect is caught | What it costs | Who usually pays | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bench sample | A courier fee and a week | Buyer | Cheapest possible place to fail |
| Production sample | A rework cycle, a delayed run | Shared, by negotiation | Still cheap; fix the process here |
| Pre-shipment inspection | Rework at the factory, a short delay | Factory, if the spec is written | The last cheap checkpoint |
| Arrival at the warehouse | Sorting, scrapping, a replacement order | Buyer, unless terms say otherwise | The point where a discount stops helping |
| In the customer's hands | Refunds, reviews, delisting, chargebacks | Buyer, permanently | The cost that never appears in a quotation |

The curve is steep for a simple reason: every stage after the factory gate adds freight, handling, and time to the same physical problem. A cracked stick found on the drying rack is scrap. The same stick found in a retail box is scrap plus a shipment plus a customer complaint.

The table also explains a common negotiation error. Buyers push for a lower unit price and accept a thinner specification, which moves the expected defect cost to the last row. The saving is visible on the invoice; the cost is not.

## The Paperwork That Decides the Argument Later

Most sourcing disputes are not about goodwill; they are about a document that does not exist. The list is short and worth assembling before the first payment.

- **Specification sheet** — materials, dimensions, burn behaviour, packaging, and the tolerances the factory will accept.
- **Approved master sample** — sealed, dated, signed by both sides.
- **Formula ownership clause** — assignment or licence, with territory, term, and what happens on termination.
- **Tooling schedule** — what was made, who paid, who holds it, and the buy-out price.
- **Inspection record** — the pre-shipment check, with the sample size and the result.
- **Compliance declarations** — whatever the destination market requires, named specifically rather than promised generally.

A supplier who resists putting these in writing is telling you something. A supplier who supplies them without being asked is telling you something better.

## A Decision Matrix by What You Already Hold

The route question is easier when you start from what you actually have rather than from what you would like to have.

| What you hold | Route that fits | Why | What to settle first |
|---|---|---|---|
| A scent brief and a target customer | OEM | The product does not exist yet; the brief is the asset | Formula ownership and the sample loop |
| A brand, a channel, no product team | Private label | Speed matters more than distinctiveness at launch | Catalogue item, packaging, and the factory's spec sheet |
| A proven product from another factory | OEM or contract transfer | You need a second source without losing the formula | Specification transfer and a master sample |
| A retail chain and a price point | Private label, then OEM | Launch fast, learn the category, then differentiate | The upgrade path and the term of the supply agreement |
| A single hero SKU with a story | OEM | The story has to be true of the product | Exclusive rights and the documentation behind the claims |

The last row is the one buyers underestimate. A brand built on a story — a single-origin material, a traditional blend, a specific making method — needs a product that can substantiate the story. Private label can supply a good product, but it cannot supply a product that is uniquely yours, and a story that any competitor can also tell is not a moat.

## Scoring a Sample: A Review Rubric

Sample rounds go wrong when feedback is a feeling. A short rubric turns "I don't love it" into a set of observations the factory can act on, and it makes successive rounds comparable instead of impressionistic.

| Dimension | What to observe | Passing | Failing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scent fidelity | Does the dry stick, the first minute, and the last third match the brief? | All three recognizable; drift within agreed tolerance | The opening matches and nothing else does |
| Burn behaviour | Even travel, no tunnelling, no repeated relights | Steady ember, ash holds | Channel down one side, frequent extinctions |
| Smoke character | Volume and sharpness in a normal room | Light haze, no acrid edge | Visible cloud, throat-catching |
| Physical finish | Straightness, coating evenness, dust in the tube | Clean, consistent, minimal dust | Bent sticks, flaking coat, loose powder |
| Packaging fit | Does the stick sit correctly in the tube or box? | No movement, no crushing | Rattles, bends in transit |

Score each row on a three-point scale and write one sentence of justification. Two consecutive rounds with the same low score on the same row means the brief and the factory's process are not converging, and the conversation needs to move from samples to the process itself.

The rubric also protects against a subtle bias: the sample that arrives after a long wait feels better than it is. Scoring each dimension separately, before discussing the product as a whole, keeps the wait out of the assessment.

## Term Sheet: The Clauses That Decide the Relationship

Six clauses carry most of the risk in either route. They are unglamorous, and they are the difference between a supply arrangement and a dependency.

1. **Specification** — the written definition of the product, with tolerances. Without tolerances, every delivery is arguable.
2. **Ownership** — formula, artwork, tooling, and master sample, each named separately. A blanket "intellectual property" clause is not enough, because it rarely says which asset is whose.
3. **Exclusivity** — whether the factory may sell the same specification to anyone else, in which markets, and for how long. OEM without exclusivity is a custom product with a public future.
4. **Change control** — what happens when the factory substitutes a material, a supplier, or a process. Substitution without notice is the most common cause of a product that quietly changes between runs.
5. **Acceptance and remedy** — how a non-conforming batch is identified, who pays for it, and what the buyer may reject. A remedy clause that only offers a discount converts a quality problem into a pricing problem.
6. **Termination and transition** — what the buyer receives on exit: the specification, the master sample, the tooling, and a defined period of supply while a new source is qualified.

A factory that negotiates these clauses seriously is usually a factory that has been through them before. A factory that treats them as distrust is telling you how the first dispute will go.

## When to Walk Away

Some signals are not negotiable, because they predict a relationship rather than a product.

- **No production address.** A supplier who will not name the workshop cannot be inspected, and cannot be held to a process.
- **No specification in writing.** If the only definition of the product is a photograph, every delivery is arguable.
- **Refusal to provide a master sample.** A factory confident in its output will seal a reference sample. A factory that will not is telling you the output varies.
- **Ownership answered with reassurance.** "Don't worry, it's yours" is not a clause. Ask for the sentence that would appear in the contract.
- **A price far below the material cost of the stated ingredients.** Either the specification is not what it claims, or the material is not what the specification claims.

None of these require hostility to check. They require asking once, in writing, and reading the answer carefully.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Using the words as synonyms.** The contract you sign should name the arrangement, not the acronym. A purchase order that says "OEM" while describing a catalogue item creates an argument nobody can win.
2. **Comparing quotes without separating the cost drivers.** A delivered private-label quote and an ex-works OEM quote are not comparable numbers.
3. **Leaving ownership to the end.** Formula, artwork, and tooling are cheapest to settle before sampling and most expensive to settle after a successful launch.
4. **Treating a low minimum as a low risk.** A small first run is a good way to test a market; it is a poor way to test a factory's consistency, because consistency shows up across runs.
5. **Skipping the master sample.** Without a retained reference, every later quality conversation becomes a matter of memory.
6. **Assuming private label means inferior.** It means existing. The quality is whatever the factory's standard is, which is exactly what you verify.

## The Author's Position

**Position:** The route question is usually answered before the first email, by whether the buyer has a product or only a brand.

**Reasoning:** Every practical difference between the routes — the sample loop, the minimum, the ownership clause, the differentiation — is downstream of that one fact. A buyer with a brief is buying development, whether or not they use the word OEM. A buyer with only a brand is buying a catalogue item, whether or not they admit it. Naming the situation correctly is most of the work.

**Boundary:** This is a framework for choosing, not a claim that one route produces better incense. A private-label product from a disciplined factory will outperform a badly briefed OEM product every time. The route sets the ceiling on differentiation, not the floor on quality.

## FAQ

### Q: What is the difference between OEM and private label?

OEM is a build-to-specification contract: the factory makes a product around a brief you supply, and you direct the development. Private label is a selection contract: an existing product, chosen from a range, with branding applied to it. The shorthand is that OEM is development plus branding while private label is branding alone. The counterfactual test settles it — if the product would exist without you, it is private label.

### Q: Is private label cheaper than OEM?

Usually at the point of purchase, because the development and tooling costs were absorbed by whoever created the product first. That does not make it cheaper overall. Private label buys a product that competitors can also buy, and the savings are partly paid for in differentiation and in the absence of an owned recipe. OEM costs more up front and can produce an asset — a formula, a format, a defensible position — that private label cannot.

### Q: Can I start with private label and move to OEM later?

Yes, and it is a common path. Launch on a catalogue product to learn the channel, then commission a proprietary formula once the demand is proven. Two things make the transition work: keep the packaging and brand architecture flexible enough to survive a product change, and do not sign a supply agreement that prevents you from developing a competing specification. The risk in the transition is that customers associate your brand with the original product; a changed formula needs to be introduced, not swapped silently.

### Q: Who owns the formula in each route?

In private label, the factory owns the formula by default and you own the brand you apply to it. In OEM, ownership is a term of the contract rather than a consequence of the word, so it has to be written down: assignment, exclusive licence, or non-exclusive licence, with a defined territory and term. If the clause is silent, assume the factory keeps it.

### Q: How long does each route take?

Private label is production plus packaging and very little else, so scheduling and freight dominate its timeline. OEM adds a development loop — brief, sample, revision, production sample — and that loop is usually the longest part of the project. Neither has a universal duration, and any supplier who quotes one without knowing your specification is guessing. Ask for the timeline for your product, in writing, with the assumptions listed.

### Q: What should be in the first brief to a factory?

Enough that the factory can price and sample without guessing: the job the product does, the scent direction in sensory terms, the format and dimensions, the packaging concept, the target market, the intended volume, and the destination. A brief that omits the market and the volume cannot be quoted accurately, and a brief that omits the sensory direction cannot be sampled. One page of specifics saves several rounds of email.

### Q: What is the biggest mistake buyers make?

Settling ownership after the product succeeds. Once a product is selling, the factory has leverage and the buyer has sunk cost, and the conversation that should have happened at the start becomes a renegotiation. Formula, artwork, tooling, and the master sample are all cheap to define in the first meeting and expensive to revisit in the second year.

## Summary

- OEM builds a product around your brief; private label applies your brand to a product that already exists.
- The counterfactual test — would it exist without me? — identifies which contract you are actually in.
- Cost differences come from development, tooling, and run size, not from the acronym.
- Differentiation lives in the formula, so ownership of the formula is the decisive OEM term.
- Private label trades distinctiveness for speed and simplicity, and that trade is legitimate when it is deliberate.
- Both routes reward the same discipline: written specification, sealed master sample, and ownership settled before money moves.

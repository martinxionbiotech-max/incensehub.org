## Why You Cannot Shop This by Price

**Direct answer:** One word, "agarwood," spans a price curve measured in orders of magnitude, so a quote without a specification is a number about nothing — and a buyer who leads with price has skipped the entire decision.

The instinct to correct is the assumption that a name implies a stable, comparable product. Agarwood breaks that assumption completely. Different species, regions, grades, and production methods sit at price points that differ by factors of ten or more, so the same four syllables cover everything from commodity filler to museum-grade 奇楠.

The reason is structural: agarwood is a condition of wood, not a species → several *Aquilaria* species across many regions produce resin-saturated heartwood → each species-origin-density combination lands at its own point on a very wide price curve → the bare name identifies none of it. And because fakes concentrate where that curve looks cheapest, the buyer who negotiates before authenticating is negotiating against a counterfeit. Specification first, every time.

## The Two Questions That Anchor the Specification

**Direct answer:** Start with "which species" and "which region," and expect a specific answer — because agarwood is not one tree, and origin changes both aroma character and legal status.

Agarwood (沉香, chénxiāng) is a condition of the wood, not a species. The two names a manufacturing buyer meets most often are *Aquilaria sinensis* (白木香, traditionally harvested in southern China) and *Aquilaria malaccensis* (the main Southeast Asian commercial species, CITES-listed). The first specification question is therefore always: **which *Aquilaria* species, and which region?**

Origin matters for two reasons that go beyond aroma. First, regional material carries different market reputation and price — Hainan and Vietnamese material has long been the aromatic benchmark in Chinese trade. Second, origin interlocks with legal status: wild-harvested material from CITES-listed species may need permits, while cultivated plantation material is the renewable mainstream. The botanical and legal specifics are in the [agarwood ingredient page](/ingredients/agarwood/).

## Cultivated vs Wild Is a Paper Question First

**Direct answer:** Wild agarwood is scarce, slow to form, and legally sensitive; cultivated agarwood comes from inoculating farmed trees and is the renewable, lower-cost mainstream — and the buyer's first job is to find out which one is on the table.

Most commercial agarwood today is cultivated: farmed *Aquilaria* trees are deliberately inoculated to induce resin formation. Wild old-growth material is depleted and subject to CITES controls. The split carries three consequences for a manufacturer:

- **Cost** — wild and old-growth material commands far higher prices than plantation stock.
- **Consistency** — plantation material can be produced more repeatably, which matters when the product must be reorderable.
- **Documentation** — wild material, or material from CITES-listed species, may need permits the buyer must see before the goods move.

The rule is straightforward: a credible supplier states whether the material is wild or cultivated, names the species and origin, and produces the documents that support those claims. A supplier who offers only an unqualified "agarwood" label has not answered the specification.

## Resin Content Is the Quality Axis

**Direct answer:** Resin content drives agarwood quality — it sets density, aroma intensity, and longevity — and for manufacturing the question is "what grade can you supply repeatably," not "what is your best single piece."

Chinese practice grades agarwood by resin density using the water test: 沉水 (sinks) is the most resin-saturated and valuable, 半沉半浮 (half-sinks) is medium, and floating material is lower. 奇楠 (qínán, "kyara-grade") is the structurally distinct top grade — "oil wraps wood" rather than "wood wraps oil" — far rarer and more expensive than ordinary agarwood.

The mechanism that ties resin to burn deserves explicit form. Resin is the aromatic and combustible payload → more resin releases more fragrance per gram and sustains a longer, steadier ember → a high-resin chip smells richer and burns longer than a low-resin one of the same weight. That is why density, a proxy for resin, is the quality axis. For a manufacturer, grade means something different than it does for a collector: the question is whether the supplier can deliver a consistent grade, batch after batch, at a workable cost. A steady mid-grade material with known species and origin usually beats an irreproducible top grade in a product. The full grading vocabulary — including the 奇楠 distinction and the five-color hierarchy — is in the [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/), and the caution specific to buying 奇楠 is in the [qínán buying guide](/blog/qinan-buying-authentication/).

## Moisture and Processing State Set the Manufacturing Variables

**Direct answer:** Whether the material is raw wood, cleaned chips, or ground powder — and at what particle size and moisture — changes how it grinds, packs, burns, and stores, and the target is agreed with the supplier per order, not copied from a list.

The same resin wood behaves differently depending on its preparation. A buyer should specify, and the supplier should state:

- **Form** — whole pieces, chips, or powder. Chips suit warming and resale; powder suits blending into sticks and cones. See [powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).
- **Particle size** (for powder) — uniformity drives even burning and consistent packing; the right target depends on the binder and the other ingredients.
- **Moisture** — too much dulls the burn and risks mold in storage; too little makes the material brittle and fast-burning.

The moisture chain is the same one that governs drying and storage elsewhere: excess residual moisture → weak smolder plus mold risk → the material underperforms and can spoil; too little moisture → brittle, fast-burning material → the fragrance flashes off before the session. These are legitimate specification points, but they carry no universal correct number — a figure that works in one factory does not transfer to another. The buyer states the intended use and asks the supplier to confirm form, particle size, and moisture for that application. This is the discipline of the [manufacturing quality-control guide](/blog/incense-manufacturing-quality-control/).

## Adulteration Is the Risk Behind Every Quote

**Direct answer:** Expensive agarwood attracts fakes — dyed white wood, unrelated woods treated with fragrance, and oil-injected low-grade material — and screening happens on a sample, with laboratory testing reserved for high-value lots.

Adulteration is the single largest risk in agarwood sourcing, and it concentrates where the price looks best. The documented fakes:

- **Dyed or oil-soaked white wood** — non-resinous *Aquilaria* wood soaked in fragrance oil and darkened.
- **Foreign woods** — unrelated species treated with synthetic fragrance and sold under the agarwood name.
- **Oil-injected material** — low-grade wood pumped with resin or oil to add weight so it passes density checks.

The last fake defeats the most common test, and a buyer should understand the trick cold. Density is the traditional quality check → a faker injects oil or resin into low-grade wood → the wood gains weight and sinks → the density test passes while the material remains inferior. Density is therefore a screening signal, never proof. Practical checks — burning a small piece for smoke character, smelling it on gentle heat, testing density — are traditional trade heuristics, not laboratory proof. A genuine piece tends to give white smoke with oil seepage and a clean, complex, sweet aroma; fakes often give black smoke and a "burnt wood" or one-note "perfume" smell. These screening methods sit inside the wider [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) and the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

## Sampling: What It Can and Cannot Prove

**Direct answer:** A sample verifies the product — species, grade, aroma, form — but not the supplier's ability to reproduce it at volume, so pull your own sample, check it against the written spec, keep it as the reference, and reserve lab testing for lots where the value justifies it.

The sequence for agarwood:

1. **State the specification in writing** — species, origin, cultivated vs wild, grade, form, particle size, moisture.
2. **Request a sample against that specification** — and pull it yourself where possible, rather than accepting a hand-picked piece.
3. **Check aroma on gentle heat** — the decisive test; it should be complex and evolving, not flat or "burnt."
4. **Check density and smoke** — as screening signals, with the caveat that fakes are engineered to pass the sink test.
5. **Keep the approved sample as the reference standard** — every later batch is checked against it.
6. **Reserve laboratory testing** — for high-value or high-volume lots, documented verification of species and authenticity is the reliable route.

The boundary worth stating plainly: a sample cannot tell you about batch-to-batch consistency, delivery reliability, or scale capability — those are exactly what a trial order and supplier references verify. A sample is a snapshot of the product, not a guarantee of the supplier. The general sampling logic is in the [samples program](/blog/samples-program/).

## Packaging, Storage, and Shipping: Protecting the Specification

## How Resin Forms in the Tree (and Why It Sets the Price)

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is a wound response, not a harvest — the tree makes resin to seal an injury, and the price of a lot tracks how much of that defense the tree had time to lay down.

The chain is the biology behind every grade: a wild *Aquilaria* tree suffers a wound — a storm break, an insect bore, a fungal invasion → the tree secretes oleoresin into the injured heartwood to seal and sterilize the damage → the resin saturates the surrounding fiber and hardens it → over years the saturated zone darkens and densifies → the water test sorts the result by whether it sinks, half-sinks, or floats → the sinker is the most resin-transformed and the most valuable. Cultivated trees skip the natural wound with a deliberate inoculation — a drill wound or an injected inoculant — which triggers the same response faster and more predictably, at the cost of a younger, less saturated resin.

For a manufacturer the chain matters because it explains the whole price spread and the whole quality axis at once. Resin is the payload — the aroma and the burn — so "more resin" is not a marketing word, it is a density statement, and density is what you are actually buying per gram.

## A Table: Match the Grade to the Product, Not the Prestige

**Direct answer:** A manufacturer does not buy "the best agarwood" — a manufacturer buys the grade that a given product can actually carry, and the table maps the tiers to the jobs.

| Grade tier | Density signal | Aroma and burn | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Floating (low grade) | Floats on water | Light, fast, less resin | Base filler in sticks and cones |
| Half-sinking (半沉) | Half-floats | Medium body, decent burn | Everyday sticks, blended incense |
| Sinking (沉水) | Sinks fully | Rich, long, resin-dense | Premium sticks, chip resale |
| 奇楠 (kyara-grade) | Soft, oil-rich | Intense, room-temperature aroma | Collector chips, top-tier products |

The table corrects the prestige reflex. A sinking-grade chip is wasted ground into a mass-market cone, because its cost adds nothing the cone's price can carry; a floating grade cannot hold up a premium stick whose whole promise is depth. The sourcing question is not "what is your best piece" but "what grade can you deliver repeatably at the cost my product can bear" — and the two are rarely the same row.

## What Happens When the Chips Become Powder

**Direct answer:** Converting chips to powder is not just size reduction — it changes how the material burns, blends, and keeps, and the particle size you specify decides the stick you can make.

The chain: whole chips are loaded and irregular → grinding reduces them to particles → the particle size distribution sets how tightly the powder packs in a stick or cone → a uniform fine grind packs evenly and burns at a steady rate → a coarse or uneven grind leaves voids that make the ember sputter and jump → the binder then has to compensate for whatever the grind did not. In other words, particle size is a burn-quality lever before the binder ever enters the formula. A fine, consistent powder gives a smooth, even ember; a ragged grind gives a stick that flares and stalls.

The same property matters in storage: fine powder has far more exposed surface than chips, so it oxidizes and loses volatiles faster, and it clumps if any moisture is present. Specify the particle size for the product you are making, and treat powder as a shorter-shelf-life form than chips — the grind is a decision, not a default.

## The Moisture Loop That Spoils a Lot in Storage

**Direct answer:** Agarwood bought with hidden residual moisture does not stay the same in your warehouse — it either molds or cracks, and both failures trace to a single cycle of water moving through the material.

The chain: the wood arrives carrying more moisture than the spec stated → in a closed carton the trapped humidity cannot escape → the damp fibers and the resin-rich zones become a substrate for mold, which stains and taints the aroma → if instead the storage runs too dry and hot, the wood sheds water unevenly → the surface dries and contracts faster than the core → fine cracks open along the resin veins → the chip becomes brittle and the fragrance flashes off too fast when burned. Both ends of the cycle — too wet, too dry — degrade the same lot through the same physical driver: water moving in or out of the fiber at uneven rates.

For a buyer the fix is procedural, not chemical. State the moisture target in the specification, confirm it on arrival with a quick weight-and-dry check rather than trusting the paperwork, and store the material in a stable, moderate environment sealed against both humidity and furnace-dry air. Moisture is not a footnote in the spec; it is a spoilage vector that starts the moment the lot lands.

Wild, old-growth, sinking-grade material is objectively more resin-dense and more expensive, but a product line needs *consistency*, not a single trophy lot — and wild material is the hardest to reproduce. ## Why Oil Injection Beats the Sink Test

**Direct answer:** The oldest quality check — drop the wood in water and see if it sinks — is defeated by a specific counterfeit, and understanding the trick is the difference between screening and trusting.

The chain: genuine agarwood sinks because its resin has displaced the wood's air spaces and raised its density above water → the sink test reads density as a proxy for resin → a faker takes low-grade, floating wood and injects resin or oil into its vessels under pressure → the added liquid fills the same air spaces the natural resin would have → the wood gains weight and now sinks → the density check passes while the resin content — and the aroma — remain inferior. The test is defeated at exactly the step where it was supposed to discriminate.

That is why density is a screening signal, never proof, and why the burn and the gentle-heat aroma are the checks that still hold: an injected fake gives off black smoke, a flat or "burnt" smell, and an oily residue, where genuine resinous wood gives white smoke with oil seepage and a complex, evolving aroma. The sink test tells you density; the burn tells you what that density is made of.

## The Sensory Screen, Done Properly

**Direct answer:** Before any lab test, a buyer should screen a sample with their own senses — on gentle heat, not open flame — because the aroma and the smoke are the two checks that a density test alone cannot fake-proof.

The sequence is the same three checks a careful trade buyer runs, in order: warm a small piece on low, indirect heat → smell for a complex, evolving, sweet aroma rather than a flat or "burnt" note → watch the smoke for white with oil seepage rather than black → note the residue and the feel of the wood. These are traditional heuristics, not laboratory standards, and they are the practical first pass that decides whether a lot is worth a lab test at all. The point is not to certify the lot by nose — it is to catch the obvious fakes cheaply, before spending on analysis.

The boundary to keep in mind: the sensory screen is a filter, not a verdict. It catches the blatant counterfeit and flags the promising lot; only laboratory identification of species and resin actually settles the question for a high-value purchase.

**Direct answer:** A defensible agarwood lot is not just wood — it is wood plus a paper trail, and each document in that trail verifies a different claim the wood itself cannot prove.

## Instrumental Checks vs the Nose

**Direct answer:** The traditional sensory screen and the laboratory instrument answer different questions — the nose judges aroma, the instrument identifies species and quantifies resin — and a careful buyer uses both, in the right order.

The chain: the sensory screen is cheap and fast → it catches blatant fakes (black smoke, flat smell) → but it cannot name a species or measure resin → the instrument (chromatography, spectroscopy, or DNA identification) does exactly that → it costs money and takes time → so it is reserved for lots where the value justifies it. The nose filters; the lab confirms. Neither replaces the other.

This is the honest division of labor: trust the nose for the first pass, and send the lot to the lab when the price, the volume, or the stakes make a definitive answer worth paying for.

## Why Wild Harvest Keeps Shrinking

**Direct answer:** Wild agarwood is getting rarer for structural reasons — habitat loss, slow regeneration, and heavy regulation — and that scarcity is exactly what both raises prices and feeds the counterfeit trade.

The chain: wild *Aquilaria* grows in forests that are being cleared → the tree regenerates slowly and resin forms only after years of wounding → CITES restrictions make legal wild material harder to move → supply thins while demand holds → prices climb → the high price attracts adulterators. The same scarcity that makes wild material valuable is what makes it a magnet for fraud, which is why plantation cultivation has become the commercial mainstream.

For the buyer, the chain explains why "wild" is not automatically a better purchase — it is scarcer, more regulated, and more likely to be counterfeited precisely because of its value. A documented cultivated lot is often the more rational commercial choice.

## The Chemistry Inside the Resin

**Direct answer:** Agarwood's fragrance is not one molecule but a library of sesquiterpenes and chromone compounds, and naming them is the difference between "nice wood" and a specification you can actually compare.

The chain: the resin-saturated heartwood accumulates sesquiterpene compounds — agarospirol, jinkohol, kusunol, and related jinkoh-family molecules → these heavier, oxygenated compounds give agarwood its warm, woody, slightly sweet-and-bitter signature → alongside them sit 2-(2-phenylethyl)chromone derivatives, a structural family chemists use to fingerprint agarwood and tell it from substitutes → the presence and ratio of these compounds is what a laboratory actually measures when it verifies a lot. The aroma is the surface of this chemistry, and the chemistry is what a buyer's money ultimately purchases.

This is descriptive chemistry, not a health claim. The compounds are named to explain identity and aroma; nothing here asserts any effect on the body.

## FAQ

### Q: How do I buy agarwood for incense manufacturing?
Buy it as a specification, not as a name. Write down species, origin, cultivated vs wild status, grade, form (chips or powder), particle size, and moisture, then ask the supplier to confirm each and provide supporting documentation. Sample against that specification, authenticate the sample, keep it as the reference standard, and confirm MOQ, price, packaging, and shipping per inquiry. A trial order then verifies the supplier can reproduce the sample at volume. The sequence — specify, vet, sample, negotiate, trial-order — is the whole discipline.

### Q: Is cultivated agarwood suitable for incense manufacturing?
Yes, and it is the mainstream for commercial production. Cultivated (plantation) agarwood is produced by inoculating farmed *Aquilaria* trees to induce resin formation, which makes it renewable, more repeatable, and lower-cost than depleted wild material. It is typically lower in grade than old-growth wild agarwood, but for a product that must be reorderable to a consistent specification, that consistency is an advantage. A buyer should still ask which species, which origin, and which grade, because "cultivated" describes how it was produced, not its quality on its own.

### Q: What should I check when buying agarwood in bulk?
Check the specification first — species, origin, wild vs cultivated, grade, form, particle size, and moisture — then the material. Pull a sample and verify aroma on gentle heat, density, and smoke character as screening signals, with laboratory testing for high-value lots. Then check the supplier — identity, capability, references, and documentation, including any CITES permits for listed material. Finally, confirm MOQ, price, packaging, storage, and shipping terms per inquiry. Specification, sample, supplier, terms — in that order.

### Q: How do I avoid fake agarwood?
Start with the seller, not the material: a credible supplier can name the species, origin, and grade, and state whether the material is wild or cultivated. On a sample, screen with the burn test (white smoke with oil seepage vs black smoke), gentle-heat aroma (complex and sweet vs "burnt" or one-note "perfume"), and density — remembering that oil-injection fakes are engineered to pass the sink test. For expensive lots, laboratory verification is the reliable route. The full screening logic is in the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

### Q: What documentation should I request for agarwood?
Request a species and origin statement, a wild vs cultivated declaration, and — for material from CITES-listed species — the permits the shipment requires. For powder, also confirm particle size and moisture. A certificate is a claim, not a guarantee, so verify it against the issuing body, and tie the document to the specific lot you are buying rather than accepting a generic statement. See [factory vetting](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/) for the full document checklist.

### Q: Is there a minimum order quantity for agarwood?
There is no universal figure, because the minimum depends on the grade, form, quantity, and destination — which is why any honest supplier confirms it per inquiry rather than publishing a number. The useful question is not "what is your MOQ" but "what is the MOQ for *this* grade at *this* quantity," answered at the quotation stage. See the [MOQ & pricing guide](/blog/moq-pricing-guide/).

### Q: Why is wild agarwood so much more expensive than cultivated?
The gap comes down to scarcity and formation time, not just quality. Wild agarwood forms only after a tree is naturally wounded and spends years laying down resin, and wild old-growth forests are both depleted and restricted by CITES controls → legal wild supply is thin → demand holds → the price climbs sharply above plantation stock. Cultivated agarwood shortens that timeline by inoculating farmed trees deliberately, so it can be produced renewably and at scale. The wild material can be more resin-dense, but the price difference is driven as much by rarity and regulation as by aroma.

## Summary

- Agarwood is a specification, not a commodity; the same label spans orders of magnitude in price.
- Pin species, origin, wild-vs-cultivated, grade, form, moisture, and processing before any price talk.
- Adulteration is the deepest risk, and the sink test can be beaten by oil injection — density is a screen, not proof.
- A sample verifies the product, not the supplier; a trial order verifies reproducibility.
- Price, MOQ, and documentation are confirmed per inquiry — never from a published list.

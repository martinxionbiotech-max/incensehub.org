**Direct answer:** A purchase of agarwood for a product line is an exercise in writing things down. Before committing volume, the buyer fixes seven answers for each consignment — tree species, district of origin, farm or forest, resin band, delivery form, grind, water content — and then proves those answers on material drawn with their own hands. Money and minimums come later, because both are settled per inquiry.

**Key facts:** What follows is written for people who will grind, blend, and press this wood into sticks, cones, and loose incense, not for collectors hunting a single display piece. It follows the order a real procurement decision runs in. It publishes no prices, minimums, or capacities, since those depend on band, form, volume, and destination — any figure printed here would be invented.

## Key Takeaways

- The word "agarwood" specifies nothing on its own. It names a resin condition that several species can produce, and the bands inside it sit far apart in price.
- Seven written lines — species, district, farm or forest, resin band, form, grind, water content — are the purchase document. A quotation is a footnote to that document.
- Density is the traditional proxy for resin, and oil injection exists to beat it. Treat a sinking piece as a hint, not a verdict.
- A sample proves that one product exists. Only a trial run proves that a plant can repeat it.
- Fraud gathers where the discount is, so screening has to happen before the haggling, not after it.
- Documents are claims with an author behind them. Each one has to be checked against its issuer and its lot.

## What the Word "Agarwood" Actually Names

**Direct answer:** The word names a transformation inside a tree, not a tree, and several *Aquilaria* species can undergo it — which is why the opening question is a species name and a place, not a price.

A healthy *Aquilaria* trunk smells of very little. What turns it into 沉香 is damage and the response to damage: bark splits, a beetle bores in, a fungus takes hold, and the tree floods the injured tissue with a dark, sticky oleoresin that seals the breach and shuts out the infection. The resin soaks outward through the grain, thickens with age, and eventually leaves a band of wood that is heavier, darker, and aromatic when warmed. Growers call the raw material 白木香 while it is still pale host wood.

Two species names come up in trade more than any others. *Aquilaria sinensis* is the Chinese tree, worked for centuries across the southern provinces. *Aquilaria malaccensis* is the Southeast Asian workhorse and appears on the CITES appendices, so a shipment of it carries paperwork obligations that a buyer inherits.

Where the wood grew matters twice over, and the two meanings can pull apart. Ask a trader which wood is best and Hainan or Vietnam usually comes back first, a ranking built over generations of sniffing rather than over any test a lab can run. In the customs file, the place of harvest decides which permits apply and which destinations will admit the consignment. A buyer who records only the first is exposed on the second. Background on the species and its regulation sits on the [agarwood ingredient page](/ingredients/agarwood/).

## Plantation Wood, Forest Wood, and the Paper Behind Each

**Direct answer:** Forest-collected material is limited, slow to form, and regulated; plantation material is grown on farms and wounded on purpose, which makes it the renewable bulk of the trade — and the buyer's opening task is to establish, in writing, which is on offer.

Almost everything sold in commercial volume now comes off farms. Growers plant *Aquilaria*, bore or inoculate the trunks to start the resin response, leave the trees to work, and cut on a schedule. Chance injury is replaced by a decision, and the supply becomes something you can plan around rather than something you wait for.

Three practical differences follow for anyone pressing the material into sticks:

- **What it costs** — forest and old-growth wood sits far above plantation stock, and the gap reflects scarcity and permit work as much as what the resin does.
- **Whether it repeats** — farmed wood can be brought to a similar grade across harvests, and a product line lives or dies on that.
- **What travels with it** — forest wood, and any listed species, may need papers that must exist before the carton leaves, not after it lands.

The test of a seller is easy to describe and awkward to pass: can they name the tree, name the district, say whether the wood came off a farm or out of a forest, and hand over paper that supports each answer? A reply containing only the word "agarwood" has answered nothing at all. A documented plantation lot can be the better purchase even where forest wood wins on the nose, because the product needs a supply it can order again.

## Reading Grade as a Production Input

**Direct answer:** The trade sorts agarwood by how much resin the wood carries, using water as the instrument — 沉水 goes under, 半沉半浮 hangs between, the rest floats — while 奇楠 stands outside that ladder altogether; for a factory the question is which band can be bought again, not which band is finest.

Resin is the part that burns and smells. Wood holding more of it weighs more, and weight is what a bowl of water measures, so the old test became the trade's shorthand for payload. Follow the logic and it stays sensible: resin occupies the spaces where air used to sit → the piece gets heavier → it drops below the surface → the sorter reads that as resin content → resin is what gives off scent under heat. A scale and a basin, in other words, standing in for a chemistry bench.

奇楠 does not belong on that ladder. Traders describe it as oil holding wood rather than wood holding oil — soft, damp with resin, fragrant before it is ever lit — and it is rare enough that it supports its own market with its own fraud risk. The separate conversation is in the [qínán buying guide](/blog/qinan-buying-authentication/); the wider vocabulary, including the five-colour hierarchy, is in the [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/).

A collector and a factory read the same grade and reach different conclusions. The collector asks whether one extraordinary piece exists. The factory asks whether the same band will arrive again in three months at a cost the finished item can absorb. A plain mid-band lot with a known species and district usually serves a product better than a spectacular one that cannot be repeated, because the product's value lives in the repetition rather than in the peak.

## Matching Grade to Product: A Sourcing Matrix

**Direct answer:** No product needs the best agarwood available; each product needs the grade whose cost it can absorb and whose aroma it can actually express, and the matrix below is the working version of that judgment.

| Band | What water does | What heat does | Where it belongs in a range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Floating | Stays up | Thin, short, mild | Supporting note inside sticks and cones, never the headline |
| 半沉半浮 | Hangs mid-basin | Medium body, fades slowly | Daily stick lines, blends, mid-tier coils |
| 沉水 | Goes down | Dense, long, resin-forward | Premium sticks, chip sales, warming sets |
| 奇楠 | Soft enough to mark | Fragrant before lighting | Collector stock and niche top-tier pieces only |

Read the matrix as a cost-containment tool. Grinding sinking-grade wood into a mass-market cone destroys value, because the cone's price cannot carry the material and its smoke output is dominated by binder and filler anyway. Pushing floating-grade material into a premium stick does the opposite kind of damage: the product promises depth it cannot deliver, and the customer notices at the first burn. The sourcing question is not "what is your best piece" but "which grade can you deliver repeatedly at a cost my product can bear," and those are usually different rows of the same table.

## Form, Grind, and Moisture: The Settings the Buyer Owns

**Direct answer:** Whether the lot arrives as whole pieces, chips, or powder, at what particle size, and carrying how much water, decides how it grinds, packs, burns, and keeps — and none of those values has a universal correct answer.

Form is the first fork. Chips suit warming, display, and resale, and they preserve the material's structure so a buyer can still inspect it. Powder suits blending into sticks and cones, and it removes the buyer's ability to see what went into the grinder. That loss of visibility is itself a risk factor, which is why powder purchases deserve more documentation rather than less. The trade-offs are set out in [powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

Particle size is a burn lever that operates before any binder is added. The chain runs: whole chips are irregular and load unevenly → grinding produces a distribution of particle sizes → a tight, fine distribution packs into a stick with few voids → the ember advances at a steady rate → a coarse or wide distribution leaves channels and pockets → the burn sputters, jumps, and races. A buyer who specifies "powder" without a particle target has handed the burn quality to the grinder.

Moisture is the third setting and the one most often left unstated. Excess water dulls the smoulder and invites mould in a sealed carton; too little leaves the fibre brittle, and the aromatic fraction flashes off faster than it should. Neither failure shows up at the factory gate. Both show up weeks later in the warehouse, which is why the moisture target belongs in the written specification and is checked on arrival rather than accepted from the paperwork. The discipline mirrors the one in the [manufacturing quality-control guide](/blog/incense-manufacturing-quality-control/).

## How Resin Forms, and Why the Price Spread Is So Wide

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is a wound response rather than a crop, and the width of its price range tracks how much of that defence a tree had time and cause to lay down.

The biological chain explains the commercial one: an *Aquilaria* trunk is injured by storm, insect, or fungal entry → the tree secretes oleoresin into the damaged heartwood to seal the breach → the resin migrates into surrounding fibre and hardens → repeated or prolonged injury extends the saturated zone → years of accumulation darken and densify the wood → the water test sorts the harvest into sinkers, half-sinkers, and floaters → the sorter's result becomes the grade, and the grade becomes the price.

Cultivation intervenes at the second step. Growers wound or inoculate the trunk deliberately, which starts the same defence on a schedule. The result is faster, more uniform, and far cheaper than waiting on nature, with the trade-off that a young, quickly induced resin is generally less saturated than old wild material. That single substitution — scheduled wounding instead of accidental wounding — is what turned agarwood from a rarity into a manufacturable input, and it is also why "cultivated" says something about provenance and nothing at all about grade.

## The Three Families of Fake, and Why Density Alone Fails

**Direct answer:** Adulteration in agarwood comes in three recurring forms — dyed or oil-soaked white wood, unrelated woods carrying fragrance, and oil-injected low-grade material — and the third exists specifically to defeat the density test buyers rely on.

White wood is the un-resined *Aquilaria* host material. It is cheap, it looks like wood, and it can be darkened and perfumed into something that photographs well. Foreign woods follow the same logic with a different starting material: a unrelated species treated with fragrance and sold under the agarwood name. Oil injection is the cleverest of the three, and the chain is worth following: genuine agarwood sinks because resin has displaced the air in its vessels → buyers use sinking as a proxy for resin → an injector forces oil or resin into low-grade wood under pressure → the liquid fills the same voids natural resin would occupy → the piece gains weight and now sinks → the test that was supposed to discriminate now certifies the fake.

What survives that trick is the burn and the nose. Good wood answers heat with something that keeps changing — sweet, then woody, then faintly bitter — and its smoke runs pale with oil beading where the flame touches. Loaded material answers with a single flat note, smoke that darkens, and a greasy ring left on the paper beneath it. Those are trade heuristics rather than laboratory standards, and they are the right first filter because they are cheap and immediate. The wider screening logic, including cases where a substitute is legitimate rather than fraudulent, is in the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) and the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

## A Sampling Protocol That Survives a Real Sack

**Direct answer:** Sampling is where a buyer stops reading and starts testing, and the protocol that works is unglamorous: draw material from several depths of the consignment, mix it, split it, and seal the splits so that a later dispute has evidence attached to it.

A hand-picked piece proves that one beautiful piece exists. It proves nothing about the sack behind it. The protocol closes that gap in four moves. First, draw incrementally — from the top, the middle, and the bottom of the container, and from more than one container if the lot spans several. Second, combine the draws into a composite so the sample represents the consignment rather than a lucky corner. Third, split the composite into sealed portions and label them with the lot code, and keep one portion unopened. Fourth, test the working portion against the written specification, recording what you observed while you observed it.

The sealed portions matter more than buyers expect. One goes to the supplier as the agreed reference, one stays with the buyer, and a third can be held for an independent check if the material later diverges. That arrangement converts a conversation into a record, and it is the cheapest insurance available in this trade.

What the sample cannot do is equally important. It cannot demonstrate batch-to-batch consistency, delivery reliability, or production capacity, because those are properties of a supplier rather than of a piece of wood. A trial order and references are what test those, and the general sampling logic is set out in the [samples program](/blog/samples-program/).

## How Much Material to Sample

**Direct answer:** A sample has to be large enough to expose variation and random enough to represent the lot, and those two requirements pull against each other every time a buyer draws by hand.

Drawing by hand tends toward the accessible. The top layer is what a supplier wants seen, the middle is what a buyer can reach, and the bottom is where dust, moisture, and foreign matter collect. Random selection fixes the bias, not the size: number the bags, pick the numbers from a list generated before the visit, and take an increment from each selected bag. Stratify when the lot visibly differs by layer or by pallet, because a single composite drawn across visibly different material hides the difference instead of measuring it.

Sample size is a decision about consequences. The question is not how much material you can afford to test but how much evidence you need before accepting a consignment whose failure would stop a production line. A lot headed for a premium line justifies more increments than a lot headed for a filler blend, and a lot from a new supplier justifies more than one from a source with a long record.

Destructive tests consume the sample, which forces a split before any testing begins: one portion for the physical checks, one for the laboratory, one sealed as the reference. Keep the split recorded, because a result that cannot be tied to a portion is a result nobody can defend six months later.

The limit of any sampling plan is worth stating plainly. It estimates the lot; it does not certify it. A composite that tests clean tells you the material you drew is acceptable, and the arithmetic of sampling means a small defect rate can pass a modest sample without ever being seen.

## Units, Weight, and the Arithmetic of a Consignment

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is priced by mass and judged by volume, and every weighing convention between the seller's yard and the buyer's store — gross, tare, net, moisture-adjusted — is a place where money quietly changes hands.

Gross weight includes the packaging, tare is the packaging alone, and net is the difference. That sounds trivial until a load travels in drums or double-lined cartons whose tare is a meaningful share of the total, and until the packing list quotes one convention while the invoice applies another. Ask which figure the price attaches to, and write the convention into the specification rather than leaving it to whoever prepares the invoice.

Moisture-adjusted weight is the convention that matters most in a botanical trade. Wood carrying more water weighs more for the same fibre, so a wet lot can look cheaper per kilo while delivering less material per unit of finished goods. Two routes neutralise this: price on a dry-weight basis using a measured moisture figure, or agree a reference moisture and adjust the invoiced weight in proportion. Either route needs the measurement method named in the document, otherwise the adjustment becomes a negotiation instead of arithmetic.

Volume is the other half, and it is where freight planning goes wrong. A container fills by volume long before it reaches its mass limit when the cargo is light and bulky, while dense sinking-grade pieces reach the weight limit with space left over. That difference decides pallet configuration, carton dimensions, and whether a freight quotation survives contact with the load.

Scale discipline closes the gap. A yard scale that has not been checked drifts, and a drifting scale is invisible until a dispute over a shortfall that nobody caused. Record the reading, the date, and the instrument, and keep the printed ticket with the intake record.

| Term | What it means | Where the error hides |
|---|---|---|
| Gross weight | Material plus packaging | Compared against a net figure elsewhere in the file |
| Tare | Packaging alone | Estimated rather than weighed |
| Net weight | Material as delivered | Used as though it were dry weight |
| Dry-weight basis | Weight adjusted to a stated moisture | Method unstated, adjustment arbitrary |
| Shrinkage | Loss between dispatch and arrival | Attributed to theft when it was water |
| Stowage factor | Space a given mass occupies | Ignored, so freight quotes miss the real constraint |

## Reading a Supplier's Technical Data Sheet

**Direct answer:** A data sheet is a set of claims with a method behind each one, and the buyer's job is to separate the guaranteed limit from the typical figure and the measured value from the adjective.

Start with the difference between typical and guaranteed. A typical value describes what the supplier usually sees; a guaranteed limit is a promise the supplier can be held to. Buyers who treat a typical figure as a specification find at the first dispute that nothing was promised. Ask which column the contract will reference, and write that answer into the purchase document.

Then look for the method. Moisture measured by oven drying and moisture measured by an electronic meter are different numbers on the same sample, and a sheet printing a figure without a method has printed a rumour. Particle size follows the same rule: a mesh number and a micron range are not interchangeable, and a distribution described only by its finest fraction says nothing about the coarse tail that decides how a stick packs.

Check the basis of every percentage. Resin content on a dry basis, volatile content on an as-received basis, ash after ignition — each is a legitimate convention and each produces a different figure for the same material. A sheet that mixes bases without labelling them is not lying; it is unreadable, which is worse for a buyer trying to compare two suppliers.

Read the revision block last. A sheet with no date, no version, and no author cannot be cited in a contract, and a sheet revised without a change note tells you the supplier's document control is informal. Neither observation condemns the material. Both tell you what to verify yourself.

| Sheet claim | What to ask | Why the answer changes the purchase |
|---|---|---|
| "Moisture: max X%" | Measured by which method, on which portion? | Two methods give two numbers on one sample |
| "Mesh" with a number | Which sieve standard, and what passes the coarse tail? | Distribution decides packing and burn |
| "Resin content" | Dry basis or as received? | One lot reads differently on each basis |
| "Typical" figure | Is any limit guaranteed? | Typical is a description, not a promise |
| "Natural" | Natural in species, or in process? | An adjective is not an identity claim |
| Revision block | Date, version, author | An undated sheet cannot be contracted |

## The Paper Trail, and What Each Document Can Prove

**Direct answer:** A defensible lot travels with a set of documents rather than one, and each document answers a different question — which is why a buyer should read them as claims to be checked rather than as proof of authenticity.

| Document | The claim it carries | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| Species and origin statement | Which tree, which region | Whether the statement is accurate |
| Certificate of analysis | Resin content, moisture, particle size as tested | That the tested sample represents the lot |
| CITES permit, where the species is listed | Legal standing for the shipment | That the wood matches the permit |
| Phytosanitary certificate | The consignment is free of listed pests | Species identity |
| Certificate of origin | Where the goods shipped from | Where they were grown |

The lesson in that table is the mismatch between what a buyer wants and what paper delivers. Paper establishes that someone made a claim in a particular form. Verification establishes whether the claim is true, and verification means checking the issuer's authority, checking the document against the lot code, and where the value justifies it, checking the material itself. A thick file is not evidence; it is a list of assertions waiting to be tested.

## Commissioning a Laboratory Test

**Direct answer:** A laboratory answers the question it was paid to ask about the specimen it received, so the buyer's job is to choose the question, control the specimen, and read the report for the boundary of what it covers.

Three questions justify an invoice. The first is identity: does the anatomy match the species claimed, or is this another wood wearing the name? A report that names the method and the features observed is worth more than a line announcing "agarwood confirmed", because the first can be argued with and the second cannot. The second is water, measured on a portion drawn from the consignment, since moisture decides mould, brittleness, and how the fibre behaves under a press. The third is whatever residue question a destination market asks of a botanical sold for burning — pesticide residues, heavy metals, microbial load on powder. Commission those where a rule requires them or a specific suspicion justifies the cost; a test whose result would not change any decision is an ornament with an invoice.

Chain of custody is the part buyers neglect. A laboratory certifies the bag it opened, not the ton in the container, and that distinction collapses the moment the bag cannot be tied back to the container. Seal the portion in front of the supplier's representative where the visit allows it, write the lot code and the date across the seal, photograph the sealed bag beside the container marking, and keep the courier record with the file. When the report comes back it should name the code the buyer wrote, not a supplier's internal reference that nobody outside the yard can resolve.

Read the result for three boundaries. Which method was used, because two accepted methods for moisture can disagree by enough to matter at the edge of a tolerance. Which portion was tested, because a hand-picked slice and a composite draw are different specimens wearing the same description. Whether the facility's accreditation covers that specific test, because an accredited laboratory can still issue an unaccredited result. None of those boundaries makes a report useless; they tell the buyer how far the conclusion travels before it stops being evidence.

| Question | What a laboratory can settle | What it leaves with the buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Which species | Anatomy of the submitted piece | Whether that piece represents the sack |
| How much water | Moisture of the tested portion | How the rest of the consignment equilibrated |
| Is the powder clean | Microbial and residue results on that sample | Whether the mill ran a previous lot through the same equipment |
| Is the resin load as claimed | A measured figure under a named method | Whether the figure holds across the lot |

A certificate that says "passes" without naming a limit has answered a question nobody asked. Ask for the figure, the method, and the sample identity, and file all three beside the intake record.

## Writing a Specification Sheet a Factory Can Sign

**Direct answer:** The seven lines are a request; a specification is a numbered document with units, methods, a revision number, and a signature block, and the difference between the two decides who absorbs the ambiguity.

Ambiguity always has an owner. A line reading "sinking grade" hands the definition to whoever inspects the load, which in practice means the party with less leverage at the moment of disagreement. A line reading "sinks within the observation time stated in clause 4, at the piece size and water temperature also stated there" puts the definition inside a document both sides signed. The same shift applies to moisture (method, portion, tolerance either side of target) and to grind (sieve designation, oversize allowance, milling date).

Build the sheet in the order a dispute would be argued. Identity first: species, district, farm or forest, supplier lot reference. Physical description second: form, dimensions or particle distribution, colour range, foreign-matter limit. Condition third: moisture, odour at first opening, freedom from mould. Documents fourth: which certificates travel, who issues them, and what each must name. Acceptance fifth: who inspects, where, within what period, against which sealed reference. Number every clause, because a numbered clause can be cited in a claim and a paragraph cannot.

Revision control looks like bureaucracy until the second order. The sheet that governed the sample is one version; the sheet that governs production may be another, and a factory handed a new version without a change note will keep running the old settings. Date each revision, list what changed, and require an acknowledgement. A specification that lives only in an email thread has no history, and history is what makes a drift visible before it becomes a product problem.

| Clause | Weak wording | Wording a factory can be held to |
|---|---|---|
| Grade | "Sinking grade" | Test method, piece size, water temperature, observation time |
| Moisture | "Dry" | Method, tested portion, tolerance either side of target |
| Grind | "Fine powder" | Sieve designation, oversize allowance, milling date |
| Origin | "Southeast Asia" | District, farm or forest, supplier lot code |
| Acceptance | "Buyer's satisfaction" | Inspector, window, sealed reference, remedy |

## Payment, Freight, and the Paperwork That Moves With the Load

**Direct answer:** Commercial terms decide when money leaves and who carries risk on each mile, and a buyer who has compared only unit prices has compared the least important variable.

Payment structures in this trade run from advance payment through documentary collection to a letter of credit, and each shifts risk to a different party. Advance payment is cheapest for the seller and worst for the buyer, because the buyer finances the harvest and owns every risk until the container lands. A letter of credit inserts a bank between the parties and pays against documents rather than against goods, which protects both sides only as far as the documents describe the material accurately. The trap is familiar: a compliant set of papers can travel with a non-compliant load, because the bank examines the file, not the wood.

Freight has its own vocabulary and its own penalties. Demurrage accrues while a container sits at a terminal beyond the allowed period; detention accrues while it sits at the buyer's premises. Fumigation or plant-health treatment may be required at origin or at destination, and the certificate recording it must match the consignment's identification rather than the supplier's usual practice. A surveyor appointed at destination can document condition before the doors are opened wide, which is often the difference between a claim that stands and an argument about who left the container exposed.

Documents travel as a set, and the set has to agree with itself. Bill of lading, packing list, invoice, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate, and the permit for a listed species each carry names, quantities, and descriptions that must line up. A mismatch as small as a consignee spelling or a net weight that differs between invoice and packing list can stop a container at a border while the material sits in the heat.

| Document | What it controls | What breaks when it is wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Bill of lading | Title to the goods and their release | A consignee error blocks collection |
| Commercial invoice | Declared value and the duty basis | Value inconsistent with the permit |
| Packing list | Counts, weights, shipping marks | Net weight disagrees with the invoice |
| Certificate of origin | Preference and admissibility | Origin inconsistent with the species statement |
| Phytosanitary certificate | Plant-health admission | Treatment unrecorded, or recorded for another lot |
| CITES permit | Movement of a listed species | Quantity or species mismatched to the load |

## Insuring a Consignment, and Recovering When It Fails

**Direct answer:** Cargo insurance is priced against a declared value and a declared packing method, and the two exclusions that catch botanical buyers most often are inherent vice and delay — neither of which any premium removes.

Cover usually arrives in one of two shapes. All-risk wording pays for physical loss or damage unless an exclusion applies, which puts the argument on the exclusions. Named-perils wording lists what is covered and leaves everything else with the buyer, which is cheaper until the loss is the one nobody listed. Read which shape you have before the container sails, because the difference only becomes visible at the claim desk.

Inherent vice is the exclusion that matters here. A cargo that spoils because of its own nature — a lot carrying too much water that turns musty inside a sealed liner, wood that cracks as it equilibrates, aroma that fades because the volatiles had nowhere to go — is generally treated as a defect of the goods rather than a casualty of transit. The insurer's position is that the shipper loaded a problem. The buyer's counter is documentation: intake moisture, packing specification, and a survey at destination showing the container's condition. Without those three, the claim is a conversation about smells.

Delay is the second exclusion, and it explains why freight problems rarely produce recoverable money. A container held at a border while a permit is corrected suffers a commercial loss, not a physical one, and commercial loss sits outside cargo cover. That is a contractual gap to close with the supplier, not an insurance gap.

Filing is procedural and unforgiving. Notify the insurer or broker within the window stated in the policy, keep the packaging as received, commission a survey before the cargo is distributed, and preserve the weighbridge tickets, photographs, and correspondence. Subrogation follows: once the insurer pays, it inherits the buyer's right to pursue whoever caused the loss, which is why the file has to stand on its own without the buyer's cooperation later.

| Insurance idea | What it means | Why a botanical buyer should care |
|---|---|---|
| All-risk wording | Cover unless excluded | Shifts the argument onto the exclusion list |
| Named perils | Only listed events are covered | A gap appears exactly where the loss is unusual |
| Inherent vice | Loss caused by the goods' own condition | Moisture and aroma failures land here |
| General average | Shared sacrifice to save a shipment | A contribution can be demanded after a casualty |
| Subrogation | Insurer inherits the claim | The file must survive without the buyer present |
| Declared value | The figure the premium is based on | Undervaluing caps the recovery |

## Who Signs Off, and What the Signature Means

**Direct answer:** A purchase is only as controlled as its signatures, and the useful design separates the person who negotiates from the person who receives, so that no single desk can approve a lot, accept it, and pay for it.

Delegated authority is the starting point. Write down who may commit the company to a value, who may approve a deviation, and who may release payment, and keep the three lists short enough that people remember them. When the same person holds all three, a bad lot arrives, gets accepted, and gets paid for before anyone with a different incentive sees it.

Dual sign-off on deviations is the cheapest control available. A concession — accepting a lot below specification at a reduced price — should require a second signature, because a concession is where a quality problem is converted into a price story. Two signatures do not guarantee honesty; they guarantee that someone else looked.

Segregation of duties covers the quieter risk. The buyer negotiates, the warehouse receives, quality inspects, and accounts pays. Kickbacks live in the gaps between those roles: a buyer who also signs the receipt can hide a shortfall, and a receiver who also negotiates can be rewarded for accepting a substitution. None of this requires accusing anyone. It requires a process where the accusation would be unnecessary.

Related-party declarations belong in the file too. If the supplier shares an owner, a family, or an address with someone inside the buying organisation, say so at the quotation stage and record the disclosure. Buyers who treat that as an insult have usually never had to unwind one.

| Control | What it prevents | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Delegated authority | One desk committing the company | A written schedule, reviewed yearly |
| Dual sign-off on concessions | Quality converted into a discount | One extra signature per deviation |
| Receiver separate from buyer | Shortfalls and substitutions hidden | A role boundary, not a new headcount |
| Benchmarking against other quotes | Drift in the accepted price level | A folder of comparable offers |
| Related-party declaration | Undisclosed conflicts of interest | A form at onboarding |

## Lot Continuity: The Cost Nobody Prices In

**Direct answer:** The most expensive agarwood problem is not a bad first lot but a good first lot that cannot be matched, because a product line built on an unmatchable material is a product line with an expiry date.

Agarwood arrives in lots. A lot comes from particular trees, a particular region, a particular harvest window, and a particular set of processing choices. When that lot is exhausted, the next one will differ — in resin density, in aroma balance, in moisture behaviour, in grind response. The difference may be small enough that no single customer notices, or large enough that the product changes character. Either way, the buyer carries the consequence.

Three practices reduce the exposure. Keep a sealed reference portion from every accepted lot, labelled and stored away from production stock, so a later comparison is physical rather than remembered. Ask the supplier how much of the current lot remains and how the next lot will be sourced, which is a capacity question disguised as a courtesy. And agree a tolerance in advance — how much aromatic drift the product can absorb before the lot is rejected — so the decision is made before anyone is under schedule pressure.

There is a structural point underneath all of this. A product line that needs a trophy-grade material has a short life, because trophy material is by definition hard to repeat. A product line built on a documented, repeatable mid-grade can run for years. The buyer who optimizes for the first burn rather than the hundredth is buying a launch, not a product.

## Packaging, Storage, and the Journey

**Direct answer:** A verified specification survives only if the material arrives intact, which makes packaging, storage, and shipping part of the specification rather than logistics detail.

Agarwood is a botanical with volatile aromatic content and a moisture equilibrium it will always try to reach with its surroundings. Sealed, moisture-resistant packaging protects both properties in transit; an open sack protects neither. Bulk presentation and retail presentation are separate decisions and should be quoted separately.

Storage repeats the same logic indoors. A stable, moderate environment with sealed containment keeps the moisture target and the aroma where they were agreed; a hot, dry store pulls water out of the fibre, and a damp one puts it back. Practical guidance on keeping material in condition is in the [storage & longevity guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/).

Shipping adds a legal layer that has nothing to do with aroma. Incoterms decide who bears cost and risk at each point in the journey, and for material from CITES-listed species the permits must be in order before the consignment moves. A shipment held at a border while paperwork is repaired is a shipment losing aroma in a container. The mechanics are in the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/).

## What to Ask Instead of "How Much"

**Direct answer:** Figures here would not survive a real quotation, because band, form, volume, and destination each move the number; the useful move is to ask what moves it.

The band spread alone is wide enough that a single price for "agarwood" describes nothing, and the minimum shifts with how much preparation an order demands — a stock band in plain sacks is a different proposition from a custom blend in printed cartons.

Three questions do the work a price request cannot:

- **What drives this figure?** Band, form, volume, packing, and destination each shift it, and a seller should be able to say which one dominates your order.
- **What is the floor for this band, in this form, at this volume?** An answer tied to your seven lines, not a policy number.
- **Which papers come with the load?** Species and district statement, farm or forest declaration, and the permits where the species is listed.

That is the discipline set out in the [MOQ & pricing guide](/blog/moq-pricing-guide/), and it bites harder on agarwood than on most materials: a figure quoted without a band is not cheap or expensive, it describes a different product.

## How a Bad Lot Reaches the Line

**Direct answer:** Sourcing failures rarely announce themselves as fraud; they arrive as small compromises in sequence, and each one looks reasonable at the moment it is made.

| The compromise | What it looks like at the time | What it becomes downstream |
|---|---|---|
| Accepting a hand-picked sample | "The supplier is being generous" | Bulk arrives at a lower grade than the sample |
| Skipping the written specification | "We both know what we mean" | Every later dispute has no reference point |
| Deferring the moisture question | "That is a production detail" | Mould or brittleness appears in the warehouse |
| Buying on price before grade | "The market is competitive" | A product that cannot hold its aroma promise |
| Ignoring lot continuity | "We will find more when we need it" | A reformulation forced by supply, mid-season |

The pattern is consistent: each compromise saves a conversation now and costs a decision later, when the leverage has moved to the other side of the table. Reading the table backwards is the practical remedy — every row names a question that costs nothing to ask at the inquiry stage.

## The Decision Sequence, Ordered by Evidence

**Direct answer:** Specification, then verification, then terms — and swapping any two of those steps hands the risk to the buyer.

1. **Write the specification.** Seven lines: tree, district, farm or forest, resin band, form, grind, water content. Without them nothing later can be judged.
2. **Look at the plant, not the brochure.** Who they are, what they can actually run, who else buys from them, and which papers they produce without being pushed. The [factory vetting guide](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/) sets out the questions.
3. **Draw and screen.** Incremental draw, composite, split, seal. Warm a sliver, watch the smoke, weigh it in water. Save laboratory work for lots whose value makes it worth the invoice.
4. **Agree terms against the document.** Minimum, price, packaging, storage, freight, papers — all measured against the seven lines, all quoted per inquiry.
5. **Run a trial.** This is the only step that tests repetition, and it is the one skipped when a sample has impressed everyone.
6. **Check before it ships.** Compare the consignment with the sealed reference and the written lines, and write down what you found.

Step five is where schedules get broken. A sample is a promise about a piece; a trial is evidence about a process. Confusing them produces a container that is nearly, but not quite, what was approved — the most expensive kind of wrong.

## Contract Terms That Decide Whether a Dispute Is Winnable

**Direct answer:** Five clauses carry most of the weight in an agarwood purchase — tolerance, retention sample, acceptance window, rework, and force majeure — and each is cheap to negotiate before the order and close to impossible to add afterwards.

Tolerance says how far a lot may drift before it counts as a failure. Written as one figure it is useless, because a moisture deviation and a grade deviation are not the same injury. Write it per attribute and put the remedy beside each one: a moisture excess may be correctable by re-drying at the supplier's cost, while a band shortfall is a rejection. Decide the consequence while both parties still want the deal, because that is the only moment when the decision is voluntary.

A retention sample clause is custody, not sentiment. It should name who holds the sealed portions, where they are stored, how long they are kept, and what happens if one is opened without both sides present. Without that, a dispute becomes a comparison between the buyer's bag and the supplier's recollection.

The acceptance window sets the clock. Too short and the buyer cannot mill and test before the deadline passes; too long and the supplier cannot release the lot to anyone else. A window sized to the buyer's actual inspection routine — including the days a laboratory needs — is the honest one, and it is worth arguing for at the quotation stage rather than at the complaint stage.

Rework deserves its own line because it is the middle remedy everyone forgets. Re-drying, re-milling, or blending down can save a lot, so the clause should say who pays for the attempt, what happens when the attempt fails, and whether the buyer may reject after a failed rework without further argument.

Force majeure in a botanical trade is not a remote clause. Harvest failure, disease in the growing area, a storm that closes a road, an export suspension, a change in permit practice — these are ordinary events in this supply, and a clause copied from a machinery contract will not fit them. Ask for four things: a definition naming the events that matter for agriculture and export, a notice duty with a deadline, the evidence the supplier must produce, and a consequence that separates delay from cancellation. The buyer needs a fifth: the right to source elsewhere while the suspension lasts, without being treated as having broken an exclusivity the supplier never actually required.

| Clause | What it protects | The version that fails in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Tolerance | The boundary between a deviation and a reject | One figure for all attributes, no remedy attached |
| Retention sample | The shared reference a dispute is argued against | "Samples may be kept", with no custodian named |
| Acceptance window | Time to inspect before money is committed | A window shorter than the laboratory turnaround |
| Rework | A middle remedy short of rejection | Silent on who pays and what follows failure |
| Force majeure | Predictability when harvest or export fails | A clause written for a factory, not for a farm |
| Title and risk | Who owns the goods at each point | Risk passing before inspection is possible |

## Keeping an Archive That Answers Questions Later

**Direct answer:** A lot archive is not paperwork for its own sake — it is the only evidence available when a later batch behaves differently, and it costs one shelf, one label printer, and the discipline to use both.

Every accepted consignment should get an internal code the moment it is booked in. The code links four things that are otherwise kept in four places: the supplier's own lot reference, the date and weight received, the observations made at intake, and the physical location of the material in the warehouse. When a press operator reports that this month's sticks smell thinner than last month's, the code is what turns an argument into a comparison.

The physical side of the archive matters as much as the file. A sealed portion from each accepted lot, stored away from production stock in a cool, dark place, lets you put the current material beside the material you approved and smell both. Memory is not a reference standard; a sealed bag is, because it holds the aroma of the lot as it arrived rather than the aroma of everything stored near it since.

Two habits keep the archive useful. First, record the grind when powder is milled, since particle size is invisible once the material is in a bin. Second, keep the archive through staff changes, which means the labels have to be legible to someone who was not present when they were written. An archive that only its author can interpret expires with that author's departure.

## Keeping Records in a System, Not a Folder

**Direct answer:** Once a buyer handles more than a handful of lots a year, the file has to become a database — a lot master record with fixed fields, a status flag, and a query that can answer what went into a batch without hunting through correspondence.

Design the record before choosing the software. Six fields do most of the work: an internal lot code, the supplier's own reference, species and district, band and form, weight received and weight issued, and a status of quarantine, released, or rejected. Everything else — moisture readings, mill dates, certificate numbers, photographs — hangs off that record as an attachment or a dated note.

The status flag prevents the commonest warehouse accident: material that failed a check and was issued anyway because nobody marked it. A rejected lot sitting in the same rack as released stock is a rejection waiting to be reversed by a busy shift. Quarantine has to mean a physical location and a system state, changed together.

Label at receipt, not later. A printed label carrying the lot code and a scannable symbol costs almost nothing and removes transcription from the process; a handwritten code copied twice is a code that will eventually be copied wrong. Scan at issue as well as at receipt, so the system can say which lot went into which batch without consulting a production diary.

Field design matters more than feature lists. Dropdowns for species, district, band, form, and status keep the data countable; free-text fields produce eleven spellings of one district and a report nobody trusts. Keep a single free-text field for observations, because the useful details are the ones that refused to fit a category.

Then use the data. A monthly count of intake rejections by supplier, and of moisture readings against target, turns the archive from a filing obligation into a management tool. Build the traceability query first: given a finished batch, list the lots inside it; given a lot, list the batches that consumed it. That query is what a customer question or a withdrawal exercise actually needs.

| Field | Why it earns its place | What breaks without it |
|---|---|---|
| Internal lot code | The key every other record joins to | Records exist but cannot be linked |
| Supplier reference | Ties the file to the seller's own paperwork | A claim cannot cite the seller's lot |
| Band and form | Explains behaviour in the mill | Quality arguments have no baseline |
| Weight received and issued | Shows yield and exposes leakage | Losses stay invisible |
| Status flag | Stops rejected stock being used | A failed lot returns to production |
| Attachments | Holds certificates, photographs, readings | Evidence lives in someone's inbox |

## Inspection on Arrival: Four Records Worth Making

**Direct answer:** Four checks at intake — weight against the declared figure, a moisture reading, the smell of the headspace when the bag is first opened, and the condition of the sack's lower layers — catch most of the failures that would otherwise appear mid-production.

Weigh the consignment and compare it with the declared figure. A shortfall is not always theft; it can be water that left the wood in transit or packaging counted into the total. The point is to notice, and to record, before the material disappears into stock.

Take a moisture reading on a small portion — weigh it, dry it, weigh it again — rather than accepting the certificate's figure. Paperwork describes the sample the supplier tested; the reading describes the sack in front of you, and the two are not always the same.

Smell the headspace when the bag is opened for the first time. Volatiles concentrate in a sealed container, so the first breath is the most informative one available. A sour, musty, or solvent note at that moment is a flag that no later inspection will explain away.

Finally, look at the bottom of the sack. Fine dust settles, foreign matter collects, and moisture pools downward, which means the lower layers carry the problems. A buyer who only inspects the top of a consignment has inspected the part the supplier wanted seen.

## A Risk Register for Agarwood Buying

**Direct answer:** Five risks account for most of the money lost in this material, and each has a control that is cheaper than the loss it prevents.

| What goes wrong | First symptom you will notice | Cheapest thing that prevents it |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk weaker than the sample | Scent thinner, smoke greyer, pieces heavier | Three sealed portions and a draw taken from the bulk itself |
| Oil forced into poor wood | Heavier than the grade suggests, greasy film, harsh heat note | Slice a face and warm it before trusting the basin |
| Water above target | Musty breath at the bag mouth, powder caking | Own moisture reading at intake, then dry sealed storage |
| Supply ends mid-season | A rushed offer of something "almost the same" | Ask how much of the lot remains before fixing a launch date |
| Paper describing other wood | Declared species or district does not fit the material | Match permit and statement to the lot code, not to the seller's name |

The register is deliberately short. A long list of theoretical risks gets ignored, while five rows that each name a visible symptom get used. The pattern across the rows is the same: the failure is cheapest to catch before the material enters stock, and most expensive to catch after a product has been pressed from it.

## What to Ask Before Trusting a Supplier

**Direct answer:** A supplier interview is not a courtesy call — six questions separate a trading desk from a producer, and the answers matter more than the samples.

Ask who actually grinds and blends the material, and whether that happens on their site or somewhere upstream. The answer tells you how many hands the specification passes through. Ask what monthly volume of this grade they can hold steady, which is a different question from what they can ship once. Ask what proportion of lots they reject at their own intake, because a supplier with no rejection figure either does not check or does not want to say.

Ask how the material is stored between production and dispatch, and whether the store is sealed against humidity. Ask whether they can show the last few lots of the same grade side by side, which tests both the archive and the honesty of the grade claim. Ask, finally, what happens when a lot runs out — whether the next one is sourced from the same plantation or bought on the open market. The full document and capability checklist is in the [factory vetting guide](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/).

## Supplier Scorecards That Stay Honest

**Direct answer:** A scorecard is useful only if the person who receives the goods scores it, on evidence that already exists in a file, against weights agreed before the first review — otherwise it becomes a ritual that rewards suppliers who are good at paperwork.

Choose five dimensions and stop. Conformance at intake, meaning how often a lot matches the specification without a conversation. Delivery reliability, meaning whether the promised date is the date the goods move. Documentation completeness, meaning whether papers arrive with the load rather than three reminders later. Responsiveness to a claim, meaning how quickly a deviation is acknowledged and what is offered. Continuity, meaning whether the supplier can say where the next lot comes from.

Weight them before scoring starts, and put the weights where the supplier can see them. Publishing them is not fairness theatre: a supplier who knows continuity carries weight will invest in lot planning, while one who knows only price carries weight will invest in discounts.

Score from the file, never from the meeting. Each dimension needs a named evidence source — the intake record, the shipping document, the deviation log, the correspondence, the lot archive. A score without an evidence field is an opinion, and opinions drift toward whoever was most agreeable on the last call.

Review on a cadence and keep the history. One quarter describes a season; four quarters describe a supplier. The pattern to watch is the variance rather than the average, because a supplier who alternates between excellent and poor lots is harder to plan around than one who is consistently adequate. The adequate one lets you schedule.

| Dimension | Evidence source | How the number gets flattered |
|---|---|---|
| Conformance at intake | Intake records checked against the specification | Only the accepted lots get scored |
| Delivery reliability | Shipping documents and arrival dates | Dates renegotiated after the fact |
| Documentation | The file exactly as received | Certificates forwarded late but filed as on time |
| Claim response | Deviation log with dates | Claims settled informally and never logged |
| Continuity | A named next-lot source | A promise rather than a plan |

## When the Lot and the Specification Disagree

**Direct answer:** A disagreement is a procedure, not a quarrel: stop, document, re-test from the sealed reference, quantify the gap, then choose between acceptance with adjustment, rework, or rejection.

The first move is to stop the material where it is. Once powder has been blended into a batch, the evidence is gone and the conversation becomes about blame rather than about facts. The second move is documentation — photographs of the packaging and the material, the weight, the intake readings, and the date the discrepancy was noticed.

The third move is a re-test drawn from the sealed portion rather than from the disputed sack, because the sealed portion is the thing both parties agreed on. Compare the result against the written specification and state the gap in the specification's own terms: this much moisture above the target, this grade band below the approved reference.

Then choose. Acceptance with a price adjustment keeps a schedule and records the deviation. Rework — re-drying, re-grinding, blending down — keeps the material and costs time. Rejection costs the relationship and protects the product. All three are legitimate; the failure mode is choosing none of them and letting the material drift into production while the argument continues.


## Price per Kilo Is the Wrong Number

**Direct answer:** Material is bought by weight and sold as finished goods, and the conversion between the two is where most of the real cost hides — which makes cost per saleable unit a more honest comparison than price per kilogram.

Three losses sit between the invoice and the shelf. The first is physical: wood becomes chips, chips become powder, and powder becomes a pressed stick, with material left on the mill, in the sieve, and on the floor at every step. The second is aromatic: some volatile content leaves during drying and during the heat of pressing, and a material with a thin resin load has less margin to lose before the finished product stops performing. The third is rejection: a stick that cracks, a cone that will not hold its shape, a batch that fails the burn check.

Those losses are not equal across grades. A resin-dense lot tends to survive processing with its character intact, while a lean lot can arrive smelling acceptable and finish smelling like binder. The consequence for a buyer is that the cheaper material is not automatically the cheaper product: a low grade that must be dosed higher, or that loses more in the press, can cost more per finished unit than a mid-grade that behaves predictably. Work the comparison on finished yield, not on the kilo price, and the ranking sometimes reverses.

There is a practical way to test this without a laboratory. Run the same small formula twice, once with the candidate material and once with the incumbent, and compare how much scent the finished pieces actually deliver when burned. The difference in dosing is the real price difference, expressed in the units your customer buys.

## Cost Modelling on Paper Before the First Order

**Direct answer:** Model the purchase as a chain of multiplications rather than a single price, because every stage between the invoice and the shelf either preserves the material or consumes it, and the consuming stages are where two offers are actually compared.

Write the model in words before writing it in figures. Landed cost per saleable unit equals what was paid for the material plus freight, duty, and inspection, divided by the saleable output that material produces. That denominator is a product of factors: input weight, yield through milling, yield through pressing, the share of units passing the burn check, and the dosing multiple the formula needs to reach its target scent. Each factor sits between zero and one, and each one multiplies the others, which is why three modest losses compound into a large one.

The model earns its keep when it is run twice. Run it with the incumbent material and with the candidate, holding the formula and the selling price constant. If the candidate needs more material per unit to reach the same scent, that dosing multiple enters the denominator and the cheaper kilo can lose. If the candidate shatters in the press because its fibre is brittle, that yield factor enters as well. The comparison that matters is not which invoice is smaller but which model produces the lower cost per saleable unit at equal sensory performance.

Then run it on the pessimistic side. Substitute the lowest plausible yield and the highest plausible rejection rate, and see whether the project still clears its margin. A model that works only at the optimistic end is not a plan; it is a hope with a spreadsheet attached. Buyers who do this before the first order find out which variable they must control — often moisture, often particle size — and that discovery is worth more than the precision of the estimate.

| Line in the model | What drives it | How it moves when the grade rises |
|---|---|---|
| Material cost | Band, form, origin, volume | Up, sometimes faster than the sensory gain |
| Freight and duty | Weight, route, Incoterm | Roughly flat per kilo, so a lighter loss hurts |
| Inspection and laboratory | Value at risk, destination rules | Up, and worth it on high-value lots |
| Milling yield | Moisture, fibre, equipment | Better on dense material, worse on brittle stock |
| Press yield | Particle distribution, binder, operator | Sensitive to grind more than to grade |
| Rejection | Burn consistency, cracking, shape | Falls when the lot is uniform |
| Dosing multiple | Resin load, target scent | The hidden term that reverses the ranking |

## Designing the Trial Order

**Direct answer:** A trial order is a test with acceptance criteria written before it starts; run without criteria, it becomes an expensive sample and teaches nothing about reproducibility.

Four things belong in the trial, and the first is a quantity large enough to expose variation. A trial of a few pieces samples one corner of the production run; a trial sized to the smallest commercial unit samples the process. The second is a specification sheet attached to the order, so the material is measured against the same document that governed the sample. The third is a written acceptance test — aroma on gentle heat against the sealed reference, burn behaviour, moisture at intake, and particle size if powder. The fourth is a decision rule: what happens if the trial passes, and what happens if it is close but not equal.

That last point is where trials usually fail. A buyer receives material that is almost right, feels the schedule pressure, and accepts it while promising to tighten the next order. The next order arrives from a different lot, the drift compounds, and the product quietly changes character. Deciding in advance how much deviation counts as acceptable — and writing it down — converts that moment from a judgment call under pressure into a procedure.

Keep the trial documentation with the lot archive. When the material is eventually reordered a year later, the trial record is the only description of what "normal" looked like at the start.


## Keeping Notes Like a Buyer, Not a Tourist

**Direct answer:** The difference between a buyer who learns from each order and one who repeats the same mistake is a notebook with fixed fields, filled in at the same points every time.

The fields are not elaborate, and they are always the same six. At inquiry: what you asked for, in the terms you asked it, and what came back. At sample: where the material was drawn from, what it looked like, what it did on gentle heat, and how it compared with the last approved reference. At trial: the quantity run, the settings used, and whether the finished pieces matched the sample in the nose. At delivery: weight, condition on opening, and the reading you took yourself. At three months: whether the material still smells the way it did when it landed.

Filling those fields takes a few minutes per lot, and it changes the next conversation. A buyer who can say "the last three lots at this band arrived at this moisture and behaved this way in the press" is asking a different kind of question than one who says "send me your best price." The first buyer gets answers about process; the second gets answers about price, which is the only lever a seller needs when the buyer has no history.

The notebook also settles disputes without heat. When a lot arrives and something feels wrong, the comparison is between two written records and two sealed bags, not between two memories. Records outrank recollection every time, and the habit costs almost nothing to build.

## What to Concede, and What Never to Concede

**Direct answer:** Negotiation in this trade works when the buyer decides in advance which variables are trading chips and which are structural, because conceding a structural variable saves money once and costs quality for the life of the product.

The chips are real and worth spending. Delivery timing can move. Order size can be split across two shipments. Packaging can go plainer. Payment structure can shift. A buyer who trades in these currencies gets movement without touching the product.

The structural variables are a short list: which species, which grade band, whether the wood is plantation or forest, and what paper accompanies it. Each one changes what the material is rather than how it is delivered. A discount obtained by dropping from one band to the next is not a discount; it is a different product arriving under the same purchase order, and the invoice will look like a saving while the finished goods quietly change character.

The boundary is worth stating plainly, because it does not mean refusing every compromise. A buyer facing a tight launch may legitimately accept a lower band for a specific SKU, provided the decision is recorded, the packaging copy is adjusted to match, and the higher band is retained for the line that carries the brand. What is not legitimate is letting the substitution happen silently inside a shipment and discovering it at the press.


## Chips or Powder: The Form Decision

**Direct answer:** The choice between chips and powder is not a preference; it decides what you can inspect, what you can blend, and how long the material keeps its character in your store.

| Consideration | Chips | Powder |
|---|---|---|
| What you can still see | Grain, colour, resin veins | Nothing — the grinder has settled it |
| Fit for pressing sticks | Needs milling first | Ready for the mixer |
| Aroma retention in storage | Slow to change, forgiving | Loses volatiles quickly once opened |
| Inspection value | High; a buyer can judge the piece | Low; you are trusting a mill record |
| Fraud exposure | Harder to hide filler | Easy to extend with dust and sweepings |
| Best use | Warming, resale, premium lines | Blends, cones, high-volume sticks |

Read the two columns as a trade rather than a ranking. Chips preserve evidence: a buyer can hold a piece up, look at the resin lines, cut a sliver and warm it. Powder removes that evidence in exchange for convenience, which is why the documentation requirement for powder should be stricter, not looser. A mill that will not say what went into the grinder, or when, has removed the buyer's only remaining check.

The storage difference matters more than buyers expect. Whole pieces hold their aromatic content behind a relatively small exposed surface; a fine powder exposes almost all of it at once. A sack of powder opened and left in a warm room will not smell the same in a month, and the loss is not recoverable by adding more material to the formula.

## What the Mill Does to the Material

**Direct answer:** Grinding is a process with its own heat, its own losses, and its own ability to change what the lot smells like, so the mill record belongs in the purchase file alongside the material specification.

Three things happen inside a grinder. The first is size reduction, which is the visible one. The second is heat: friction warms the powder, and aromatic volatiles are exactly the fraction that leaves first when material gets warm. The third is blending by default — a mill that runs several lots through the same equipment without cleaning between them produces a powder that is a little bit of everything.

That third point is where powder purchases go wrong most often. A buyer orders a grade and receives a powder that carries traces of whatever was milled before it, which may be harmless or may be a cheaper aromatic masking the one that was paid for. The control is mundane: ask what the mill ran previously, ask for the cleaning routine in writing, and ask for a milling date on the certificate. None of those questions is technical, and all three are answered differently by a careful supplier and a careless one.

Grinding also changes the arithmetic of the purchase. Some material leaves as dust that is too fine to use, some is retained in the equipment, and some is lost to the sieve. The yield from a kilo of chips is therefore less than a kilo of powder, which is another reason to price the material as finished output rather than as weight received.

## When the Volume Is Too Small for a Custom Run

**Direct answer:** A small buyer should not commission a custom specification; the sensible move is to attach to an existing grade that a supplier already runs, and spend the effort on verification instead of on customization.

Custom work front-loads cost. A new grade means new trials, new milling settings, a separate approval, and a run that exists only for one customer. The minimum that makes that viable is far above what a small buyer can absorb, and pushing for it produces either a refusal or a price that carries the whole setup.

The alternative is to pick from what already exists. A supplier running a steady mid-band grade has a lot history, a repeatable outcome, and a price that reflects volume rather than novelty. The small buyer's leverage is not in the specification but in the relationship: ordering the stock grade consistently, paying on time, and being easy to deal with is what moves a buyer up the priority list when material is short.

There is a legitimate middle path. A buyer can take a stock grade and differentiate downstream — through the blend, the packaging, the story, and the finish — rather than upstream through the material. That keeps the supply simple and puts the distinctiveness where the buyer actually controls it.


## Who You Are Actually Talking To

**Direct answer:** The same ton of wood can pass through four or five hands before it reaches a press, and knowing which hand you are shaking changes what the seller can promise and what they cannot.

The chain usually starts with a grower who owns trees and harvests on a cycle. Above the grower sits a collector or cooperative that buys from several farms and sorts the output into bands. Above that, a processor cleans, cuts, and mills. Above the processor, an exporter who handles permits and freight. Brokers float across all of these, matching loads to buyers without ever taking possession.

| Who you are dealing with | What they control | What they usually cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Grower | Trees, harvest timing, inoculation method | Sorting consistency across a large order |
| Collector or cooperative | Volume from multiple farms, first sort | The processing history of a specific bag |
| Processor | Cleaning, cutting, milling, blending | Where the raw material came from |
| Exporter | Papers, freight, consolidation | Anything about how the wood was grown |
| Broker | Matching a load to a buyer | Ownership of the goods, or recourse if they fail |

The practical consequence is about recourse. A grower who sells you a bad load has a reputation in one district to protect. A broker who sells you the same load has a phone number. Neither is automatically better, and a good broker can find material a grower never could — but the contract should reflect which one you are actually buying from.

There is a second consequence, about claims. A processor can tell you the moisture of a milled batch because they measured it; they cannot tell you the species with certainty if they bought the raw wood from a collector who bought from three farms. When a seller answers a species question confidently and a processing question vaguely, that pattern tells you where they sit in the chain.

## What an Audit Visit Should Look At

**Direct answer:** An audit is a walk that follows one lot backwards from the bag you were shown to the tree it came from, and the stops that matter are the ones where material can be mixed, mislabelled, or left to absorb water.

Start at the store, not the office. Look at how lots are separated: bags stacked against a shared wall, a quarantine area for rejected material, a label on every unit that ties it to a lot code. A store where lots blend is a store where a specification cannot be honoured, whatever the file says.

Move to drying and milling. Ask what ran through the equipment before this lot and what the cleaning routine is, then look at the equipment rather than at the answer. Residue in a mill is visible. So is a brush that has not moved.

Walk the trees if the supplier grows. Look at how the trunks were wounded, how many carry marks, and whether harvest records match the volumes being offered. A supplier who buys raw wood from several farms will not have this walk available, which is itself information about where they sit in the chain.

Then follow paper backwards. Pick one bag, read its lot code, and ask for the intake record, the drying record, the milling record, and the document that travelled with the material. A supplier with a working traceability system produces those four items in a few minutes. A supplier without one produces a narrative.

| Stop on the walk | What to look at | What a weak answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Store | Segregation, labels, quarantine area | "It is all the same grade anyway" |
| Drying floor | Airflow, turning schedule, weather cover | "It dries when it dries" |
| Mill | Residue, cleaning log, previous lot run | "We always clean it", with no log |
| Growing area | Wound marks, spread of tree ages, harvest records | "The buyer does not need to see the trees" |
| Records | One bag traced back to four documents | A story instead of the documents |

## Agents, Brokers, and Whose Side the Commission Is On

**Direct answer:** An intermediary earns a commission for access, and the buyer's first question is not the size of that commission but who pays it and what it obliges the intermediary to disclose.

Three arrangements look identical on a quotation. A buyer's agent works for the buyer, is paid by the buyer, and is expected to disclose what they earn from any seller. A seller's agent represents the goods and is paid out of the seller's proceeds. A double agent takes from both sides and discloses neither, which is not a rare pathology but a structural temptation in a market where information is the product.

Ask for the arrangement in writing: who pays the commission, whether any volume rebate exists, and whether the intermediary or an affiliate holds stock in the lot being offered. A person holding stock has a different incentive from a person matching a load, and both differ from a person earning a monthly retainer.

The practical test is arithmetic. If the commission cannot be seen in the landed cost, it is either small or folded into the price. Ask what the same material would cost bought direct, and compare. Not because direct is always better — an agent who can produce a lot nobody else can find has earned the fee — but because a buyer who does not know the number cannot judge whether the access was worth buying.

## What a Failed Lot Costs After It Leaves the Warehouse

**Direct answer:** The invoice price is the smallest part of a bad purchase; the expensive part is what happens to the product, the schedule, and the customer after the material has been worked.

Follow the money through a failure. The load arrives short of specification → the formula has to be dosed higher to reach the same scent, or the scent is accepted as weaker → the pressed batch either costs more per unit or sells worse → if the weakness is only noticed by customers, returns and replacements follow → the line is reformulated under time pressure → the new formula is validated on a different lot, which may drift again.

None of those steps appears on a purchase order, and all of them are paid for. That is why the discipline of a sealed reference and an intake check pays for itself even when it feels like bureaucracy: it moves the discovery from the customer's nose to the warehouse floor, where it is still cheap.

The schedule cost deserves its own line. A rejected container in a season when material is scarce cannot be replaced quickly, so the choice becomes accepting a deviation or stopping production. Buyers who plan a buffer — an extra lot held in reserve, or a second approved supplier for the same band — are buying the option to say no. That option is worth more than the discount they might have negotiated instead.

## Heat, Humidity, and When to Buy

**Direct answer:** The material is a botanical with a water content that moves with the weather, and the calendar around a purchase matters as much as the price inside it.

Two seasonal effects act on every order. The first is at the source: harvest and drying depend on weather, so material offered in a wet season carries more water and dries less evenly than the same grade offered in a dry one. The second is in transit: a container crossing a hot corridor can arrive with condensation inside the liner, which is why sealed packaging and desiccant belong in the specification rather than in the supplier's discretion.

The buying calendar that follows is simple. Ask when the current lot was harvested and dried, and how it has been stored since. Ask whether the next harvest is expected to produce the same band, which is a different question from whether the supplier intends to sell you more. Place orders so that the material lands with enough lead time to be inspected, rested, and milled before it is needed — a load that arrives the week production starts has effectively skipped the inspection step.

For a buyer in a humid climate, the warehouse is part of the purchasing decision. A sealed, climate-stable store protects both the water content and the aromatic fraction; a shed that swings with the weather will undo a careful specification in a season. The [storage & longevity guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) covers the practical measures.


## What the Conversation Reveals

**Direct answer:** A supplier's answers carry as much information in their shape as in their content, and four habits — vagueness about the tree, fluency about the price, urgency about the offer, and silence about the last customer — deserve more attention than any photograph of a sample.

Vagueness has a location. Almost every seller can describe the wood; the informative ones describe the tree, the district, and the year. When a question about species produces a sentence about aroma, the reply is a deflection, and a second question phrased differently usually confirms it: ask what else grows in the same district, and listen for whether the answer is concrete or atmospheric.

Fluency about price signals the opposite problem. A trader who can quote a band, a form, and a minimum without pausing is reading a list, which is fine for stock material and useless for a specification. Ask what would change the figure. A producer names a variable — moisture, grind, packing — while a reseller names a discount.

Urgency is the oldest lever in the trade and still the most effective: a lot that must be decided today, a price that expires tonight, a buyer who is also looking. Each shortens the interval in which a sealed sample might be requested. The remedy is procedural rather than psychological. Keep a standing rule that no consignment is committed during the first conversation, and the lever stops working.

Silence about the last customer is the most diagnostic of the four. A supplier who cannot describe a previous buyer's specification, even in general terms and without naming anyone, has probably never supplied one. A supplier who describes the specification, the friction, and the fix is describing their own process.

Watch what gets volunteered. Information offered before it is requested — a harvest date, a permit number, a moisture reading — tends to be information the seller trusts. Details that surface only under direct questioning are not automatically false, but the seller has learned not to lead with them, and that habit is worth understanding before money moves.

| What you hear | What it may indicate | The question that tests it |
|---|---|---|
| Aroma described, species evaded | Species unknown, or a blend | "Which species, and what else grows nearby?" |
| Price quoted instantly, variables absent | A list being read, not a lot described | "What would change this figure?" |
| Offer expires tonight | Time pressure replacing evidence | "Send a sealed sample first" |
| No previous specification described | New to trade supply, or avoiding detail | "What did your last buyer specify?" |
| Permit number volunteered | Confidence in the paperwork | Verify it against the lot code |

## A Note on Currency and Quotation Validity

**Direct answer:** A price is a statement with an expiry date and a currency attached, and a quotation that omits either has left the buyer holding a risk nobody priced.

Ask three questions of every figure. Which currency, and whether the invoice will be issued in it or converted at a rate chosen on a date the buyer does not control. For how long the figure holds, since a quotation valid for a season is a different commitment from one valid for a week. And what happens if the underlying material cost moves before shipment — whether the seller absorbs the movement, passes it through with evidence, or cancels.

Pass-through clauses deserve particular care. A clause that lets the seller raise the price when the market rises, without letting the buyer walk away, is a one-way option. Either the mechanism works in both directions or the buyer has bought a floor under someone else's margin.

Currency risk is smaller and easier to manage. Invoicing in the seller's currency puts the exposure on the buyer; invoicing in the buyer's currency puts it on the seller, who will price it in. Neither is wrong. What fails is leaving the question open until the invoice arrives, when the exchange rate has already made the decision.

## When the Market Is Rising: Cover, Substitution, and Single-Source Risk

**Direct answer:** A rising market changes which risk dominates — from getting a good price to getting any material at the grade specified — and the buyer's tools are forward cover, a qualified second source, and a substitution rule written before the pressure arrives.

The mechanism explains why loyal buyers still get squeezed. Demand outruns harvest → holders of stock wait, expecting a better price next month → spot availability thins at the top bands first → buyers who must produce bid up the remaining lots → the spread between bands widens as buyers trade down → a product line specified on one band quietly receives another. No dishonesty is required at any step. The drift happens because the specification never carried a substitution rule.

Forward cover is the obvious response and the easiest to misuse. A forward commitment fixes a quantity and a grade for a future delivery, which protects the schedule but transfers quality risk onto a lot that does not exist yet. The clause that makes it usable is the reference: the forward lot gets measured against the sealed sample and the specification, with a rejection right if it fails. Cover without that reference is a bet on a stranger's future harvest.

The second source is the slower tool and the more valuable one. Qualifying an alternative costs samples, trials, and time, which is exactly why buyers skip it until the market forces the issue. Do it while material is plentiful, approve a grade that overlaps the incumbent's, and keep the relationship warm with small orders. A qualified alternative is the only thing that lets a buyer refuse a bad lot during a shortage.

Substitution deserves a written rule rather than a habit. Decide which products may move down a band, how far, and what packaging and customer communication must say when they do. A rule written in a calm month prevents a silent substitution in a tight one.

## Handing the Material to the Formulator

**Direct answer:** The formulator inherits every assumption the buyer made, so the handover has to carry evidence rather than conclusion — reference sample, signed specification, mill record, and the question the trial is meant to answer.

A handover package has five parts. The sealed reference, because the formulator needs to smell what was approved rather than what arrived. The signed specification with its revision number, because dosing decisions get made against those numbers. The mill record, because particle distribution governs how the powder packs and how the stick burns. The intake readings, because moisture at handover is the condition the trial actually starts from. And a plain-language note on the intended product and the sensory target, written without formula codes that only one person understands.

The trial should answer a question, not demonstrate competence. Agree in advance what the formulator will vary — dose, binder ratio, particle size, pressing pressure — and what will be held constant, so a failed trial yields information rather than blame. Record the settings, retain samples of the finished pieces, and compare them against pieces made from the incumbent material under the same conditions.

The feedback loop is where handovers break. The formulator reports "works well" or "does not work", and the buyer has nothing to act on. Ask for the specific: how the material behaved in the mixer, whether the stick held its shape, how the burn progressed, whether the aroma arrived early or late. Those observations translate into a purchasing question — a moisture target, a particle specification, a band — which is the only reason to run the trial at all.

| Handed over | Why it matters | What fails without it |
|---|---|---|
| Sealed reference sample | Defines the approved nose | The formulator optimises toward the wrong aroma |
| Signed specification | Carries the numbers dosing depends on | Targets get set from memory |
| Mill record | Explains packing and burn behaviour | Grind problems get blamed on the formula |
| Intake moisture | Sets the starting condition | The trial begins with wet or brittle stock |
| Trial question | Makes failure informative | A failed batch teaches nothing |

## Traditional Language, Kept at a Distance

**Direct answer:** Chinese medical literature has described agarwood for centuries in its own idiom — moving qi, warming the middle — and those phrases belong to traditional belief, not to clinical evidence, and certainly not to a label claim.

Three kinds of record get mixed together in sales conversations, and separating them is useful. The first is cultural practice: people have burned this wood in temples, studies, and evening rooms for a very long time, valuing the scent and what it signalled about taste and composure. The second is textual: the material is listed among high aromatics in classical materia medica and incense manuals, and the sink-in-water sort is recorded trade practice rather than a modern invention. The third is chemical: laboratories study the resin's sesquiterpenes and chromones and use their patterns to identify material.

Each record is genuine within its own category. None of them is a finding about the human body. A purchase decision should therefore rest on identity, grade, and consistency — the three things a document and a sample can establish — rather than on a therapeutic description that nothing in the file supports.

## The Author's Position

**Position:** Here, the piece of paper is the purchase. Anyone who opens with a number has skipped the only question that makes the number mean something.

**Reasoning:**
- One word covers a spread wide enough that an unspecific figure cannot be compared with another unspecific figure, let alone with a real offer.
- Fraud follows the discount, so a buyer who haggles before screening is arguing about the quality of a counterfeit.
- A workshop needs stock it can order again, not a piece it can admire once.

**Boundary:** "Old and wild" is not a synonym for "correct." Saying so does not mean forest wood is worthless — it means that what a production buyer needs is repetition, and forest supply is the least repeatable supply there is. The distinction matters most when an exceptional lot appears at a tempting figure: the question is not whether it is beautiful but whether a second lot exists.

## A Glossary of Trade Terms

**Direct answer:** Most agarwood arguments begin as vocabulary problems, so a buyer should keep a short glossary and insist that a supplier use each term in the same sense the buyer does.

| Term | What it means in a purchase | Where it misleads |
|---|---|---|
| 沉香 (chénxiāng) | The resin-bearing wood as traded, across several species | Treated as one product; it is a condition, not a species |
| 白木香 (báimùxiāng) | The pale host wood of *Aquilaria sinensis* before resin forms | Used loosely for low grade, though it names a species state |
| 沉水 (chénshuǐ) | Sinks in water; the high resin band | Names a test result, not a measured resin figure |
| 半沉半浮 (bàn chén bàn fú) | Hangs between surface and bottom | The widest band and the least uniform |
| 奇楠 (qínán) | Soft, resin-rich material outside the sink ladder | Its rarity invites substitution by treated stock |
| *Aquilaria sinensis* | The Chinese species | Origin claims get attached to it without evidence |
| *Aquilaria malaccensis* | The main Southeast Asian traded species | Listed status changes the paperwork, not the grade |
| CITES listing | A permit requirement for movement of listed species | A permit names a species and quantity, never a quality |
| Phytosanitary certificate | Confirms the consignment meets a destination's plant-health rules | Says nothing about identity or resin |
| Certificate of origin | States where the goods shipped from | Not the same as where the tree grew |
| EXW | Buyer collects at the seller's premises and carries onward cost and risk | Encourages buyers to undercount freight and permits |
| FOB | Risk passes when the goods are on board the vessel | Often mistaken for a delivered price |
| CIF | Seller pays freight and insurance to a named destination; risk still passes at shipment | Read as "seller carries risk to the port", which it does not |
| DAP | Seller delivers to a named place; buyer handles import clearance and duty | Duty surprises land after the price is agreed |
| Retention sample | Sealed portion held by both sides as the dispute reference | Kept informally, so it proves nothing later |
| Tolerance | The permitted deviation before a lot fails | Written once for all attributes and therefore unenforceable |
| Lot code | The identifier linking material to its records | Absent from the certificate, which breaks the chain |

The Incoterms rows deserve a second look, because they divide cost, risk, and duty separately and buyers routinely collapse the three. CIF is the usual trap: the seller arranges carriage and insurance to the destination port, yet risk passes when the goods went on board, so a container damaged mid-voyage is the buyer's problem to pursue. The glossary is not decoration. It is the shortest available test of whether two parties are discussing the same transaction.

## FAQ

### Q: What does a disciplined agarwood purchase look like from the buyer's side?

It looks like paperwork before it looks like wood. The buyer writes down which tree species is acceptable, which region, whether plantation or forest material, what density band, what delivery form, what grind, and what water content — then sends that list to the supplier and asks for confirmation line by line. Only after the answers arrive does a sample request make sense. The commercial conversation comes last, when there is a document to price. Reversing that order is how buyers end up comparing two numbers that describe two different products.

### Q: Can plantation-grown agarwood carry a commercial product line?

It can, and in practice it usually does. Farmed trees are wounded or inoculated deliberately, which starts the resin response on a schedule rather than waiting for a storm to do it. The material is generally less saturated than old forest wood, and that gap is real rather than a marketing fiction. What plantation supply offers instead is predictability: a similar grade across harvests, a known origin, and a volume that can be reordered. For a product that has to smell the same next year, predictability is worth more than a peak.

### Q: Where does a bulk purchase actually start?

It starts with a written specification and a supplier who will answer it, not with a request for a price. Ask what grade the supplier can hold steady, in what form, at what monthly volume, and with what documents. Then ask what the minimum is for that specific grade in that form. The answers separate a producer from a trader, because a trader can promise anything and a producer has to name a lot.

### Q: What separates genuine wood from treated material in the hand?

Three observations, made in order. Warm a sliver on gentle heat and judge whether the scent develops and changes or stays flat and sharp. Watch what the smoke does — resinous wood tends to give pale smoke with oil beading at the edge, while treated material often gives darker smoke and an acrid note. Then test density, knowing that density is the one property a faker can manufacture by forcing oil into low-grade fibre. No single check settles the question; the combination narrows it, and only a laboratory closes it.

### Q: Which papers should travel with the consignment?

A statement naming species and region, a declaration of plantation or forest origin, and — where the species appears in a CITES appendix — the permit that authorises the movement. Powder shipments should carry particle size and moisture figures on an analysis sheet. Treat each document as a claim with an author behind it: check who issued it, check that it names the lot rather than a generic shipment, and check that the declared species matches what the material appears to be.

### Q: Why does the same weight of agarwood carry such different values?

Because weight measures fibre and the market pays for resin. A kilogram of pale host wood and a kilogram of deeply saturated heartwood differ in how much aromatic material they contain, how long they burn, and how much of the scent survives grinding. Add the reputation of an origin, the legal status of a species, and the rarity premium attached to 奇楠, and two lots of equal mass end up far apart in value. The grade band explains most of the gap; scarcity explains the rest.

## Summary

- Agarwood is a resin condition inside several *Aquilaria* species, so the name alone specifies nothing and the band carries the meaning.
- Seven lines carry the whole specification, and the trial run is the only test of repetition.
- Density is a proxy that oil injection can fake; heat and smoke remain the stronger screens.
- Bad lots usually arrive as small compromises in sequence, each reasonable on the day it is made.
- Lot continuity is the quiet killer: material that cannot be matched quietly ends a product line.
- Price and minimum follow the specification, and both are confirmed per inquiry.

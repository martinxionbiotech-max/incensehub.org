**Direct answer:** Incense materials sort into a handful of families — woods, resins, flowers, spices, binders, and a small residual group of animal and mineral notes. Name the family and you already know most of how a material will behave under heat: how fast it releases, how much it needs, and what it does to the burn.

**Key facts:** This page is a working reference rather than a complete catalogue. Each entry gives the material, its naming, and its general character; deeper treatment sits on the linked pages. The idea that holds the whole glossary together is simple: family predicts behaviour better than the individual name does.

## Key facts

- **Six families carry the palette.** Woods, resins, flowers, spices, binders, and a small animal and mineral residue.
- **Woods are the skeleton.** Sandalwood (*Santalum*) is the dependable one; agarwood (*Aquilaria*) is resin-formed and variable within a single log.
- **Resins open loud and fade fast**, so a few percent can define the first minute and a wood spine has to carry the rest.
- **Spices stay under a few percent.** Clove is eugenol-dominant and reads dental above a low threshold.
- **Makko is the reference binder** — powdered bark of *Machilus thunbergii* — and binder is the largest single component by weight in most sticks.
- **Regulation shapes supply.** Agarwood species sit under CITES controls, and several animal materials are restricted or banned.
- **Format changes proportion.** A coil or a cone needs a high binder share; loose powder needs none at all.

## Key Takeaways

- Family first, name second. Wood, resin, petal, spice and binder each have a characteristic burn signature.
- The same Chinese name can cover several botanicals, and the same botanical can carry several trade names.
- A botanical (Latin) name is the only label that resolves ambiguity — when a supplier will give one.
- Substitution works by role, not by aroma: a resin can stand in for another resin, not for a wood.
- Quantity is a design decision: spices are loud in small doses, woods quiet in large ones.
- Storage damage is usually invisible at purchase and obvious a season later.

## Six Families, One Logic

**Direct answer:** The families are not a marketing taxonomy. They group materials by how they hold and release aromatic compounds under heat.

| Family | Physical form | How it gives up scent | Typical share of a blend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Woods | Dense, fibrous, resin-rich heartwood | Slowly, as heat penetrates the fibre | The bulk — often the chief |
| Resins | Brittle tears, gums, balsams | Fast at first, then a long tail | A few percent to a fifth |
| Flowers | Dried petals, sometimes whole | Quickly and delicately, easily lost | Small, often the accent |
| Spices | Hard seeds, bark, buds | Sharply, in tiny amounts | Under a few percent |
| Binders | Fine wood or gum powder | Continuously, as fuel | Enough to hold the body |
| Animal / mineral | Grains, tinctures, crystals | Erratically; small doses only | Trace, where legal at all |

The table is a first approximation, and it earns its keep because it answers the question a beginner actually has: why does a blend with more agarwood smell quieter than one with a spoonful of clove? The answer is release rate, not aromatic strength.

## Woods: Structure and Slow Release

**Direct answer:** Woods are the skeleton of most blends because a dense fibre releases aroma slowly and steadily, which is what a long burn needs.

The two reference woods are sandalwood (*Santalum* species) and agarwood (*Aquilaria* species). Sandalwood is the dependable one: a sweet, creamy, slightly buttery heartwood that behaves predictably across batches and holds a blend together. Agarwood is the erratic one: the wood is only aromatic where the tree has responded to injury by producing resin, so quality varies within a single log, let alone between logs.

Both are regulated in trade — agarwood species sit under CITES controls, and sandalwood supply has its own restrictions — which shapes what reaches a workshop and at what price. That is one practical reason a modern blend may lean on sandalwood where an older recipe called for agar.

The mechanism matters for blending. Heat must travel into the fibre before aroma can leave it, so a wood-dominated stick warms up slowly → the first minute is quiet → the middle of the burn is where the wood speaks → the tail is the heaviest resin fraction, which is why the last third of a good stick is often the most satisfying. Chop the wood finely and the same material opens faster and finishes sooner; the material is unchanged, the particle size is not.

Other woods appear in supporting roles: cedar and cypress for a dry, clean background; camphor wood for a cooling edge; various local hardwoods in regional traditions. They rarely lead a blend outside their own traditions.

## Resins: The Tear That Melts

**Direct answer:** Resins are the fast, bright, sometimes sharp fraction — they melt before they burn, and that is why they announce themselves early.

Frankincense (*Boswellia*), myrrh (*Commiphora*), benzoin (*Styrax*), labdanum, copal, mastic, and the various balsams all behave alike in outline: a solid tear that softens, then volatilizes, then leaves a small residue. Heat reaches them quickly because they are not fibrous, which is why resin-heavy blends open loud.

The differences inside the family are worth knowing. Frankincense is bright and citrus-tinged when fresh, duller and dustier with age. Myrrh is darker, more bitter, with a medicinal edge. Benzoin is sweet, vanillic, and often used as a fixative that slows the evaporation of lighter notes. Labdanum is heavy and leathery; copal is clean and quick.

Two practical notes. Resins are usually the first thing to go sticky in a humid store, because they absorb moisture and clump; and they are the cheapest way to make a dull blend smell busier, which is why their proportion is a useful thing to ask about. A blend that leans on resins for its opening and has no wood underneath will smell strong and finish abruptly.

## Flowers and Petals: The Fragile Fraction

**Direct answer:** Flower materials carry the most fragile aromatics in the palette, which is why they are used sparingly and why they fade first.

Dried jasmine, rose, osmanthus, champaca, lotus, and similar petals contribute top and heart notes: bright, recognisable, and short-lived. Their aromatic compounds are light and volatile, so they leave the heated zone early and are easily lost in storage, in over-drying, or in a hot mill.

That fragility sets the handling rules. Petals go in late in the blend, at low temperature, and the finished stick is cured gently rather than baked. A floral note that smells generous in the mixing room can be nearly absent after a fast dry, which is the most common reason a blend loses its promised opening.

Whole dried blooms versus powdered petal is a real distinction: whole material releases unevenly and looks better; powder distributes evenly and disappears faster. Neither is superior, and a supplier's choice usually reflects the price point rather than a theory.

The [lotus page](/blog/lotus-incense/) is a fair case study in how difficult a single floral material can be, and why most "lotus" products rely on a reconstruction rather than the flower.

## Spices: Small Dose, Loud Voice

**Direct answer:** Spices are the loudest materials per gram in the palette, which is why they are measured in fractions and why overdosing is the classic beginner's error.

Clove, cassia, cinnamon bark, cardamom, star anise, nutmeg, and pepper all carry potent volatile oils. Clove in particular is dominated by eugenol, which reads as dental and medicinal above a very low threshold; a blend that smells of clove is usually a blend that used too much clove.

Two rules cover most of the territory. First, spices are accents: they mark a blend's character at the opening and then should recede. Second, they interact with resins: a sweet resin can round a harsh spice, and a sharp spice can cut through a heavy balsam. That interplay is where most of the craft in spiced blends actually lives.

Storage behaviour is another distinguishing trait. Whole spices keep their oils far longer than ground ones, so a workshop that grinds its own will get a different result from one that buys pre-ground powder — not because the material is different, but because the surface area and the time since grinding are.

## Binders: The Invisible Majority

**Direct answer:** Binders are the largest single component by weight in most sticks and the least discussed, because they are not supposed to contribute a scent.

Makko — powdered bark of *Machilus thunbergii*, used widely in Japanese-style work — is the reference binder: a wood flour that holds the mix and then burns along with it. Other traditions use gum binders (tragacanth, acacia), which hold well but behave differently at the ember, or rely on the mucilage naturally present in some powders.

The choice of binder is a design decision with audible consequences. More binder → a forgiving dough and a slower, cooler burn → a duller top end. Less binder → a brighter scent and a more brittle rod. A hand-forming workshop usually runs a higher binder share than a machine line, for the simple reason that hands cannot work a lean, crumbly mix at speed.

The dedicated treatment sits in [makko and natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/). The short version for a reader of ingredient lists: if a product names no binder at all, the list is incomplete rather than pure.

## Animal and Mineral Notes

**Direct answer:** A small residual group of materials sits outside the plant families, and most of it is restricted, expensive, or both.

Traditional perfumery used animal-derived materials — musk from musk deer, civet, castoreum, ambergris from sperm whales — as fixatives and animalic accents. Their legal status varies by jurisdiction and several are controlled or banned in trade; modern incense formulations overwhelmingly use synthetic or botanical substitutes, and a page like this should describe the history without recommending the material.

Mineral and crystalline notes behave differently again. Camphor and borneol are plant-derived crystals with a cooling, penetrating character; they appear in small amounts in Chinese and Japanese formulas, and they volatilize quickly. Salt and mineral inclusions appear in some regional traditions as much for symbolism as for scent.

The practical point for a buyer: these materials are where label language is least reliable and where regulation is most relevant. A product claiming musk should be read with the same caution as a product claiming anything else that is restricted.

## Names: Chinese, Pinyin, Botanical

**Direct answer:** A glossary is mostly a naming problem, because the same material travels under several names and the same name sometimes covers several materials.

| Chinese | Pinyin | Botanical / trade | Family | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 檀香 | tán xiāng | *Santalum album* | Wood | The default sandalwood of the trade |
| 沉香 | chén xiāng | *Aquilaria* spp. | Wood | Resin-formed heartwood; CITES-controlled |
| 乳香 | rǔ xiāng | *Boswellia* spp. | Resin | Frankincense |
| 没药 | mò yào | *Commiphora* spp. | Resin | Myrrh |
| 安息香 | ān xī xiāng | *Styrax* spp. | Resin | Benzoin; a common fixative |
| 龙涎香 | lóng xián xiāng | Ambergris | Animal | Restricted; historical use |
| 丁香 | dīng xiāng | *Syzygium aromaticum* | Spice | Clove; eugenol-dominant |
| 桂皮 | guì pí | *Cinnamomum* spp. | Spice | Cassia / cinnamon bark |
| 甘松 | gān sōng | *Nardostachys jatamansi* | Root | Spikenard; earthy, persistent |
| 排草 | pái cǎo | *Lysimachia* spp. | Binder/root | Used in some Chinese formulas |
| 楠木 | nán mù | *Phoebe* / *Machilus* spp. | Binder | The makko family in Chinese practice |

Two cautions. Pinyin spellings vary (xiang / hsiang, chen / chenxiang), and older English sources transliterate differently, so a search on one spelling may miss the material entirely. And a Chinese name may be a category rather than a species: 檀香 can mean sandalwood generally, not *Santalum album* specifically.

The classical manual tradition is where most of these names were fixed; the [Xiangpu manuals page](/blog/xiangpu-incense-manuals/) covers how the lists were compiled and what they assumed.

## Reading a Label You Have Never Seen

**Direct answer:** Read the list in four passes — family, proportion, role, and claim — and most labels stop being opaque.

1. **Sort each named material into a family.** Wood, resin, petal, spice, binder. If a name does not sort, it is probably a trade name or a blend component rather than a raw material.
2. **Estimate proportion from order.** Lists are usually ordered by weight, so the first two or three names carry the blend. A list where the only recognisable material sits last is telling you something.
3. **Look for the binder.** If no binder appears, the list is incomplete. This is common and not necessarily dishonest, but it limits what the list can tell you.
4. **Separate claim from material.** "Premium", "traditional", "natural" are claims; "sandalwood", "benzoin", "clove" are materials. Only the second group can be checked.

A worked example: a label reading "sandalwood, benzoin, jasmine, clove" suggests a wood-led blend with a resin fixative, a floral accent and a spice edge — a conventional and coherent construction. The same words in reverse order would describe an odd product: mostly clove, a little jasmine, a trace of benzoin and a whisper of sandalwood. Order is information.

## The Pairs That Keep Getting Swapped

**Direct answer:** A handful of confusions recur so often that they deserve their own table, because each one changes what you should expect from a blend.

| Pair | What separates them | Why the confusion persists |
|---|---|---|
| Frankincense vs benzoin | Citrus-resinous and bright vs sweet, vanillic, fixative | Both are pale tears from tree wounds |
| Myrrh vs labdanum | Bitter, medicinal vs leathery, sweet-heavy | Both dark, dense, and slow |
| Sandalwood vs cedar | Creamy and buttery vs dry and pencil-sharp | Both are "woody" in a one-word description |
| Cassia vs cinnamon | Coarser, hotter, more pungent vs sweeter, finer | Trade usage overlaps; both are *Cinnamomum* |
| Agarwood vs sandalwood | Resin-formed, variable, expensive vs uniform, dependable | Both are "the good wood" in marketing |
| Makko vs ordinary wood flour | Binds structurally vs merely bulks | Both are wood powders |

The pattern behind the table: confusion flourishes where a single English word covers several materials, and where the price difference is large enough to make substitution tempting. A buyer who asks for the botanical name, and accepts "we don't disclose" as an answer, will avoid most of these traps.

The full cross-reference treatment, including substitution logic, sits in the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

## A Substitute Matrix by Role

**Direct answer:** Substitution works by role — chief, accent, fixative, fuel — because two materials in the same role can trade places, while two in different roles cannot.

| Role in the blend | Primary material | Workable stand-in | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood chief | Sandalwood | Cedar plus a resin fixative | Less cream, more dry edge; shorter tail |
| Resin chief | Frankincense | Copal, or benzoin in small part | Brighter and shorter vs sweeter and slower |
| Floral accent | Jasmine absolute | Rose, osmanthus | Different flower, same slot |
| Spice accent | Clove | Cassia, cardamom | Less dental, more bark-like |
| Fixative | Benzoin | Labdanum | Sweeter vs leathery |
| Binder | Makko | Gum binder | Different burn temperature and dough behaviour |

Two rules keep substitution honest. Never substitute across a family boundary and call it equivalent — a resin is not a wood, and the burn will say so. And always adjust the dose, because stand-ins are rarely equally potent: cassia in clove quantity will dominate a blend that clove merely seasoned.

## What "Better" Means Per Family

**Direct answer:** Quality language is family-specific, and applying one family's standard to another produces nonsense grades.

| Family | What "better" usually means | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| Woods | Resin density, species identity, soundness of the heartwood | Stronger smell in the packet |
| Resins | Clean tears, freshness, absence of grit | Larger lumps, darker colour |
| Flowers | Whole petals, colour retention, aromatic strength when crushed | Volume of powder |
| Spices | Whole, recently ground, oil-rich | Heat or pungency alone |
| Binders | Consistent fineness, reliable behaviour in the dough | Any scent contribution |
| Animal / mineral | Provenance and legality above all | Rarity as a proxy for quality |

The trap is that each family's quality markers are invisible in different ways: wood quality is internal, resin quality is visual, floral quality is perishable, spice quality is olfactory. A buyer who applies one test across the shelf will misjudge at least half of it.

## The Burn Signature of Each Family

**Direct answer:** Every family has a characteristic arc across a burn, and recognising the arc is faster than reading a label.

**Wood-led blend.** Heat penetrates fibre slowly → aroma builds through the first third → the middle carries the main character → the tail is heavy and resinous → ash holds its shape.

**Resin-led blend.** The tear softens quickly → the opening is the loudest moment → the middle thins → the tail is a whisper of the same note → ash is fine and pale.

**Floral blend.** Light volatiles leave early → the first minute is the peak → the middle is a memory → the tail is whatever wood or resin was underneath → the arc is short and steep.

**Spice-forward blend.** The spice dominates immediately → it recedes within minutes → the blend underneath becomes legible → the tail depends entirely on what was supporting the spice.

Read across the four and the design rule appears: a blend needs a wood or resin spine if it is to have a second half. Flowers and spices are punctuation; they cannot carry a burn.

## The Classical Palette and the Modern Gap

**Direct answer:** The historical manuals list materials that are now scarce, restricted, or unobtainable, and modern blends substitute quietly.

The Chinese compounding tradition compiled its palettes over centuries, and those lists include materials a contemporary workshop cannot legally or economically use. Some are restricted in trade, some have become too scarce to source at any sensible price, and some were regional specialities that never travelled.

Three adjustments follow, and they are normal rather than scandalous. Substitution by role keeps a formula's architecture while changing its ingredients. Reduction in the proportion of scarce materials preserves the character without the cost. And re-composition from scratch, using what is available, produces a new blend in an old style rather than a copy.

The honest framing for a buyer: a modern "classical" blend is a reconstruction, not a reproduction. That does not make it bad. It does mean that claims of exact historical fidelity deserve a raised eyebrow, particularly when the original material is regulated.

## Roots, Barks, and the Materials Between Families

**Direct answer:** A number of materials sit between the tidy families — roots, barks, and woody herbs that behave partly like woods and partly like spices — and they are where most regional character comes from.

Spikenard (甘松, gān sōng, *Nardostachys jatamansi*) is the clearest example: a rhizome with an earthy, persistent, slightly animalic character that behaves like a woody base but arrives in small quantity. Vetiver root is similar in role and different in tone — dry, smoky, and long-lasting. Patchouli is a leaf that behaves like a resin, which is exactly why it confuses people sorting by plant part rather than by burn behaviour.

Orris root, from *Iris* species, is the quietest material in this group: a fixative that smells of violets only faintly and mostly works by slowing everything else down. Barks such as cassia and cinnamon belong with the spices on the strength of their oils, but their physical form behaves like a woody material, releasing slowly and irregularly.

The lesson is that a family is a behavioural category, not a botanical one. Sorting by plant part — root, bark, leaf, heartwood — produces a tidier taxonomy and a worse prediction. Sort by how the material gives up its aroma and the table above holds.

## How Proportions Change With Format

**Direct answer:** The same palette produces a stick, a coil, a cone, or a loose powder with different proportions, because each format asks something different of the mix.

| Format | Binder share | Particle size | What the format demands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coreless stick | Moderate to high | Fine and even | Enough body to stand without a splint |
| Cored stick | Moderate | Fine | Paste that grips a bamboo cane and does not tear |
| Coil | High | Fine | A dough that bends without splitting on the spiral |
| Cone | High | Fine | Stiffness to hold a point while drying |
| Loose powder | None | Coarse to fine by use | No body at all; the burner supplies the structure |

The mechanism is straightforward: a shape that has to hold itself needs more structural material, and more structural material means proportionally less aromatic load. A coil and a coreless stick built from the same recipe will not smell identical, even though nothing in the formula changed.

Three consequences follow. A format change is a reformulation, not a repackaging. A blend that works beautifully as a stick may crumble as a cone, because the geometry demands a different dough. And a comparison between two formats is only fair when the aromatic load per gram is held constant — which almost no marketing comparison does.

Particle size interacts with the same logic. Finer powder packs denser, holds a shape better, and releases more slowly; coarser powder needs more binder to cohere and burns more openly. Grinding is therefore a formulation variable that sits alongside the ingredient list, which is why two products with identical labels can behave differently.

## Storage: What Quietly Changes a Scent

**Direct answer:** Materials degrade in storage in family-specific ways, and the damage is usually complete before the product reaches a customer.

| Family | What goes wrong | What it looks like afterwards |
|---|---|---|
| Woods | Surface drying, loss of volatiles | Duller, dustier, less creamy |
| Resins | Moisture uptake, clumping | Sticky mass, cloudy tears |
| Flowers | Oxidation, fading | Pale, papery, nearly scentless |
| Spices | Volatile loss after grinding | Flat, dusty, no bite |
| Binders | Moisture uptake | Dough behaves differently, sticks crack |

The mechanism is shared: aromatic compounds are volatile, and volatility means loss. Heat accelerates it, light accelerates it, oxygen accelerates it, and moisture does structural damage on top. Sealed, dark, cool and dry covers most of the fix, and the [storage and longevity page](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) covers the rest.

For a workshop, the practical discipline is stock rotation: older material moves first, and material is bought in quantities that can be used within its aromatic life rather than its physical shelf life. Those two lifetimes are not the same, and the aromatic one is shorter.

## The Glossary's Honest Limits

**Direct answer:** A family-level reference cannot tell you what is in a specific stick, and it should not pretend otherwise.

Four limits are worth stating plainly. **Family predicts behaviour, not quality** — a well-made and a badly made sandalwood stick belong to the same family and differ enormously. **Names are not identities** — a label word can cover several species, and the species is what determines behaviour. **Proportion is invisible** — a list without quantities cannot tell you what dominates the burn. **Sensory description is personal** — words like "creamy" or "medicinal" are conventions, not measurements, and two experienced noses will disagree at the edges.

What the framework does deliver is a way to ask better questions: which family, which species, what proportion, what role, and what was substituted. Those five questions will get more information out of a supplier than any amount of adjective-reading.

## Evidence status

This page synthesises craft practice, trade documentation and classical manuals; it reports no measured test of its own. Family assignments are editorial, based on observed burn behaviour rather than on a formal classification, and the behaviour descriptions are qualitative — no release rate, percentage or threshold here is a laboratory figure. Where a classical text is the only record, as with the 合香 palettes and historical material lists, it is cited as recorded tradition, not as current sourcing advice. CITES and regulatory mentions describe listing status; they say nothing about the quality or legality of a particular piece.

## FAQ

### Q: How many ingredient families does incense use?
Five plant families carry almost everything — woods, resins, flowers, spices and binders — plus a small residual group of animal and mineral materials that are mostly restricted or obsolete. The five are enough to predict burn behaviour in most cases, which is why the glossary is organised around them rather than around an alphabetical list.

### Q: Do the flowers in incense come from real blooms?
Sometimes, and less often than the label implies. Whole dried petals appear in better masala and classical blends; cheaper products use floral fragrance compounds that imitate the flower. A blend naming jasmine but smelling of soap is usually telling you which of the two it used. The [scent guide](/blog/scent-guide/) covers how floral accords are built when real petals are impractical.

### Q: How does wood incense differ from resin incense?
Mainly in release rate. Wood holds its aromatics inside dense fibre, so heat has to penetrate before anything leaves — the result is a slow build and a long tail. Resin melts and volatilizes almost immediately, giving a loud opening and a short arc. That is why wood usually leads a blend and resin usually accents it.

### Q: What does a binder do in incense?
It turns powder into a body that can be shaped and that will hold together while burning. In traditional work the binder is a wood flour — makko being the classic — so it also contributes fuel and a faint woody background. Gum binders do the same structural job with different burn behaviour, which is why binder choice changes the smell even when the aromatic recipe is identical.

### Q: Can I swap clove for cassia?
You can swap the role, not the material. Both are warm spices, but clove is dominated by eugenol, which reads dental above a low threshold, while cassia is bark-like and hotter in a different register. Use cassia at a similar or slightly higher weight where clove was the accent, and expect a drier, less sweet result. Never swap by volume without tasting the blend first.

## Summary

- Five plant families — wood, resin, flower, spice, binder — plus a small restricted residue cover the palette.
- Family predicts burn behaviour: woods build slowly, resins open fast, petals fade early, spices shout briefly.
- Naming is the hard part: one material, several names; one name, several materials.
- Substitution works by role, never across family boundaries.
- Quality language is family-specific, and no single test reads the whole shelf.
- Storage damage is family-specific too, and mostly invisible until it is total.

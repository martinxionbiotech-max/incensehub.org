**Direct answer:** Incense ingredients fall into a handful of families — woods (sandalwood, agarwood), resins (frankincense, myrrh, benzoin), flowers (jasmine, rose, osmanthus, lotus), spices (clove, cassia), and binders (makko). Once you can name the family, you already understand most of how a scent will act under heat.

**Key facts:** This glossary is a fast reference, not a complete catalog. Each entry lists the material, its origin, and its general character; the linked guides carry the depth. The one idea to keep throughout is *family predicts behavior*.

---## Why the Family Beats the Individual Name

**Direct answer:** The family — wood, resin, flower, spice, binder — predicts how a material burns better than the material's own name does, because the family fixes the physics of the burn.

Here is the chain the whole glossary hangs on. Burn behavior comes from structure → wood and resin are dense, resinous solids that smolder slowly; flowers and leaves are thin, delicate tissue that flares and scorches; binders are selected to be neutral fuel → that structural contrast decides whether a note shows up early or late, loud or quiet. So when a label names something you have never met, the family tells you its role before you light it. That is the entire point of arranging the glossary by family.

---## Woods: The Slow, Deep Foundation

**Direct answer:** Woods anchor a blend — they burn slowly and carry the deep, steady base, with agarwood and sandalwood as the two great classical woods and cedar as a lighter, everyday note that hides a naming snag.

| Material | Origin | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Sandalwood** (檀香, tánxiāng) | *Santalum* heartwood | Milky, honeyed, gentle timber |
| **Agarwood** (沉香, chénxiāng) | Resin-impregnated *Aquilaria* wood | Dark, balsamic, sweetly resinous |
| **Cedar** (雪松, xuěsōng) | *Cedrus* spp. (often *Juniperus virginiana* "cedarwood") | Dry, clean, pencil-wood |

The cedar row hides a trap: "cedarwood" in Western aromatics frequently means Eastern redcedar (*Juniperus virginiana*), not a true *Cedrus*. One common name points at two unrelated plants — which is precisely the kind of ambiguity the scientific name exists to remove.

---## Resins: The Bright, Balsamic Lift

**Direct answer:** Resins are dried tree exudates — brighter and more balsamic than woods — and they often double as fixatives that help a blend hold its scent; frankincense is the fresh ritual resin, benzoin the sweet fixative.

| Material | Origin | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Frankincense** (乳香, rǔxiāng) | *Boswellia* gum-resin | Zesty, citrus, balsamic |
| **Myrrh** (没药, mòyào) | *Commiphora* gum-resin | Bitter, balsamic, loamy |
| **Benzoin** (安息香, ānxīxiāng) | *Styrax* resin | Sweet, vanilla-like, warm |
| **Pine resin** (松香, sōngxiāng) | *Pinus* oleoresin (rosin) | Woody, balsamic |
| **Sweetgum** (枫香, fēngxiāng) | *Liquidambar formosana* | Sweet, balsamic |

The wood-vs-resin split is the first distinction worth memorizing. Wood is a tree's heartwood; resin is the tree's dried exudate. The two react differently to heat, and that difference is the whole reason classical blends pair them together.

---## Flowers: The Accord Trap

**Direct answer:** Flowers are the classic "accord trap" — in most modern incense the floral note is a constructed fragrance accord rather than the raw flower, because the flower cannot survive combustion.

| Material | Origin | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Jasmine** (茉莉, mòlì) | *Jasminum* flowers | Rich, sweet, heady floral |
| **Rose** (玫瑰, méigui) | *Rosa* petals | Classic, sweet floral |
| **Osmanthus** (桂花, guìhuā) | *Osmanthus fragrans* | Sweet, apricot-fruity floral |
| **Lotus** (莲花, liánhuā) | *Nelumbo* flower | Fresh, subtle, aquatic-floral |
| **Chrysanthemum** (菊花, júhuā) | *Chrysanthemum morifolium* | Herbal, faintly bitter floral |

When a label says "lotus" or "lavender," ask whether it means the flower or an accord. They are not the same thing — a raw flower that will not survive the flame, versus a blended impression assembled from other materials that will.

---## Spices: The Warm Lift

**Direct answer:** Spices contribute warmth and lift, and they are almost always blend components rather than solo scents.

| Material | Origin | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Clove** (丁香, dīngxiāng) | *Syzygium aromaticum* buds | Warm, spicy, sweet |
| **Cassia** (桂皮, guìpí) | *Cinnamomum cassia* bark | Warm, sweet-spicy |
| **Aged mandarin peel** (陈皮, chénpí) | *Citrus reticulata* peel | Warm, sweet, earthy citrus |

Clove is the sharp one; cassia the warm-sweet one. Both read as "warm spice," yet they hold different roles in a 合香 formula, so they cannot be swapped.

---## Roots, Herbs, and the Rest: The Catch-All

**Direct answer:** This is the catch-all family — roots, leaves, and aromatics that are neither woods nor resins — many of them workhorses in 合香 blends as herbal middle tones and cool, sharp accents.

| Material | Origin | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Mugwort** (艾草, àicǎo) | *Artemisia* herb | Herbal, green, slightly bitter |
| **Angelica** (白芷, báizhǐ) | *Angelica dahurica* root | Herbal, aromatic |
| **Calamus** (菖蒲, chāngpú) | *Acorus calamus* rhizome | Spicy, aromatic (safety caveat) |
| **Patchouli** (广藿香, guǎnghuòxiāng) | *Pogostemon cablin* leaf | Earthy, deep |
| **Vetiver** (岩兰草, yánláncǎo) | *Chrysopogon zizanioides* root | Deep, earthy, smoky |
| **Borneol** (龙脑, lóngnǎo) | *Dryobalanops aromatica* (natural d-borneol) | Camphoraceous, cool |

The calamus caveat needs its own sentence: *Acorus calamus* contains β-asarone, and some varieties are restricted in food use, so calamus incense should get the same ventilation caution as any aromatic — a material warning, not a fragrance note.

---## Binders: The Quiet Signal

**Direct answer:** Binders are functional — they hold the form and help it burn without adding their own scent — and the binder you find is a quiet signal of what kind of stick you are buying.

| Material | Origin | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Makko** (抹香, mǒxiāng) | *Machilus thunbergii* bark | Near-odorless combustible binder |
| **Honey** (蜂蜜, fēngmì) | — | Sweet binder in classical pills |
| **Charcoal / bark powder** (炭皮末) | — | Combustion aid |

The mechanism that makes the binder a signal: a natural wood binder (makko) means the stick is held together by material itself → it suggests a material-based stick; a charcoal or bark-powder base means the stick needs an added combustion aid → it usually marks a cheap fragrance-oil stick. Checking the binder is a fast, honest way to read a label. See [makko & natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

---## How Each Family Behaves Under Flame

**Direct answer:** Each family behaves differently under heat, and the difference is why a good incense is rarely one ingredient — no single material covers the whole burn.

- **Woods** burn slowly and carry the deep, persistent base — the "long" notes.
- **Resins** release bright, balsamic top-and-middle notes quickly and often act as fixatives.
- **Flowers** fade fast and are fragile under direct flame, which is why they are usually accords.
- **Spices** add a quick warm lift at the start and support the middle.
- **Roots/herbs** give earthy or herbal middle tones that ground the brighter materials.
- **Binders** add no scent of their own; they keep the form burning evenly.

This is the practical logic of 合香: a blend spreads the scent across the full duration, layering a fast top note over a slow base so the piece unfolds rather than arriving all at once.

## Confusion vs: The Ingredient Pairs That Get Swapped

**Direct answer:** Several ingredient pairs get substituted for each other because they share a word or a character class — benzoin vs frankincense, clove vs cassia, wood vs resin, and flower-as-material vs flower-as-accord — and each pair is told apart by its source, not its smell.

| Pair | What separates them | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Benzoin vs frankincense | Different plants — *Styrax* vs *Boswellia* | One sweetens and fixes, the other freshens and lifts |
| Clove vs cassia | Bud vs bark — *Syzygium* vs *Cinnamomum* | One is sharp and pungent, the other warm and sweet |
| Wood vs resin | Heartwood vs exudate | Wood is the slow base; resin is the fast balsamic lift |
| Flower material vs accord | Raw flower vs constructed impression | The flower won't survive the flame; the accord will |

The table collapses to a single lesson: when two ingredients get confused, the confusion almost always traces to a shared *name* rather than a shared *material*. The fix is the same every time — chase the scientific name.

## Names: Chinese, Pinyin, and Botanical

**Direct answer:** Each ingredient carries three names — the Chinese term (沉香), its romanization (chénxiāng), and the scientific name (*Aquilaria*) — and only the scientific name unambiguously identifies the plant.

The three are not interchangeable, and one Chinese word does not always map to one English word. 香 alone can mean fragrance, incense, or a fragrant material; 桂 can point at cassia or osmanthus depending on context. Each name serves a different job: the Chinese name is the tradition's vocabulary, the pinyin is how it is pronounced, and the scientific name is the anchor that common names lack. "Sandalwood," "cedarwood," and "lotus" are English common names that can each point at several different things — which is why the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/) builds its whole argument on species names.

## Ingredients in the Classical Manuals

**Direct answer:** The classical manuals (香谱, 香乘) name a working palette that overlaps heavily with this glossary — agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, borneol, musk, clove, spikenard, tonka/mellilot, operculum, and honey — but two of them are flagged for modern use.

Two entries deserve a flag. Musk (麝香) is animal-derived and largely restricted today, and 甲香 ([operculum](/ingredients/onycha/)) is shellfish-derived and rarely used in modern reproductions. The manuals are recipes for a specific historical palette, not a checklist for the modern kitchen. ## Reading an Ingredient Label

**Direct answer:** Three questions cut through most label ambiguity — which species, material or accord, and which binder.

1. **Which species?** "Sandalwood" can be *Santalum album*, another *Santalum*, or a synthetic; "cedarwood" can be true cedar or juniper.
2. **Material or accord?** "Lotus" or "lavender" is often a fragrance accord, not the flower.
3. **Binder?** A natural binder (makko) suggests a material-based stick; a charcoal base suggests a fragrance stick.

The family tells you the behavior; the label tells you whether you are getting the real material or a name. See the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/) for the full version.

## The Judgment Boundary

**Direct answer:** The family framework — woods, resins, flowers, spices, binders — is the fastest way to become ingredient-literate, but it works only if you hold two distinctions firmly: wood versus resin, and flower-as-material versus flower-as-accord.

My position is that the family predicts the behavior, and the two distinctions do the real work. The condition under which this breaks down is when a buyer trusts the common name without chasing the species — because every misleading label in this glossary is a common name pointing at the wrong plant or at an accord instead of a material. Memorizing the families gets you the map; the species name is what keeps you from reading it upside down.

## FAQ

### Q: What are the main families of incense ingredients?
Six families cover the field: woods (sandalwood, agarwood, cedar), resins (frankincense, myrrh, benzoin, pine resin, sweetgum), flowers (jasmine, rose, osmanthus, lotus, chrysanthemum), spices (clove, cassia, aged mandarin peel), roots and herbs (mugwort, angelica, calamus, patchouli, vetiver, borneol), and binders (makko, honey). Each family behaves differently under heat: woods give the deep, slow base; resins are bright and balsamic; flowers are fragile and usually accords; spices add warm lift; roots and herbs contribute earthy middle tones; binders hold the form without adding scent. Learning the family is most of the battle.

### Q: Are flowers real in incense?
Often not. Many floral incense scents are fragrance accords rather than the actual flower, because a raw flower does not burn well and its delicate scent does not survive an open flame. A "lotus" or "lavender" incense is usually a constructed floral impression, the same way perfume builds a floral accord from non-floral materials. When a label names a flower, check whether it means the real flower or an accord — the answer changes what you are actually smelling and buying. See the [lotus guide](/blog/lotus-incense/).

### Q: What is the difference between wood and resin incense?
Woods (sandalwood, agarwood) are tree heartwood; resins (frankincense, myrrh) are dried tree exudates. They behave differently: woods give a deep, persistent, slow-developing scent and form the base of a blend, while resins give fresh or balsamic brightness that arrives quickly and often doubles as a fixative. Wood is the body, resin is the lift. A material-based incense can be either, and many classical blends pair a wood chief with a resin assistant precisely to get the deep base and bright top together.

### Q: What is a binder in incense?
A functional ingredient — usually a combustible wood powder like makko (抹香, *Machilus thunbergii* bark) — that holds the formed incense together and helps it burn evenly, without adding its own scent. Honey fills a similar role in classical pill recipes, and a charcoal or bark-powder base is the binder of cheap modern sticks. The binder matters because it signals what kind of stick you have: a natural wood binder suggests a material-based stick, while a charcoal base usually means a fragrance-oil stick. See [makko & natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

## More Questions Answered

### Q: What is the difference between benzoin and frankincense?
Both are resins, but from different trees: benzoin (*Styrax*) is sweet and vanilla-balsamic; frankincense (*Boswellia*) is fresh, citrus, and earthy. Benzoin works more as a sweet base and fixative — it anchors and rounds a blend — while frankincense is a bright ritual resin that opens at the top. They do not swap: one sweetens and fixes, the other freshens and lifts. A blend can carry both, benzoin for the sweet base and frankincense for the bright top, but each holds a different job in the formula.

### Q: What is makko and why does it matter?
Makko (抹香) is the powdered bark of *Machilus thunbergii*, the traditional combustible binder. It holds incense together and burns cleanly with little odor of its own, the mark of a material-based stick. Why it matters: the binder is a quiet signal of what you are buying. A natural makko binder suggests the stick is made from real wood and resin powder, while a charcoal base usually indicates a fragrance-oil stick. Checking the binder is a fast, honest way to read a label. See [makko & natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

### Q: Why do some ingredients have Chinese names in this glossary?
Because the material tradition is Chinese, and the Chinese terms are the vocabulary the manuals and the trade actually use. Each entry gives the Chinese name, its pinyin, and the scientific name because they serve different purposes: the Chinese name is the tradition's vocabulary, the pinyin is how it is pronounced, and the scientific name is the unambiguous botanical anchor. English common names alone are unreliable — "sandalwood" or "cedarwood" can point at several different things — so keeping all three names is how this glossary stays precise rather than just familiar. When comparing products, the scientific name is the part to carry across labels.

### Q: What is spikenard (甘松) and is it common?
Spikenard (甘松, gānsōng) is an aromatic herb whose roots give a warm, earthy, slightly sweet-woody note, and it shows up repeatedly in classical 合香 recipes from the 香谱 and 香乘 as a minister or assistant ingredient. It is common in the historical recipes but rare on modern retail labels, which is why it sits in the manuals rather than the everyday spice rack. If you meet 甘松 in a recipe, treat it as a specialist 合香 herb: it rounds and grounds the woody base, an example of how the classical palette reached beyond the familiar woods and resins.

### Q: Are clove and cassia interchangeable in incense?
They are both warm spices but distinct: clove is the dried flower bud (*Syzygium aromaticum*), cassia is bark (*Cinnamomum cassia*). Clove is sharper and more pungent, with a slightly numbing edge; cassia is warmer and sweeter. They serve different ends in a blend — clove for a sharp spicy lift, cassia for warm sweetness — so they do not substitute, even though both read as "warm spice." In a 合香 formula they occupy different roles and proportions, so swapping one for the other changes the balance of the whole blend rather than simply trading one warm note for another.

## The Physics That Sorts the Families

**Direct answer:** The families in this glossary are not arbitrary bins — they are four different physical responses to heat, and that physical difference is what a buyer is really reading when they name a family.

Walk the physics. Dense, low-moisture solids — heartwood and hardened resin — store their volatiles deep and conduct heat slowly → they warm rather than flash → their scent releases over a long, steady window → they read as a base. Thin, moist tissue — petals and leaves — holds its scent in fragile surface cells → heat vaporizes that oil almost instantly → the note scorches and vanishes → the material must be captured or rebuilt. Dry, aromatic structural parts — buds, bark, peel — sit in between → they release a quick bright lift at the top of the burn. A neutral combustible powder — the binder — adds structure and carries the ember without adding scent. Four families, four heat responses, one physical story.

## A Substitute Matrix for the Kitchen and the Shelf

**Direct answer:** When a material runs out or a recipe calls for something unavailable, the question is always what can stand in — and the answer depends on the job the material was doing.

| You have | You want to swap for | Workable stand-in | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood | A soft, milky base | Cedarwood or a blend of mild woods | Loses some creaminess, keeps the base |
| Frankincense | A bright citrus lift | A touch of lemon peel or sweetgum | Brighter, slightly less balsamic |
| Benzoin | A sweet vanilla fixative | A small amount of tonka or vanilla note | Sweeter, less resinous body |
| Clove | A sharp spicy lift | A smaller dose of cassia plus pepper | Warmer, less numbing |
| Makko | A neutral binder | Another clean-burning wood powder | Must burn equally clean |

The matrix is a substitution guide, not a recipe, and the rule it encodes is: swap by role, not by name. Each row changes the blend's character in a predictable way, and knowing that change is what makes a substitution honest rather than a guess. For any serious substitution, the scientific name is the part that matters, because a common name can hide a different plant.

## The "Accord" Word Is Not an Insult

**Direct answer:** A misconception worth correcting is that a fragrance accord is a fake — it is a legitimate, often superior way to deliver a floral scent, and the only thing wrong with it is when a label hides it.

An accord is a constructed impression built from materials that can survive the flame. For flowers, which cannot be burned directly, the accord is frequently the better tool: it is stable, consistent, and far cheaper than a genuine absolute. The problem is never the accord itself; it is the label that says "lotus" and lets a buyer assume the raw flower. My position: treat "accord" as a method, not a slur. The honest version of a floral label names the method, and a buyer who reads "lotus" should simply ask "extract or accord" and accept either answer when it is told truthfully. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## More Questions Answered

### Q: How do I read an ingredient label I have never seen before?
Work it through the family first. Name the material and ask what family it belongs to — wood, resin, flower, spice, root, or binder — because the family tells you its role before you ever light it. Then chase the scientific name, because the common name is where labels mislead. Then check the binder, which signals whether the stick is material-based or built on a fragrance-oil filler. Three questions — which family, which species, which binder — will decode almost any label, even one full of names you have never met.

## The Scientific Name Is the Only Unambiguous Label

**Direct answer:** Every ingredient carries at least three names — the common name, the Chinese term, and the scientific binomial — and only the last one identifies the plant without ambiguity.

A common name like "cedarwood" can point at a true *Cedrus* or the unrelated *Juniperus virginiana*; "sandalwood" can mean *Santalum album*, a cheaper *Santalum*, or a synthetic; "lotus" drifts across traditions. The Chinese term is precise within its tradition but still a common name — 桂 can mean cassia or osmanthus depending on context. The scientific binomial resolves all of it, because it names one species and only one. This is why the glossary pairs every material with its binomial, and why a buyer comparing two labels should carry the species name across them, not the pretty English word.

## A Grading-Lens Table: What "Better" Means Per Family

**Direct answer:** "Quality" means a different thing in each family, so a buyer needs a different lens for woods, resins, flowers, and binders — the table below is that set of lenses.

| Family | What "better" means | What to look for | What to ignore |
|---|---|---|---|
| Woods | Resin density and aroma depth | Species, origin, sink test, warm aroma | Color alone, which dyes fake |
| Resins | Tear size, clarity, cleanliness | Pale, whole tears; named species | Dust and mixed fragments |
| Flowers | Extract over accord (if claimed) | "Extract" or "absolute" on the label | A vague "floral" note |
| Binders | Clean, neutral burn | A natural wood binder like makko | A charcoal filler |

The table's lesson is that each family hides its own fraud. Woods are faked by color and density, resins by mixing grades, flowers by passing an accord as an extract, and binders by swapping a neutral wood powder for a cheap filler. The lens that fits the family is the one that catches its particular trick.

## The Manual Palette and the Modern Gap

**Direct answer:** The classical manuals name a palette that overlaps heavily with this glossary, but two of its entries — musk and operculum — sit outside modern use, and the gap between the manual's shelf and today's shelf is worth naming.

Musk (麝香) is animal-derived and largely restricted, and 甲香 (operculum) is shellfish-derived and rarely used in modern reproductions. Their presence in the manuals is a record of a historical palette, not a recommendation for the modern kitchen. The practical point is that a classical recipe cannot always be reproduced ingredient-for-ingredient, and a modern maker often substitutes or omits. Reading the manuals as a window into a past palette — rather than a checklist — is the honest way to use them, and it is why the glossary flags these two entries instead of hiding them.

## How a Blend Spreads One Scent Across the Whole Burn

**Direct answer:** A good 合香 blend is a time-release structure, layering fast and slow materials so the scent unfolds instead of arriving all at once — and the family framework explains exactly how.

A top note — a spice or a bright resin — releases within the first moments of heat → it opens the piece with a lift. A middle note — a resin or an herbal — follows as the top fades → it carries the body. A base note — a wood — releases slowly across the whole burn → it anchors everything and is still going when the rest is spent. Layering all three spreads one scent across the full duration, which is why a single material rarely makes a satisfying incense. The family tells you which layer a material will occupy, which is the practical payoff of arranging the glossary by family in the first place.

## A Chain for Each Family's Burn Signature

**Direct answer:** Each family has a signature chain from structure to burn, and memorizing the four chains is a faster shortcut than memorizing forty materials.

Wood: dense, low-moisture heartwood → conducts heat slowly → releases volatiles over a long window → deep, persistent base. Resin: hardened exudate → melts and volatilizes under gentle heat → bright, balsamic lift that can also fix the blend. Flower: delicate surface oil → destroyed by direct flame → survives only as a captured extract or a rebuilt accord. Spice and peel: dry aromatic structure → quick, vivid release → a warm or bright lift at the top. Binder: neutral combustible powder → carries the ember without scent → holds the form and evens the burn. Five chains, five behaviors, and the whole glossary is an elaboration of them.

## Reading the Binder Before the Fragrance

**Direct answer:** The binder is the last thing a buyer looks at and the first thing that tells the truth about a stick, because a natural wood binder and a charcoal filler are the signatures of two different kinds of product.

A natural binder like makko means the stick is held together by clean-burning plant matter → the fragrant material is likely real powder rather than a perfumed filler. A charcoal or bark-powder base means the stick needs an added combustion aid → it usually marks a cheap fragrance-oil stick where the "scent" is a manufactured accord on an inert core. Neither is a moral judgment — a fragrance-oil stick is a legitimate product — but the two are different things sold at different prices, and the binder is the label that tells them apart. Check the binder first and you know what kind of stick you are holding before you light it.

## The Cedar Naming Snag, and Why It Repeats

**Direct answer:** The "cedarwood" confusion is not a one-off — it is an instance of a pattern that repeats across the glossary, where one common name points at unrelated plants, and the scientific name is the only fix.

In Western aromatics "cedarwood" frequently means Eastern redcedar (*Juniperus virginiana*), not a true *Cedrus*. The same snag appears with sandalwood, lotus, and cassia. The pattern is always the same: a common name was coined for one familiar plant, then borrowed for a lookalike or a substitute, and the two drifted apart. The remedy is identical every time — chase the binomial. When two materials get confused, the confusion almost always traces to a shared name rather than a shared material, and the scientific name is what breaks the tie.

## A Confusion Table for the Pairs That Keep Colliding

**Direct answer:** Four ingredient pairs get swapped constantly because they share a word or a character class — and each is separated by its source, not its smell.

| Pair | Shared word or class | What separates them | Which to reach for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benzoin vs frankincense | "Resin" | *Styrax* sweet fixative vs *Boswellia* bright lift | Fixative vs opener |
| Clove vs cassia | "Warm spice" | Bud vs bark; sharp vs sweet | Pungent lift vs warm sweetness |
| Sandalwood vs cedarwood | "Wood" | *Santalum* heartwood vs *Cedrus*/*Juniperus* | Creamy base vs dry pencil note |
| Flower vs flower accord | "Flower" | Raw material vs constructed note | Extract vs impression |

The table collapses to one rule: when a pair collides, chase the source. The word that confuses them is the common name; the thing that separates them is the species and the part. Read the binomial and the collision resolves itself.

## What the Chinese Names Add

**Direct answer:** The Chinese terms in this glossary are not decoration — they carry information the English common names lack, and glossing them recovers it.

沉香 (chénxiāng) literally means "sinking fragrance," encoding the density test that grades agarwood. 乳香 (rǔxiāng) means "milk fragrance," naming the milky resin that hardens into tears. 龙脑 (lóngnǎo) means "dragon's brain," a poetic name for the camphoraceous borneol. Each term is a compressed description, and reading them restores a layer of meaning the English loanword drops. The pinyin gives the pronunciation, and the binomial gives the identity — three names, three jobs, and only together do they cover what a material actually is.

## A Field Guide to What to Sniff For

**Direct answer:** Before you buy, a few quick checks — the family, the species, the binder, and any "accord" note — turn a shelf of labels into a set of predictions you can test.

1. Name the family. Wood, resin, flower, spice, or binder — this tells you the role.
2. Chase the species. The binomial, not the common name, is the label that cannot lie.
3. Check the binder. A natural wood binder versus a charcoal filler tells you the build.
4. Ask about the flower. "Extract or accord" is the question that exposes the method.

Run those four and the label stops being a list of nice words and starts being a recipe you can read. A wood name tells you slow base; a resin name tells you bright lift; a flower name without a method tells you to ask one more question before you pay.

## The Six Families in One Burn

**Direct answer:** A single stick is a small orchestra, and the family framework lets you hear which section is playing at any moment in the burn.

Light a material-based stick and the timeline unfolds by family. The first seconds are the spices and bright resins → they give the opening lift. The middle of the burn is the resins and herbs → they carry the body. The long tail is the wood → it anchors the whole thing and lingers after the rest is spent. The binder never audibly enters — it only keeps the ember moving. A stick that is all wood would open dull; a stick that is all spice would burn out in a flash; the blend exists to spread one scent across time, and the family tells you which layer each ingredient occupies.

## Why "Family Predicts Behavior" Is the Whole Glossary

**Direct answer:** The one idea this page keeps repeating — family predicts behavior — is not a slogan but the organizing principle, because it compresses forty materials into four or five behaviors a buyer can hold in their head.

You cannot memorize every material, and you do not need to. What you need is the mapping from family to behavior — wood to slow base, resin to bright lift, flower to fragile accord, spice to quick lift, binder to neutral fuel. Once that mapping is in place, any new name is instantly placed: "this is a wood, so it anchors; this is a resin, so it lifts." The family is a compression algorithm for a large and confusing catalog, and it is the reason the glossary is arranged the way it is.

## The Line Between Material and Name

**Direct answer:** The deepest distinction in this glossary is not between two ingredients but between a material and a name — and every misleading label is a name drifting free of its material.

A material is a plant part with a species and a smell. A name is a word on a label. The two stay attached when a maker names the species and the method; they drift apart when a maker sells "sandalwood" that is a cheaper *Santalum*, "cedarwood" that is juniper, or "lotus" that is an accord. The glossary's whole purpose is to keep the name tied to the material by giving every entry three anchors — Chinese, pinyin, and binomial — so the word cannot float. Buy the material, not the name, and the drift stops mattering.

## Why a Wood and a Resin Smell Different Under the Same Flame

**Direct answer:** Wood and resin land on the same heater but release through different physics — wood gives its volatiles up slowly from a solid matrix, resin melts first and then volatilizes — and that difference is why one reads as a base and the other as a lift.

Track the two pathways side by side. Heartwood: heat must first penetrate a dense, porous solid → the trapped volatiles migrate out gradually → the release is slow and drawn out → the note arrives as a long base. Resin: heat first softens the hardened exudate → the resin melts into a liquid film → the liquid surface evaporates its volatiles quickly → the note arrives bright and fast. The same flame, two different phase changes, two different timelines. This is why a blend pairs them: the wood supplies the length, the resin supplies the opening brightness, and the two together cover a burn that neither could hold alone.

## A Substitute Matrix by Role, Not by Name

**Direct answer:** The correct way to substitute any ingredient is to match the role it was playing, not the name it goes by — and the table below maps the common roles to their stand-ins.

| The role you need to fill | First-choice stand-in | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Deep, slow base | Sandalwood for agarwood, or an aged wood | Loses the dark resin depth |
| Bright, balsamic lift | Sweetgum or a citrus resin for frankincense | Brighter, less earthy |
| Sweet vanilla fixative | Tonka or a vanilla note for benzoin | Sweeter, thinner body |
| Warm spice accent | Cassia plus a trace of pepper for clove | Warmer, less numbing |
| Neutral combustible binder | Another clean-burning bark powder for makko | Must burn equally clean |

The matrix's lesson is that substitution is a judgment about function, not a trivia question about names. A stand-in that fills the role well may be a better choice than a "correct" material that fills it badly. The scientific name matters because the role lives in the species and the part, not in the common name on the tin.

## The Accord Is a Method, Not a Deception

**Direct answer:** A recurring misconception is that a floral accord is a fake — the honest truth is that an accord is a legitimate method for delivering a scent that cannot survive the flame, and the only fault is a label that hides the method.

An accord is a constructed impression, assembled from materials that can burn cleanly, standing in for a flower whose own oil is destroyed by heat. For most florals it is not merely acceptable — it is the better tool, more stable and far cheaper than a genuine absolute. The wrongness enters only when the label says "lotus" and lets a buyer assume the raw flower. My position: ask "extract or accord," and accept either answer when it is told straight. An accord is not a lie; a hidden method is. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## More Questions Answered

### Q: Why do the same Chinese names keep appearing in recipes from different centuries?
Because the Chinese incense canon is a continuous, self-referencing tradition — later manuals copy and adapt earlier ones, and the same core palette (agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, borneol, musk, clove, spikenard) recurs across the 香谱 and 香乘. A recurring name is a sign of a living tradition, not of a single author. This is also why the glossary leans on the classical manuals for its working vocabulary: the names on the modern shelf descend from those pages, and reading the old palette is how a buyer learns to read the new label.

## The Two Distinctions That Do the Real Work

**Direct answer:** The family framework is the map, but two distinctions inside it — wood versus resin, and flower-as-material versus flower-as-accord — are what actually separate a careful buyer from a careless one.

Take the first distinction. Wood is heartwood, the tree's body; resin is exudate, the tree's secretion → the two react to heat differently → wood gives a slow base, resin a fast lift → a blend pairs them for that reason. Take the second. A flower can be the raw material or a constructed accord → the raw flower dies under flame, the accord survives → the label's job is to say which. Everything else in the glossary — the naming traps, the substitution risks, the binder signals — is an elaboration of these two distinctions. Memorize the families and you can place any ingredient; hold these two distinctions and you can read any label.

## How the Manuals Became the Modern Shelf

**Direct answer:** The ingredients on a modern label descend from the classical manuals in a mostly unbroken line, and reading that descent explains why the same handful of materials recurs everywhere.

The 香谱 and 香乘 fixed a working palette — agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, borneol, musk, clove, spikenard, operculum, honey → later makers copied and adapted those formulas → the palette survived because it worked → the modern shelf carries the survivors, minus the two animal-derived entries that fell out of use. What this means for a buyer: the names on a contemporary tin are not random marketing, they are the tail end of a centuries-old tradition, and the two entries that dropped out — musk and operculum — are the visible seam where the old palette met modern restriction. Read the manuals and the modern label stops looking arbitrary.

## Why One Word, Several Plants Is the Default, Not the Exception

**Direct answer:** The glossary's naming traps are not rare misfortunes — they are the normal state of common names, because a common name is coined for whatever plant a given region knew, then borrowed for every lookalike that followed.

Trace how a name spreads. A trade or a village names one familiar plant → a merchant or a botanist later meets a different plant that smells or looks similar → the old name gets reused for the new plant, because it is easier than coining a word → two unrelated species now share one label → the confusion is born. This happened to "cedar," to "sandalwood," and to "cassia," each time leaving one word pointing at several plants. The scientific binomial is the fix because it refuses to travel this way — it pins one name to one species and stays there. The naming trap is not a bug in the glossary; it is the reason the glossary exists.

## What "Family Predicts Behavior" Does Not Predict

**Direct answer:** The family framework is powerful but bounded, and knowing its boundary keeps it honest — it predicts burn behavior and role, not quality, grade, or whether the material in your hand is the real one.

State the boundary plainly. The family tells you how a material behaves under heat → a wood smolders slow, a resin melts bright, a flower dies → that is a statement about structure, and it is reliable. It does not tell you whether this particular sandalwood is high-grade, whether that agarwood is genuine, or whether the "lotus" accord is well made → those are statements about quality and authenticity, and they need grading, the seller, and the nose, not the family. My position: use the family to place an ingredient and predict its burn, then switch tools for everything else. The framework is a map, and a map is not a verdict. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Physics of the Burn: Pyrolysis, Not Flaming

**Direct answer:** Incense does not burn the way a candle does — it smolders through pyrolysis, a low-oxygen, low-flame process that decomposes the material into smoke and volatiles — and that single fact is why different ingredients release at different rates.

A candle melts wax into a wick and flames. Incense skips the flame. The glowing tip is a slow decomposition front → heat breaks down cellulose, lignin, and resin into gases and carbon char without a visible flame → the gases carry the fragrance volatiles into the room → the char advances the front. Why this matters: pyrolysis is slower and cooler than flaming, so a material's release rate is set by how easily its structure decomposes. Dense, resin-laden heartwood decomposes slowly → it reads as a long base. Light, thin tissue decomposes fast → it reads as a quick top. The family framework is, underneath, a pyrolysis map — and that is the physical reason "family predicts behavior" actually holds.

## How a Stick Is Actually Assembled

**Direct answer:** A material-based stick is a wet dough that is kneaded, shaped, and dried — the binder is what makes the dough hold together, and the drying is what makes the scent survive until you light it.

Follow the assembly. The fragrant powders are mixed with a binder and just enough water to form a workable dough → the dough is kneaded to an even consistency so no single ingredient clumps → it is extruded or rolled into rods → the rods dry slowly, losing their water without cracking → the dry rod is the finished stick. Each step is a control point. Too little binder and the dough crumbles; too much and the stick is mostly filler. Uneven kneading leaves pockets where one note dominates. Fast drying cracks the rod; slow drying lets it hold. The binder is not glue in the furniture sense — it is the dough's backbone, and the drying is what locks the fragrance in place for the shelf.

## Buying and Storing: What Quietly Changes the Scent

**Direct answer:** An ingredient's scent is not fixed at harvest — oxygen, light, heat, and humidity keep working on it after you bring it home, so how you store a material changes what it smells like months later.

The changes are slow but real. Oxygen reacts with the volatile oils → terpenes and other aromatics oxidize → the scent dulls or turns flat. Light adds energy to the same reactions → it accelerates the dulling. Heat does the same → a warm cupboard ages material faster than a cool one. Humidity invites mold and softens powder → it can ruin a batch outright. The counter-move is the same across every family: seal the material, keep it dark and cool and dry, and use it within a reasonable window. A buyer who treats incense as shelf-stable is quietly trading away the top notes; a buyer who stores carefully keeps them. See the [storage guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/).

## The Smell Wheel the Family Framework Skips

**Direct answer:** The glossary groups by family, but the family alone does not tell you how a scent *feels* — for that you need a second, independent axis: fresh versus warm versus earthy.

Split the same ingredients a different way. Fresh covers frankincense, borneol, and citrus peel → they lift and open. Warm covers clove, cassia, benzoin, and sandalwood → they round and sweeten. Earthy covers agarwood, myrrh, patchouli, and vetiver → they ground and deepen. This second axis crosses the family axis at right angles: a resin can be fresh (frankincense) or warm (benzoin), a wood can be warm (sandalwood) or earthy (agarwood). The two axes together are a crude scent wheel, and it is the missing half of "family predicts behavior" — the family tells you how it burns, the feel tells you what it will do to the room.

## A Beginner's First Three Purchases, and Why

**Direct answer:** A beginner building a first kit is better served by three contrastive materials — one wood, one resin, one spice — than by any single expensive wood, because contrast is what teaches the families.

Start with a wood, a resin, and a spice. A sandalwood or cedar powder gives the slow, deep base → you learn what a base does to a burn. A frankincense or benzoin gives the bright, balsamic lift → you learn what a resin does. A clove or cassia gives the quick, vivid lift → you learn what a spice does. Three materials, three families, three clearly different behaviors, all cheap. A single costly agarwood teaches you one material; three contrastive cheap ones teach you the system. The beginner's error is to buy the most expensive wood first and learn nothing but its price. Buy contrast, not prestige, and the families teach themselves.

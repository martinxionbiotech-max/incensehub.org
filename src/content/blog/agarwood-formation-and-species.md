**Direct answer:** Agarwood forms when an *Aquilaria* tree is wounded, microbes colonise the wound, and the tree deposits resin into the surrounding heartwood. The genus has 21 accepted species, and the resin is a stress response rather than a normal growth product — which is why healthy wood is pale, light, and nearly scentless.

## Key facts

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| What triggers resin? | Wounding plus microbial colonisation, often described as a stress response |
| How many species? | 21 accepted *Aquilaria* species, plus *Gyrinops* |
| What is in the resin? | Sesquiterpenes and 2-(2-phenylethyl)chromones, among other compounds |
| How long does it take? | Years in plantations; decades in wild trees |
| Is it legal to trade? | *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* are CITES Appendix II |

The useful distinction is between **formation** and **quality**. Formation explains why the wood exists at all. Quality explains why one piece smells like a damp forest floor and another like honey and incense. They are related, but they are not the same process, and the trade routinely conflates them.

## Key Takeaways

- A wound alone is not enough. The tree must respond, and the microbial community shapes the response.
- The old single-fungus story — *Phaeoacremonium parasitica* and nothing else — is too simple for the current evidence.
- Sesquiterpenes and chromones are different chemical families with different behaviour in heat and solvent.
- 生结 and 熟结 describe where the resin formed, not how good it is.
- Plantation induction is a real technology, not a synonym for fake. The question is method, dose, and honesty.
- Species identification is the first legal and commercial fact; grade language comes later.

## The five-stage formation clock

**Direct answer:** Agarwood formation runs through wound, colonisation, chemical response, maturation, and — in wild material — death and burial. Each stage leaves a different fingerprint in the wood.

**1. The wound.** Wind, lightning, insects, animals, or a deliberate cut breach the bark and cambium. The tree cannot heal the breach quickly, so the site stays open to air and water.

**2. Colonisation.** Bacteria and fungi enter. The literature has spent decades trying to name the responsible organism. *Phaeoacremonium parasitica* is the classic candidate, *Fusarium solani* appears repeatedly in inoculation experiments, and newer work treats the community rather than a single pathogen as the relevant unit.

**3. The chemical response.** The tree produces sesquiterpenes and chromones in the tissue around the wound. Sesquiterpenes carry much of the woody-spicy aroma; chromones are a large, structurally diverse family that has attracted pharmacological interest and also contributes to the material's colour and bitterness. A 2024 study in *Frontiers in Plant Science* tracked 2-(2-phenylethyl)chromones during whole-tree induction and found their concentration rising as resin filled the wood.

**4. Maturation.** Resin accumulates over years. The wood darkens, gains density, and becomes hydrophobic. This is the stage buyers are actually paying for, and it is the stage a photograph cannot verify.

**5. Death and burial.** In wild forests, a resinous tree may die, fall, and lie in leaf litter for years. The result is 熟结 — "ripe" or "matured" formation — where the wood has weathered, the resin has concentrated, and the aroma has had time to change. Plantation material is usually harvested from living trees, which is 生结.

The clock is useful because it separates claims that are often bundled. "Wild" refers to stage five, or at least to a forest origin. "Old" refers to stage four. "Sinking" refers to the resin load at the end of stage four. A piece can be old without being wild, wild without being old, and heavy without being either.

## What the resin is made of

**Direct answer:** Agarwood resin is not one substance. It is a mixture of sesquiterpenes, chromones, and related compounds whose ratio changes with species, wound type, microbes, and time.

The two families behave differently:

- **Sesquiterpenes** are volatile enough to reach the nose when the wood is warmed. Compounds such as agarospirol and α-agarofuran are part of the classic agarwood vocabulary. They are the reason a chip can smell complex at low temperature.
- **Chromones** are heavier and less volatile. They contribute colour, bitterness, and much of the pharmacological literature around agarwood. They are also a reason burning and heating produce different scent profiles: the wood is not a single oil being released at one temperature.

A 2022 review in *Molecules* puts the number of fragrant molecules at roughly 150 and describes the aroma as the product of "complex biotic, abiotic, and physical stress." That is a useful corrective to the perfume-industry habit of naming a single "oud note." The material is a chord, not a note.

The chemistry also explains a common buyer experience: two pieces from the same species can smell different. Species sets a range; the wound, the microbial community, and the maturation time decide where inside that range the piece lands.

## The species, and why the list keeps changing

**Direct answer:** *Aquilaria* contains 21 accepted species native to South and Southeast Asia. The commercial names — Malaccan, Vietnamese, Chinese — refer to a handful of them, while the rest sit in regional trade and taxonomic revision.

| Species | Common trade association | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Aquilaria sinensis* | Chinese agarwood, 白木香 | The Chinese species; plantation supply is significant |
| *A. malaccensis* | Malaccan agarwood | Classic Southeast Asian trade species; heavily exploited |
| *A. crassna* | Vietnamese agarwood | Large plantation sector in Vietnam and Laos |
| *A. hirta* | — | A source of "gaharu" in parts of Indonesia and Malaysia |
| *A. beccariana* | — | Borneo and Sumatra |
| *A. rugosa* | — | Mainland Southeast Asia |

The genus is in Thymelaeaceae, the same family as *Daphne* and *Edgeworthia*. *Gyrinops* is a separate genus in the same family, also resin-producing, also CITES-listed. The practical consequence is that "agarwood" in a trade document may cover several genera and more than twenty species.

Taxonomy matters for two reasons. First, CITES permits are species- and shipment-specific; a permit for one species does not automatically cover another. Second, species affects aroma and price. *A. crassna* plantation wood and *A. malaccensis* wild wood are not interchangeable, even when both are sold as "agarwood."

A nomenclatural trap: *Aquilaria agallocha* appears in older literature and trade lists as a synonym. It is not a separate species in current treatments. A supplier quoting "A. agallocha" is either using an old name or repeating a catalogue, not demonstrating botanical expertise.

## Four formation types the trade still uses

**Direct answer:** 生结, 熟结, 虫漏, and 脱落 describe how and where resin formed. They are descriptive categories, not a quality ranking.

- **生结 (shēng jié)** — resin in a living tree. The tree is harvested while the resin is still being deposited. Most plantation wood is 生结.
- **熟结 (shú jié)** — resin in a dead or fallen tree. The wood has weathered in the forest, and the resin has concentrated. Wild 熟结 is the scarce, expensive category.
- **虫漏 (chóng lòu)** — resin formed around insect borings. The wound geometry is small and repeated, and the resin can be concentrated in pockets.
- **脱落 (tuō luò)** — resin formed at an external break or where a branch has fallen away.

The categories are useful for reading a seller's story, but they are not testable from a photograph. A piece sold as 熟结 should come with a forest origin and a plausible chain; otherwise the word is doing marketing work. The [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) explains how these categories interact with the water test and the 奇楠 distinction.

## Induction: what plantations actually do

**Direct answer:** Plantation agarwood is induced, not waited for. Growers wound trees deliberately — by drilling, nailing, cutting, or inoculating with microbial cultures — and harvest the resinous wood years later.

The methods fall into a few families:

| Method | What is done | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical wounding | Drilling, nailing, cutting, bark removal | Cheap; inconsistent resin distribution |
| Microbial inoculation | Applying cultured fungi or bacteria to wounds | More uniform in some trials; depends on strain and environment |
| Chemical induction | Applying compounds that stress the tree | Faster; residues and quality are open questions |
| Whole-tree induction | Treating the standing tree so resin spreads through the trunk | Higher yield; requires technique and time |

The 2024 *Frontiers in Plant Science* study followed whole-tree induction for a year and found chromone concentration rising with resin filling. That is a useful data point because it shows induction is not a binary "fake versus real" switch. It is a biological process with a measurable trajectory.

The honest position is that plantation agarwood can be good, bad, or indifferent. The variables are the tree's age, the induction method, the microbial environment, the harvest timing, and the drying and storage after harvest. A plantation origin tells you the wood was not taken from a wild forest. It does not tell you the resin is mature.

## The contradiction log: one fungus or a community?

**Direct answer:** The older literature names *Phaeoacremonium parasitica* as the agarwood fungus. Newer work describes a mixed bacterial and fungal community. The contradiction is real, and it matters for how induction is sold.

The single-pathogen story is attractive because it is simple: infect the tree with the right organism, get resin. The community story is messier: different sites, species, and wound types host different assemblages, and the resin response may depend on the consortium rather than one member.

A 2024 review in *Stress Biology* examined bacterial and fungal communities in *A. sinensis* and argued that both contribute to formation and quality. That does not disprove a role for *P. parasitica*; it changes the unit of analysis from "the pathogen" to "the microbiome."

For buyers, the practical consequence is scepticism toward any product that claims a proprietary "single-strain" technology without explaining what it does to resin chemistry. For growers, it means that inoculation is an ecological intervention, not a vaccine.

## Wild versus plantation: the part the labels leave out

**Direct answer:** Wild agarwood is defined by origin and scarcity; plantation agarwood is defined by method and scale. Neither word is a quality grade, and the premium attached to "wild" is only partly about smell.

The wild premium has three components:

1. **Scarcity.** Wild resinous trees are rare, and legal harvest is restricted across most range states.
2. **Maturation.** Wild material is often older, and older resin has had more time to concentrate and oxidise.
3. **Story.** A verifiable forest origin is itself a luxury good.

Plantation supply has improved, but it has a different profile. Trees are younger, induction is deliberate, and the resin distribution is often more uniform but less complex. Whether that matters depends on use: a consistent plantation chip can be excellent for blending, while a collector may value the irregularity of wild material.

The mistake is to treat "plantation" as an insult and "wild" as a certificate. Both are origin claims. Only inspection and paperwork can support them.

## CITES and the species question

**Direct answer:** *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* are listed in CITES Appendix II. The listing covers all species in those genera, so a shipment's legality depends on permits and provenance, not on the marketing name.

Appendix II is often misunderstood. It does not ban trade. It requires that exports be legal and sustainable, and it uses a permit system to make that claim auditable. For a buyer, the relevant questions are:

- Which species is declared?
- Which country issued the export permit?
- Does the permit cover the form of material — chips, powder, oil, or finished product?
- Can the seller connect the permit to this batch?

A "CITES-certified" badge on a website answers none of those. A permit number and a matching shipment description answer most of them. The [buying guide for incense manufacturing](/blog/how-to-buy-agarwood-for-incense-manufacturing/) applies the same logic to bulk lots.

## What this means for a buyer

**Direct answer:** Formation and species give you a set of questions that are answerable before you smell anything. Use them to filter suppliers, then use the physical checks to filter material.

A short sequence:

1. **Ask for species.** If the answer is "agarwood," ask again. A supplier who cannot name the species is not tracing the supply chain.
2. **Ask for the formation type.** 生结 or 熟结, wild or plantation. Listen for a specific answer rather than a mood.
3. **Ask for the year and region.** Resin maturation is time-dependent; a harvest year is a basic fact.
4. **Ask what was done after harvest.** Drying, storing, and any treatment. This is where oil loading and dyeing enter.
5. **Then inspect.** Use the [authentication ladder](/agarwood-sandalwood/) — story, surface, water, heat, cut.

The sequence is deliberately boring. It filters out the suppliers who are selling a word, and it leaves the ones who are selling a material.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** The "one fungus makes agarwood" story has done more harm than good. It turned a complex ecological process into a product feature, and it made a whole category of plantation wood sound fake when the real problem is immature resin and dishonest labelling.

**Reasoning:** First, the microbiological evidence has moved toward communities, not a single pathogen, and a claim that names one organism as the whole mechanism is behind the literature. Second, the single-pathogen frame encourages buyers to accept "inoculated" as a quality claim, when inoculation is only the start of a multi-year process. Third, the wild premium is real but poorly verified; the formation clock gives a buyer better questions than the wild/plantation binary.

**Disclosure:** This is an editorial reading of the research, not a laboratory conclusion. The science of agarwood induction is still developing, and a future study could revive a larger role for a single taxon.

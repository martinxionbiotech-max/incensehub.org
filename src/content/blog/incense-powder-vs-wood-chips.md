**Direct answer:** Two products sit on the same shelf under different names. One is aromatic wood that has been through a mill and come out as grains; the other is aromatic wood that has been sawn or split and left in pieces. The first is built to be combined with other materials and formed. The second is built to be heated on its own. Neither is a higher grade of the tree — they are two ways of presenting it.

**Key facts:** A mill turns one block into many small bodies, and every fresh face lets scent leave sooner. A piece keeps its inside closed, so warmth must work inward and the note arrives over time. Grains are bought against a written range (screen, water content, freedom from filler); pieces are bought against what shows on the surface (resin bands, grain, pale sapwood). Agarwood has its own inspection and adulteration problems, covered separately in [agarwood powder vs chips](/blog/agarwood-powder-vs-chips/); what follows applies to aromatic woods broadly.

## Key Takeaways

- The two products differ in geometry, not rank. One is milled stock; the other is cut stock.
- Milled stock is the working material of incense making (制香); cut stock is what the appreciation practice (品香) works with.
- Exposure is the mechanism: more faces open means faster, more even scent; fewer faces means slower, sequential scent.
- Grains are audited with measurements; pieces are audited with the eye and the nose before heat is applied.
- Milling erases the surface evidence that identifies a wood, which is why substitution is easier in grains and why a trial batch matters.

## Two Ways to Buy the Same Tree

**Direct answer:** Aromatic wood reaches a buyer by two routes — through a mill or through a saw — and the route determines what the buyer can check, how the material behaves, and what it costs to be wrong.

Take a single log of sandalwood. One buyer wants it for a production line that extrudes sticks, so the log goes to a mill, through a screen, and into bags of grains with a stated range. Another buyer wants to sit with the material and smell it, so the log is cut into shavings and lumps, sorted by size, and sold by the piece. Both buyers receive the same species from the same tree, and neither receives what the other bought.

The divergence starts early. Wood destined for milling is often the material that would be awkward to sell whole: offcuts, narrow branches, pieces with irregular grain. Wood destined for cutting is selected because it can be shown — a visible resin band, a clean surface, a size that fits a heater. That selection effect matters more than most buyers realise. It does not mean milled stock is inferior; it means the two channels begin with different selection criteria.

For resin, the same fork appears. Frankincense and myrrh arrive as tears, which are the lump form, or as ground material for compounding. A tear is a small object; a powder is an ingredient. The handling, the pricing and the check you can perform all change with that shift.

## A Worked Example: Two Buyers, One Log

**Direct answer:** Walking the same log through both channels shows why the two products diverge before either buyer opens a bag.

Imagine a sandalwood billet with a clean outer face, a knot at one end and a section of pale sapwood along one edge. The cutting buyer takes it to a bench, saws away the sapwood, splits the clean section into shavings, and grades the result by thickness. The knot end becomes scrap or a low grade. What remains is a small lot of pieces that show well: visible grain, a clean break, enough size to fill a heater cup.

The milling buyer takes the same billet — including the sapwood and the knot — and sends it through a mill. The output is a bag of grains with a stated screen range. The sapwood is still in there, averaged into the batch. So is the knot, unless someone sorted it out first. Nothing dishonest has happened: the specification describes the particle range, not the resin content, and the buyer knew that when they ordered.

The lesson is not that one channel is cleaner. It is that the two channels select for different things. Cutting selects for appearance because appearance is what the buyer can see. Milling selects for homogeneity because homogeneity is what a production line needs. A buyer who expects milled stock to carry the same evidence as cut stock is asking the mill to do something it cannot do.

When the same log contains both good and poor material, the cutting channel tends to skim the good part and the milling channel absorbs the rest. That is a structural fact of the trade, and it explains why a very cheap bag of grains and a very cheap bag of pieces are not equivalent risks. The grains hide more, so the discount has to be larger to be honest.

## What Changes When a Block Becomes Grains

**Direct answer:** Milling converts one object into thousands, and the conversion changes how fast scent leaves, how evenly a batch behaves, and how much of the material's identity survives.

Follow the first chain. A blade or hammer strikes the wood → the block fractures along weak planes → faces that were interior become exterior → the aromatic fraction now sits close to air on every particle → scent leaves quickly and across the whole mass at once. That is why a well-milled batch smells consistent: every spoonful has roughly the same exposure.

Follow the second chain, which is the miller's problem rather than the buyer's. Friction heats the material → volatile compounds near the surface evaporate during the process itself → the finished grains are slightly poorer than the wood that entered → the loss is invisible in the bag but present in the smell. A mill that runs hot produces a product that peaks early and fades fast. This is one reason a sample should be smelled before it is trusted.

Follow the third chain, which explains the failure mode. Fine particles pack closely → the gaps between them close → air cannot move through the bed → a smoulder either starves or, if the binder holds it open, races → the stick goes out or burns harsh. Coarse particles leave larger gaps → air moves freely → the burn runs hot and short. The mill's screen setting is therefore a formulation decision, not a cosmetic one.

None of this is visible in a photograph. It is visible in a specification and in a trial batch, which is why milled stock is a paperwork product as much as a sensory one.

## The Milling Chain, Stage by Stage

**Direct answer:** Milling is not a single act but a sequence — clean, dry, grind, screen, blend, store — and every stage changes the material in a way the buyer eventually smells.

**Cleaning.** Wood arrives with bark, grit and sometimes foreign matter. A mill removes what it can before grinding, because a stone in the hopper damages the screen and a piece of bark adds a note nobody ordered. The thoroughness of this stage is invisible in the finished bag, which is why a supplier's process description matters more than the adjective on the label.

**Drying.** Wood is hygroscopic, meaning it takes up and gives off water with the surrounding air. A mill dries to a target so the material grinds consistently and does not clog the equipment. Over-drying makes the grains brittle and prone to static; under-drying makes them clump. The target is a band, not a point, and the band depends on the mill and the product.

**Grinding.** A blade mill cuts fast and heats the material; a hammer mill shatters it and produces a wider spread; a burr mill is slower and more uniform. The choice shapes the particle distribution, and the distribution shapes how the product packs and burns. Heat is the hidden cost: friction can drive off the lightest aromatic compounds before the bag is sealed.

**Screening.** A screen sorts the output into bands. What passes is the product; what does not is either re-milled or sold as a coarser grade. This is where the mesh figure comes from, and why the same nominal figure can describe two very different distributions.

**Blending.** A mill may combine lots to hit a specification. Blending is normal and not a scandal, but it does mean the bag is an average. For a producer, that is the point. For a taster, it is the reason grains cannot answer a question about a single tree.

**Storing.** The finished grains wait in lined bins or sealed sacks. Time and air work on them from the moment the mill stops. A mill that turns stock quickly delivers a fresher product than one that holds a large inventory, and no specification captures that difference.

Read the sequence as a chain: cleaning removes what should not burn → drying fixes the water band → grinding sets the particle spread → screening separates the bands → blending averages the lots → storage starts the oxidation clock. Each stage is a decision, and the buyer inherits all of them in a single scoop.

## What a Whole Piece Preserves

**Direct answer:** Cutting keeps the architecture of the wood intact, and that architecture is what produces a sequence of notes rather than a single impression.

A piece has an outside and an inside. Heat arrives at the outside first, so the lighter compounds leave first; the interior stays cooler for a while, holding the heavier material back. The result is a session with a shape: an opening, a middle, a tail.

The piece also preserves evidence. Resin distribution follows the grain; sapwood sits where the tree put it; rot shows as soft or discoloured patches. A buyer can look at a piece and form a view before spending anything. Milled stock removes that view, and with it the cheapest check available.

There is a cost to the buyer in the other direction. A whole piece may be uneven — rich at one end, poor at the other — and the buyer carries that variance. Milled stock averages it out. For a production line, averaging is a feature. For someone tasting a single tree, averaging is the loss of the very thing they wanted to taste.

## The Buying Decision, in Order

**Direct answer:** Decide what you are doing before you decide what to buy; the form follows from the task, and the task is usually one of four.

| Task in front of you | Form to buy | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Extruding sticks, cones, coils or seals | Milled | A paste needs grains that distribute evenly and repeat |
| Sitting with one material | Cut | Sequence survives only in a whole piece |
| Auditing a supplier's claim | Cut | Surface evidence exists only before milling |
| Testing a production run | Milled, sampled | The trial must use what the line will use |

The order matters. Buyers often start with price, then discover the form does not fit the task. A home user who wants a pleasant evening buys cut stock, finds it needs a heater and a plate, and ends up with a hobby instead of a scent. A small producer who buys cut stock to save money discovers the milling step, the loss and the inconsistent particle range. Neither mistake is fatal, but both are avoidable by asking the first question first.

There is a legitimate middle: buy cut stock and mill it yourself, in small batches, when you want freshness and control. That route trades convenience for a closer relationship with the material, and it is covered below.

## Auditing Milled Stock

**Direct answer:** Milled stock is checked by specification and by trial, because the material no longer carries visible proof; the audit is a sequence of questions and one small batch.

Start with the questions a supplier should be able to answer without guessing. What species, and from which part of the tree? What screen range, and is it a tight band or a wide one? What water content, measured how? Is the material single-lot or blended? What is the declared shelf life, and what is it based on? A supplier who answers all five is selling a specification. One who answers none is selling a bag.

Then run the physical checks. Take a pinch and rub it: grit means a coarse fraction, slickness means too many fines. Let a sample fall through your fingers and watch whether it flows or clumps. Smell it at room temperature, then warm a small dish gently and smell again — the warm note should be richer, not acrid. Acrid at low heat suggests contamination or a scorched milling run.

Finally, run a pilot batch at the ratio you actually use. Note how much water the material takes, whether it forms cleanly, whether it cracks as it dries, and how it smells at the start, middle and end of the burn. A powder that forces you to change your process is telling you that the specification and your line disagree. That is information, not a defect.

Keep a retained sample of every lot you accept. It is the only way to prove, six months later, that the delivery changed. If you form sticks, the [makko binder guide](/blog/makko-natural-binders/) explains how binder choice meets the particle range.

## Auditing Cut Stock

**Direct answer:** Cut stock is checked with the eyes first and heat second; the surface carries evidence that the mill destroys.

Begin with size. A bag with a wide spread of thicknesses will heat unevenly, because a thin shaving and a thick lump do not reach the same temperature at the same moment. Ask for a graded lot if consistency matters, or sort it yourself if you are working in small quantities.

Then look at the wood. Resin shows as darker, denser bands following the grain, or as a mottled pattern in root material. Uniform pale wood is sapwood, which contributes little beyond a neutral woody note. Soft or crumbly patches indicate rot. A clean break should smell stronger inside than outside; if the broken face is mute, the piece is probably resin-poor.

Heat is the final check. Warm a sliver rather than a chunk, note how long the first scent takes, what it does at its peak, and what remains after the piece cools. A fast arrival and quick fade suggests a thin, surface-rich piece. A slow arrival with a long tail suggests density. Neither is automatically better; they suit different lengths of sitting.

## A Grading Heuristic for Pieces

**Direct answer:** A three-band reading — resin-rich, mixed, sapwood-heavy — is a faster first filter than a single glance at colour, and it is a working heuristic rather than an industry standard.

| Band | Surface evidence | What it promises |
|---|---|---|
| Resin-rich | Dense dark bands, weight for size, smell at room temperature | A long unwinding note; a little goes a long way |
| Mixed | Resin pockets against pale wood, moderate smell | A shorter sitting with a woody finish |
| Sapwood-heavy | Pale, light, even, faint | Little scent; keep it for practice |

Two pieces that look alike can behave differently, because resin content is not the only variable. Moisture and density matter, and so does where the piece sat in the tree. Treat the bands as a first filter, not a verdict. The judgment improves with a handful of sessions, which is the opposite of how a specification works — and that is the honest difference between buying grains and buying pieces.

## Handling: Dust, Water and Time

**Direct answer:** Milled stock is a dust-and-moisture problem; cut stock is a sorting-and-space problem; both are storage problems in the end.

Grains migrate. They coat surfaces, cling to screens, and become airborne when a container is opened. A workshop that handles them in volume needs extraction at the mill and at the filling point, a room where humidity is controlled, and respiratory protection for the people nearest the dust. Static makes fine material cling; over-drying makes it worse. Damp material bridges in hoppers and clogs a die, so the moisture target is a narrow band rather than a slogan.

Pieces are quieter but bulkier. They arrive in sacks, get tipped onto a table, and are sorted by hand. The losses are dust at the bottom of the bag and the temptation to mix grades to complete an order. Storage takes floor space, and a dry room keeps mould away.

Time treats the two differently. Grains have more surface in contact with air → oxidation proceeds faster → the brightest notes leave first → the material reads flatter and woodier within months. Pieces slow the same process because most of their aromatic content sits behind intact faces. Keep grains sealed, cool and dark in lots you will use; keep pieces in a lined tin and let them rest. Neither likes heat, sunlight or a shelf above a stove.

## Moisture: The Variable Nobody Tests

**Direct answer:** Water content affects both forms, but it is easier to notice in grains and easier to forgive in pieces, which is why it is the most under-checked specification in the trade.

Wood is not inert. It exchanges moisture with the air until it reaches an equilibrium, and that equilibrium shifts with the season and the room. A bag of grains that was correctly dried at the mill can pick up water in a humid warehouse and arrive clumped. A piece can do the same, but its interior stays drier longer, so the surface tells only part of the story.

For grains, moisture shows up as behaviour. Damp material bridges in a hopper, sticks to a screen, and forms a paste that takes more water than the recipe expects. Dry material throws dust, clings with static, and can crack as it dries further. Neither state is visible in a photograph, and neither is captured by the word "dry." A supplier who states a measured range, and the method used to measure it, is giving the buyer something to work with.

For pieces, moisture is mostly a storage question. A damp piece can grow mould in a closed container; a very dry piece can split along the grain as it re-equilibrates. The practical response is the same in both cases: keep the material in a stable, dry place, and let it settle before use rather than moving it between humid and dry rooms.

There is a sensory angle too. Water suppresses the perception of aroma, so a damp sample can read weaker than the same material after a day in a dry room. Judging a supplier on a sample that arrived in the rain is a mistake that has cost buyers good material.

## What Goes Wrong, and What It Implies

**Direct answer:** Each form fails in its own way, and the symptom usually points to a cause that is cheaper to fix than to replace.

| Failure | Fault sits in | First move |
|---|---|---|
| Stick dies out | Milled stock | Loosen the fill; check the binder before blaming the screen |
| Burn runs hot and rough | Milled stock | The air path is too open — tighten the screen and dampen slightly |
| Note disappears within weeks | Milled stock | Airtight tin, cooler spot, smaller purchases |
| A face scorches | Whole pieces | Lower the plate temperature, or use a thicker piece |
| Nothing happens for ten minutes | Whole pieces | Split the piece; sapwood will not open |
| Batch smells unlike the last | Either | Sort the lot; trial before committing |

Read the table as an order of checks, not a menu. Change one variable at a time. Add ash and swap the piece in the same session and you will learn nothing about either.

## Two Tests You Can Run Today

**Direct answer:** A break test and a warm test will tell you more about a piece than any label, and both cost nothing but a few minutes.

The break test: snap a small piece and smell the freshly exposed face. Interior aroma should be stronger and cleaner than the weathered outside. A break that smells of dry wood and nothing else is evidence of a resin-poor piece, whatever the surface promised.

The warm test: put a sliver on a heater at a moderate setting and time three moments — first scent, peak, and what remains after cooling. Write the three observations next to the size band. After a few pieces you will have a personal reference table, which is more useful than a generic grade because it matches your equipment and your nose.

The cold test: smell the piece at room temperature before it goes anywhere near heat. A good piece announces itself faintly but clearly; a poor one smells of dry wood or nothing. The cold reading is not a substitute for warming — some material only opens with heat — but it is a useful baseline, because a piece that smells of nothing cold rarely becomes generous warm.

The paired test: warm two pieces side by side, one you know and one you are judging. Comparison is easier than absolute judgment, because the nose is better at telling two things apart than at assigning a score. Keep the known piece as a reference across purchases and the exercise becomes repeatable.

For milled stock, the equivalent is a spoon test. Warm a small, thin layer in a dish, smell at thirty seconds and again at two minutes, and note whether the note turns acrid. A grain that scorches at low heat is telling you about its milling history or its purity, not about the species.

## A Procurement Checklist

**Direct answer:** A short written checklist turns a vague purchase into a comparable one, and it works for either form.

1. **Name the use.** Stick production, seal work, tasting, or resale. The form follows from the answer.
2. **Name the species and the part.** Trunk, root, branch, or mixed. The part explains much of the behaviour.
3. **Ask for the size specification.** A screen range for grains; a thickness band for pieces.
4. **Ask for the moisture figure and the method.** A number without a method is decoration.
5. **Ask whether the lot is single or blended.** Both are legitimate; the answer changes how you interpret variance.
6. **Request a sample and split it.** One part to smell, one to trial, one retained.
7. **Run the trial at your own ratio.** Not the supplier's recommendation — your process.
8. **Record the lot and the date.** The retained sample is your evidence if the next delivery differs.
9. **Agree on what happens if it fails.** Replacement, credit, or return. A supplier who will not discuss failure is telling you something.

The checklist is deliberately boring. Its value is that it makes two offers comparable on the same axes, so the decision stops being a choice between adjectives. Price enters last, after the specification is on the table, because a price without a specification is not a number you can compare.

## Packaging, Transit and What Arrives

**Direct answer:** The bag that reaches you is the end of a journey, and the form determines what the journey does to the material before you open it.

Grains settle. Vibration during transport compacts the fine fraction to the bottom of the sack, so the first scoop and the last scoop are not the same product. A supplier who blends before packing reduces the problem; one who fills straight from the mill does not. Static also matters: a dry plastic liner can hold a film of fine dust on its inner face, and that dust is the most aromatic part of the batch.

Pieces abrade. Every knock chips a corner, and the chips become dust that coats the rest of the lot. By the time a sack is opened, a visible fraction may be powder that the buyer did not order. Cushioning and a rigid outer box reduce it; a thin bag does not. If a delivery arrives with a thick layer of dust at the bottom, that is transport loss, not a bonus.

Moisture is the third variable. A sealed barrier bag protects against a humid warehouse, but only if it is intact. A paper sack breathes, which is fine in a dry climate and poor in a wet one. The useful question for a supplier is not "is it packed well" but "what barrier, and for how long." For a buyer, the practical move is to repack on arrival into containers that suit your own room, and to label the lot date.

There is a commercial consequence. Grains that compact and pieces that abrade both lose value in transit, and the loss is uneven across the bag. A buyer who samples only the top of a sack is sampling the part that travelled best.

## What the Form Does to the Air

**Direct answer:** Burning and smouldering both put material into the air, and the form changes how much and how fast — but no form makes indoor smoke a neutral presence.

A lit stick is a small combustion source: material oxidises, and fine particles plus gases leave with the smoke. A warmed piece sits below ignition, so the aromatic compounds evaporate rather than burn, and the particle load is far lower. That difference is real and is the strongest argument for the indirect method in a small room. It is not a guarantee of clean air. Charcoal under the plate still produces carbon monoxide, and a long session in a closed bedroom is a bad idea regardless of what the meter would say.

For the indoor-air side, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq) and the [American Lung Association](https://www.lung.org/clean-air/indoor-air/indoor-air-pollutants) are the starting points. The practical rules are short: ventilate, keep sessions finite, use a heat-safe base, and never leave a live ember unattended. The form decides how the scent arrives; it does not decide whether the air is safe.

## When to Mill Your Own

**Direct answer:** Milling at home makes sense when freshness and grading matter more than time, and it introduces two problems a factory manages for you: heat from the grinder and a much faster oxidation clock.

A blade grinder heats as it runs. Heat drives off the volatile compounds you are trying to keep, so the correct technique is to pulse in short bursts and let the material cool between them. A burr grinder produces less local heat but takes longer. Either way, smell the result: if the fresh grains smell toasted, the grinding was too aggressive.

Sieve what comes out. The coarse fraction is not waste — it can be warmed in a dish or returned for another pass. Keeping coarse and fine separate gives you two materials from one grind: fine grains for forming, a coarser meal for sprinkling.

Small batches matter more than any other habit. Grinding multiplies surface → the material meets air across a far larger area → oxidation accelerates → the brightest notes leave within weeks rather than months. Grind what you will use soon, keep the rest as pieces, and clean the grinder between materials so a resinous film does not carry into the next batch.

The economics are worth stating plainly. Home milling costs time and loses a little material to dust and retention. It buys freshness, control over the screen, and the ability to test a piece before committing it to powder. For a small maker, that trade is often worth it. For someone producing at volume, it is a distraction from the line.

## Ten Technical Words, Translated

**Direct answer:** Specification sheets use a small vocabulary that sounds forbidding and is not; knowing it lets you read a supplier's document without guessing.

**Hygroscopic** means the material borrows water from the air. **Granulometry** is the study of particle-size distribution — what a screen range describes. **Agglomeration** is clumping, the visible result of damp or static. **Flowability** is how readily material moves through a hopper or a sieve. **Desiccant** is a drying sachet. **Equilibration** is the wait while material settles to the humidity of its room. **Volatilisation** is the aromatic fraction turning to vapour; it is what you want in the session and what you lose in storage. **Adsorption** is vapour clinging to a surface, which is why a clean tin matters. **Friable** means crumbly, and describes over-dried grains. **Retention** is the material left behind in the mill, the sieve or the bag.

None of these words is a grade. They describe behaviour, and behaviour is what you can test. A supplier who uses them precisely is easier to audit than one who uses adjectives, because each term points to something measurable.

## The Trade's Vocabulary

**Direct answer:** Much of the confusion is lexical, because the trade uses separate words for the material, the product and the method, and English collapses them.

- **香粉 (xiāng fěn)** — aromatic grains, the working material for anything formed.
- **香材 (xiāng cái)** — raw aromatic stock, usually sold as pieces.
- **香炭 (xiāng tàn)** — the small dense charcoal under a warming plate.
- **香篆 (xiāng zhuàn)** — a seal pattern laid in grains; **打香篆** is the act of pressing and lighting it.
- **隔火 (gé huǒ)** — "separated fire," the indirect method.
- **制香 / 品香** — making versus appreciating, two practices that share a vocabulary but not a goal.

English adds ambiguity of its own. "Powder" can mean raw milled wood or a finished loose blend that already contains binder and fragrance. "Chips" can mean sandalwood offcuts, agarwood nuggets, or the dust at the bottom of a bag. When the difference matters, ask for the species and the size band rather than the category word.

## What a Retail Label Can and Cannot Tell You

**Direct answer:** A retail label is a starting filter, not a specification: it usually names the category and the weight, and almost never names the things that decide how the product behaves.

Most labels state the product type, the net weight, an ingredient list, a country of origin and a caution about burning. Some state a species. Very few state a screen range, a moisture figure, a lot number, a harvest year or a resin assessment. Those omissions are not necessarily evasive — retail packaging has limited space and a different audience — but they mean the label cannot answer the questions a serious buyer asks.

Certain phrases deserve a second look. "Natural" describes a source, not a purity level. "Agarwood" without a species or a region is a genus-level word that covers a wide range of material. "Handmade" describes a process, not a grade. "Premium" has no agreed meaning at all. None of these words is a lie on its own; each becomes misleading when it is the only information offered.

The chain runs like this: vague label → no comparable attributes → price becomes the only visible difference → the cheapest offer wins → suppliers who invest in grading lose the sale. That is how a category drifts away from specification and toward adjectives. The buyer's counter-move is simple and slightly awkward: ask for the technical sheet behind the label, and treat a refusal as data.

## The Cost of a Session, Not a Gram

**Direct answer:** Price per gram flatters the wrong form; the useful unit is the session, because a small resin-rich piece and a scoop of grains do not deliver the same number of sittings.

A whole piece is reusable in a way that grains are not. Warm a chip gently and it gives scent, cools, and can be warmed again; the aromatic fraction declines, but the piece is not consumed in one go. A stick made from grains is gone when it burns. Comparing the two by weight therefore compares a durable object with a consumable one.

The chain that follows: higher resin content → a smaller piece reaches the same intensity → more sessions from the same gram → a higher price per gram can still be the lower cost per session. The inverse also holds: a pale sapwood piece at a modest price can be the most expensive session in the drawer, because it delivers almost nothing.

This does not mean expensive material always wins. A costly piece that is mostly surface resin fades in one sitting, and a well-graded bag of grains can be extremely efficient for someone producing sticks. The point is to measure the right thing. Count sittings per lot, note the size used, and the comparison becomes real instead of rhetorical.

There is a practical boundary: session counting only works if you control the heat. A piece scorched on a hot plate is consumed as surely as a stick. The form sets the potential; the method decides whether you realise it.

## An Author's Position

**Position:** The question is not which form is better but which form lets you check the claim you are paying for, and the answer changes with the buyer's position.

**Reasoning:**

- A home user who wants atmosphere should buy a finished product and ignore the form debate; the craft is not the point of the purchase.
- A small producer should buy grains against a written specification because the line, not the eye, will find the defects.
- A taster should buy pieces because structure is the information, and milling removes it.

**A boundary worth keeping:** "whole pieces are more authentic" does not mean grains are inferior. It means the two forms conceal different things. A piece hides its interior until heat reaches it; grains hide their identity as soon as the mill is done. The buyer's job is to know which concealment they can see through.

*This is the author's editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## FAQ

### Q: Are the two products the same material?

In the ordinary case, yes. Both are aromatic wood — sandalwood, agarwood or another species — and the difference is preparation. One is milled and screened; the other is sawn or split and left in pieces. A single log can supply both, and often does: the showable pieces become cut stock and the rest goes to the mill. Resins follow the same pattern, sold as tears or as ground material.

### Q: Does one form last longer than the other?

A stopwatch comparison does not work, because the two are used differently. Milled stock is normally formed into a stick, cone or seal, and its duration depends on shape, density and recipe. Cut stock is warmed rather than burned, and one piece can keep giving scent for a long time because heat reaches the interior slowly. Species, size and heat source decide duration; the form only sets the pattern.

### Q: Is milled stock a lower grade?

No. Milling is a processing choice, not a rank. A resin-rich log milled with care produces excellent grains, and a poor piece can be mostly sapwood. The confusion arises because grains are easier to extend with filler, so a bad bag is harder to spot than a bad piece. That is a verification problem, and it is solved with a specification and a trial batch.

### Q: Can grains be warmed like a piece?

With limits. A thin, even layer in a small dish on a heater will release scent, but the material touching the hot surface tends to scorch before the rest has warmed, so the note turns acrid early. A lower setting and a thinner layer reduce the problem. Most people who want the warmed experience are better served by a piece, because the whole object heats through without a hot contact face.

### Q: Why do grains smell weaker than pieces from the same supplier?

Two likely reasons. Grains have more surface exposed to air, so the brightest notes leave faster in storage; a bag opened months ago will read flatter than a freshly cut piece. Milling also averages resin across the batch, so a mixed log yields an average rather than the best pocket. Compare a freshly opened lot before concluding that the material itself is weak.

### Q: How should each form be stored?

Grains behave like a ground spice: sealed, cool, dark, and bought in lots small enough to use within a reasonable period. Pieces are more forgiving because most of their aromatic content sits behind intact faces, so a lined tin in a cupboard is adequate. Neither benefits from heat, direct sun or a humid kitchen. Store them separately so the faster decline of the grains is not blamed on the pieces.

## Summary

- Milled stock and cut stock are the same tree prepared two ways; the form follows the task, not a quality order.
- A mill opens the wood to air, so scent leaves sooner and a batch can be made uniform; a whole piece keeps its inside closed and gives the note over time.
- Grains are audited by specification and a pilot batch; pieces are audited by surface evidence and a warm test.
- Substitution hides more easily in grains because milling destroys the visual cues that identify a wood.
- Storage clocks differ: grains oxidise quickly and want sealed, cool, dark, small lots; pieces fade slowly and tolerate ordinary storage.
- Both forms add material to indoor air when heated; ventilation and a heat-safe base apply either way.

> **Direct answer:** Middle Eastern incense is a family of practices built on two materials — resin-rich agarwood, sold as *oud*, and *bakhoor*, wood chips that have been compounded with oils and resins before they ever reach the buyer — heated as pieces on a burner rather than lit as a stick. The setting is the home: a reception room scented before guests arrive, a wedding, a holiday morning, a shop doorway. What separates a memorable session from a disappointing one is rarely the prestige of the material. It is how the chip was prepared, how much heat reaches it, and how the room is ventilated.

## Key facts

- **Two goods, one shelf.** *Oud* is raw, resin-loaded *Aquilaria* wood, warmed as chips or distilled to oil. *Bakhoor* (بخور) is a prepared product: wood soaked in fragrant oil and resin powder, cured, dried, and graded by size before it is sold.
- **The rest of the vocabulary is furniture.** *Luban* names frankincense, the bright resin that sits opposite oud in the regional palette. *Mabkhara* (مبخرة) names the burner, and the word serves for a clay bowl, a brass stand, and a modern electric unit alike.
- **Bakhoor is manufactured, not harvested.** Sorted agarwood offcuts → soak in fragrant oil and resin → cure until the core is stable → dry to a workable moisture → grade by size → pack.
- **Heat delivery sets the aroma curve.** A dense piece on a hot surface releases its outer layer first and its core minutes later, so the plume moves from top note to resin to char across a session.
- **Four regional buying patterns share the materials, not the habits.** Gulf volume and grade in the *majlis*, Levantine church frankincense beside family bakhoor, North African pressed pastes and blocks, South Asian benzoin (*sambrani*) and *loban*.
- **Charcoal and electric elements are different tools.** A coal runs above the aromatic range and adds a char note; an element sits closer to that range and gives a cleaner, narrower band of scent for longer.
- **Storage and smoke belong to the method.** Heat, humidity, light, and air migrate the oils and let the wood take moisture — a tacky chip can sometimes be aired, a mouldy one is discarded — and any indoor burn needs ventilation, a heatproof base, and an attendant.

## Key Takeaways

**Direct answer:** Almost every practical question about this tradition resolves into two variables — what the chip contains, and how the heat is delivered to it.

- The working format is a piece: a chip, a resin tear, or a pressed block sitting on heat. Sticks exist in the region, but they are not the centre of the practice.
- Bakhoor is a manufactured good; oud is an agricultural one. The first is compounded to a recipe, the second is graded after harvest.
- Regional markets buy on different criteria. The Gulf buys volume and status, the Levant buys resin for church and household use, North Africa buys pressed and paste forms, South Asia buys benzoin and loban.
- Heat delivery shapes the aroma curve more than the price tag on the chip does.
- Smoke is both the point and the hazard. Ventilation, a stable heatproof base, and an attended burner are part of the method, not optional extras.
- Nothing on this page is a health claim. Where a practice carries a traditional meaning, it is labelled as traditional belief.

## Two Materials, One Ritual

**Direct answer:** Oud is a substance; bakhoor is a product made from a substance. Confusing the two is the most expensive mistake a new buyer makes, because the words are used interchangeably in shops and almost never interchangeably in price.

Agarwood begins as ordinary *Aquilaria* wood. When the tree is wounded — by insects, weather, or a fungal response that follows injury — it lays down a dark, aromatic resin as a defence. That resin is what the trade grades, and the [agarwood guide](/blog/agarwood-incense/) covers the botanical side, 奇楠 (kinam), and the "sinking fragrance" scale in detail. Nothing about that process is a recipe. A grower cannot dial resin content up or down; harvesters find it, cut around it, and sort what they get.

Bakhoor starts where that sorting ends. Chips too small, too pale, or too uneven for the display tray get soaked in oil, dusted with resin powder, sometimes sweetened, and dried back down into a consistent, saleable piece. The chain runs like this:

**Sorted agarwood offcuts → soak in fragrant oil and resin → cure until the core is stable → dry to a workable moisture → grade by size → pack.**

Two consequences follow. Bakhoor is repeatable in a way raw oud is not, which is what a hotel or a gift brand needs. And the word "oud" on a bakhoor box describes a scent direction rather than a guaranteed material content — the label answers a different question than the one most shoppers are asking.

## What Bakhoor Actually Is

**Direct answer:** Bakhoor is wood that has been loaded, cured, and dried before sale, so a single piece releases several layers at once instead of the flat smoke of plain timber.

**Base selection.** The cheapest bakhoor uses light, porous wood with little scent of its own; the wood is a scaffold for the oil. Better grades use agarwood that already carries resin, so the added fragrance sits on top of a real base note. Between those two poles sits most of the market, and the base is the first thing a buyer should ask about, because no amount of oil turns a scentless scaffold into oud.

**Infusion.** Oils go on warm or at room temperature depending on the maker's method. Rose, jasmine, sandalwood, and amber accords are common; resin powders such as frankincense or benzoin add body; honey or sugar syrup is used in some traditions to bind and to round the top note. The liquid has to reach the end grain, not just wet the surface.

**Curing.** This is the stage that separates careful work from fast work. A chip that is packed and shipped while its centre is still wet will sweat inside the bag, grow a musty note, and burn unevenly. Curing lets the oil distribute through the pores and lets excess solvent or water leave.

**Drying and grading.** Final moisture is a compromise. Bone-dry chips crumble and scorch quickly; damp chips sputter and steam. Makers aim for a piece that feels dry to the touch, breaks with a short snap rather than a bend, and holds its shape in the bag.

| Preparation variable | What it changes | Failure mode when wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Base wood density | How long the piece holds heat and how much base note it contributes | Light wood burns through in minutes and leaves only oil smoke |
| Oil load | Intensity at first heat, and how much of the session is top note | Overloaded chips flash off fast and go flat |
| Sugar or syrup | Binding, sweetness, and how the piece browns | Too much caramelises into a bitter, scorched edge |
| Curing time | Evenness of the scent through the chip | Wet cores turn musty in the bag |
| Chip size | Heat penetration and session length | Oversized pieces stay raw in the middle; dust smokes harshly |
| Final moisture | How cleanly the piece heats | Damp chips hiss, steam, and produce acrid smoke |

*Editorial table — a diagnostic aid built from trade practice, not a specification.*

## Why the Format Is Chips

**Direct answer:** A chip holds heat in its mass, releases fragrance slowly from the inside out, and keeps producing smoke for as long as the ember or element stays hot — which is precisely what a room-scenting ritual needs.

The mechanism explains most troubleshooting:

**Dense piece on a hot surface → heat travels inward slowly → the outer layer volatilises first → the core catches up minutes later → the plume shifts from top note to resin to char.**

That gradient is why a good bakhoor session changes character over twenty or thirty minutes instead of sitting at one level. It is also why a cheap, thin chip and an expensive, dense one behave so differently at the same heat: the thin one crosses its whole gradient in a couple of minutes and then scorches. A stick is engineered the other way, for a fixed rate and a known finish — a virtue for a private daily burn, a limitation for a room that is meant to announce itself and then settle.

## Four Regional Buying Patterns

**Direct answer:** The Gulf, the Levant, North Africa, and South Asia share materials but not habits — they differ in the format they reach for, the volume they burn, and the criteria they use to judge a batch.

**The Gulf.** Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman carry the most visible version of the practice. The reception room — *majlis*, and in Kuwait the *diwaniya* — is the stage. Sessions are generous: a host may burn repeatedly through an evening, and the scent is expected to reach fabric, curtains, and the guests' clothing. Buyers in this market tend to be informed about grade and are quick to notice a chip that smells of oil rather than wood. *Oud muattar* — agarwood chips that have been soaked in oil and cured — sits at the prestige end, with plain chips and blended bakhoor below it. Electric burners have moved into many homes alongside the traditional *mabkhara*, largely because they suit an indoor room with air conditioning and closed windows.

**The Levant.** Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, and Palestine use incense in two registers that rarely meet in the same room: church and home. Frankincense dominates the ecclesiastical side, where the resin is burned in quantity on charcoal in a censer and the smoke is part of the liturgy. Household use leans toward bakhoor for weddings and family gatherings, with less of the oud-grading culture that shapes Gulf buying. A supplier selling into this market is really selling two different products to two different buyers.

**North Africa.** Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Egypt show a wider spread of forms. Pressed pastes and solid blocks appear alongside loose chips, and the household practice is often tied to weddings, births, and seasonal occasions rather than to a nightly reception-room habit. Woodchip-based bakhoor competes with resin and paste products, and the buyer's question is usually about the occasion rather than the grade.

**South Asia.** India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh import agarwood for attar and for burning, and they burn their own regional resins in parallel. Benzoin — sold in southern India as sambrani — and *loban*, the local name for frankincense, are everyday household smokes. The Gulf-influenced bakhoor trade is strong in the cities, but the older habit is resin on a coal or in a small cup, and it is judged on cleanliness of smoke rather than on perfume style.

| Market | Dominant forms | What buyers check first | Typical session scale | Seasonal pressure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gulf | Oud chips, oud muattar, blended bakhoor, electric burners | Grade and origin of the wood; whether the oil matches the wood | Long, repeated through an evening | Ramadan, Eid, wedding season |
| Levant | Frankincense for church, bakhoor for family events | Resin cleanliness; smoke volume in a large space | Burst use around ceremonies | Feast days, weddings |
| North Africa | Pressed pastes, blocks, loose chips | Occasion suitability; how the piece behaves on charcoal | Event-driven | Wedding season, new births |
| South Asia | Benzoin, loban, imported agarwood, cup-style burns | Clean smoke; price per household burn | Short daily burns | Festival calendar |

*Editorial generalisation — practice varies by city, family, and generation, and the table describes buying patterns rather than rules.*

## Formats on the Market and What Each Demands

**Direct answer:** Every format in this tradition is a different answer to the same question — how to get aromatic volatiles into a room — and each one makes different demands on the material.

**Loose chips.** The default. Sold by weight, often in a small pouch or a decorated box. They demand a heat source with a stable surface and enough thermal mass to keep working; a thin metal dish over a tea light will not do it. Chips reward patience and punish haste.

**Oud muattar.** Agarwood chips that have been soaked in oud oil or an oil blend and cured. Denser, oilier, and more expensive. The demand here is restraint: too much heat drives the oil off in a sharp opening and leaves charred wood behind. These pieces want a gentler element or a partially banked coal.

**Pressed blocks and cones.** Binder-heavy forms that hold shape and burn with a steady, compact plume. They are convenient and travel well, and they are the form most likely to hide a weak base, because the binder and the surface fragrance carry the performance. Ask what the block is bound with.

**The mabkhara.** The traditional vessel: a clay or brass bowl on a stand, sometimes with a pierced lid, sometimes open. It demands a heatproof base under it, clearance from fabric, and a room with somewhere for the smoke to go. Brass holds heat and stays hot long after the coal has died, which is a safety point as much as a performance one.

**Electric burners.** A controlled element — coil, plate, or mica — under a small dish. They demand a stable surface and a dish that can take repeated heating, and they reward small chip sizes, because the element delivers heat more evenly than a coal but with less peak intensity. The trade-off is covered in the heat section below.

**Cup-style burns.** A small quantity of resin or powder in a cup over a coal, common in South Asian household use. It demands very little material and gives a short, clean burn, which makes it the cheapest way to test a new resin.

**Sticks.** Present in the region, especially in modern retail, but not the backbone of the practice.

| Format | Heat demand | Material demand | Best room | Weak point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loose chips | Sustained medium heat with mass | Dense, resin-bearing wood | Reception room, hallway | Uneven pieces scorch at the edges |
| Oud muattar | Low to medium, controlled | High-grade agarwood plus oil | Small to medium room | Oil flashes off if overheated |
| Pressed block or cone | Medium, steady | Good binder and honest base | Any room with airflow | Binder can mask a weak base |
| Mabkhara with charcoal | High peak, long hold | Anything that tolerates fast heat | Large room, open plan | Heat lingers after the burn ends |
| Electric burner | Low, even, adjustable | Small chips, thin pieces | Apartment, office, bedroom | Never reaches charcoal intensity |
| Cup-style resin burn | Medium, short | Clean resin tears | Kitchen, doorway, small room | Short duration |

## Matching Format to Occasion and Room

**Direct answer:** Choose the format from the room and the moment first, then choose the material to fit the format — not the other way around.

The reasoning is simple. A large, airy *majlis* with guests coming and going will absorb a small electric-burner plume within minutes. A bedroom with the door shut will be overwhelmed by a full charcoal session and will hold the smoke in the curtains for days. The room sets the ceiling on how much smoke is useful; the format sets how much you can deliver.

| Situation | Format | Heat | Material | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Guests arriving within the hour | Loose chips on charcoal in a mabkhara | High | Mid-grade chips with good resin | Fast, visible plume that reaches fabric before people sit down |
| Long evening with family | Electric burner, low setting | Low and steady | Small chips or muattar | Keeps a background level without turning the room hazy |
| Wedding or large celebration | Charcoal with repeated reloads | High | Blended bakhoor by weight | Volume matters more than nuance at that scale |
| Small apartment, one room | Electric burner, smallest dish | Low | Two or three small chips | Controllable, and easy to stop when the room is scented |
| Testing a new supplier | Cup-style burn or a single chip | Medium | A representative sample | Cheapest way to judge cleanliness before committing |
| Gift or display | Sealed pouch or decorated box | Not applicable | Mid to high grade | Presentation and shelf life carry the purchase |

Two boundaries are worth stating plainly. A high price does not mean a chip should be burned hot — the most expensive muattar is usually the one that suffers most from careless heat. And a large room does not require the strongest material; it requires enough pieces at the right heat, which is a quantity question, not a grade question.

## Sugar, Oil, and Moisture: How Composition Changes the Burn

**Direct answer:** Sugar browns, oil flashes, and water boils — three reactions that run at different temperatures, and the order in which they happen on a hot chip is what a nose reads as the opening, the heart, and the tail of a session.

Start with water. Any moisture left in the wood has to leave before the aromatic compounds can volatilise cleanly. A damp chip therefore spends its first minutes steaming, and steam carries partially combusted material upward with it. The result is a hiss, a grey plume, and a sharp, sour edge that people often blame on the oil.

**Damp chip on heat → water turns to steam → steam lifts half-burnt material → the opening smells sour and thin → the good middle arrives late and shorter.**

Oil behaves the other way around. A heavily loaded chip gives an immediate, generous opening because the surface oil is the first thing to meet heat. That opening is impressive and brief. Once the surface film is gone, the piece has to rely on what soaked into the pores, and an over-oiled chip often has less in the pores than it appeared to.

Sugar and syrups sit between the two. They bind the oil, add a soft sweetness, and hold moisture — which is useful in a dry climate and unhelpful in a humid one. Heated past the point where they caramelise, they turn bitter and produce the acrid, scorched note that ruins a session. A traditional sweetened chip is a balance, not a recipe for more sweetness.

**Excess syrup → surface caramelises before the core is hot → bitter edge forms early → the resin note underneath never gets a clean run.**

This is why two boxes labelled with the same scent name can behave completely differently: the difference is not the perfume, it is the loading and the drying. When a batch scorches at low heat, suspect residual moisture or too much syrup before blaming the coal.

*Editorial reasoning from material behaviour; individual recipes vary and are not published by makers.*

## Charcoal, Electric, and the Temperature Question

**Direct answer:** Charcoal delivers a hot, fast, smoky session that fills a room and carries a char note; an electric element delivers a cooler, slower, cleaner session that never reaches the same volume.

| Signal | Charcoal disc | Electric burner |
|---|---|---|
| Heat source | A smouldering coal with a hot core | A controlled element, often adjustable |
| Time to full plume | A few minutes | Five to fifteen minutes |
| Peak intensity | High | Moderate |
| Smoke density | Heavy, room-filling | Light to moderate |
| Char and acrid edge | Present, and part of the traditional profile | Largely absent unless the element is turned up |
| Control | Coarse — you move the chip or bank the coal | Fine — you turn a dial |
| Residue | Ash, and a hot vessel to handle | Oil film on the dish |
| Best use | Hospitality, large rooms, ceremony | Apartments, offices, long low sessions |

The underlying difference is thermal. A coal runs far above the temperature at which aromatic compounds volatilise, so the chip closest to it is always being pushed past its fragrant range toward char. That is not a defect in the traditional method; the char note is part of what people recognise as the smell of a scented home. An element run at a moderate setting sits closer to the volatilisation range, so the same chip gives a cleaner, narrower band of scent for longer.

**Coal hotter than the aromatic range → outer layer chars while the core is still warming → smoke carries both resin and burnt wood → the session reads as dense and traditional rather than clean.**

The practical consequences are worth naming. Anyone burning on charcoal indoors needs airflow that actually removes smoke, not a window cracked for appearance. Anyone using an electric burner needs to accept that a large room will not fill, and should size the material and the dish accordingly. Neither method is a shortcut past the basic rule: heat the piece, do not incinerate it.

## Keeping Bakhoor Alive in a Hot, Humid Climate

**Direct answer:** Heat, humidity, light, and air are the four enemies, and in a Gulf or South Asian summer all four are present at once — so storage is a material decision, not housekeeping.

Oils on a chip are volatile by definition; that is why they smell. Left in a warm room, they migrate to the surface, oxidise, and turn from bright to dull or faintly rancid. Wood is hygroscopic: it takes moisture from the air, swells slightly, and gives mould a place to start. Sunlight accelerates both processes.

**Warm, humid air → wood absorbs moisture → oil film dilutes and migrates → mould and oxidation begin → the chip smells dusty or sour before it ever meets heat.**

What works, in practice:

- **Glass with a good seal** beats thin plastic, which is permeable enough to let aroma escape and to pick up the scent of whatever sits beside it.
- **Smaller containers, filled fuller** leave less headspace for oxygen and for moisture to cycle through.
- **A desiccant sachet** in the storage box helps in a humid climate, provided it is not touching the chips directly.
- **A dark cupboard** away from a kitchen wall or a window does more than an expensive jar in the sun.
- **No refrigerator.** Condensation on a cold chip is worse than the warmth you were avoiding; bring the container to room temperature before opening if it has been somewhere cool.
- **Separate the grades.** A strong muattar will scent a mild chip stored beside it within weeks, and the exchange is one-way in practice.

Signs that a batch has turned: the chip feels tacky when it should be dry, the surface has gone dull where it was glossy, the smell in the jar is dusty or flat rather than sweet, or a white bloom appears on the wood. A tacky chip can sometimes be rescued by airing it in a dry room for a day; a mouldy one should be discarded.

How long a batch keeps depends on the oil load, the climate, and the seal — makers rarely publish a shelf life, and the honest answer is that the buyer's storage decides it. Buy in quantities you will burn within a season rather than stockpiling a prestige grade in a hot flat.

## What Commercial Buyers Screen For

**Direct answer:** A hotel, a retailer, and a gift brand are buying three different things from the same shelf, and a batch that delights one will fail another.

**Hotels and hospitality.** The brief is consistency and containment. A lobby or a corridor needs the same scent at the same intensity on every shift, which rules out anything that depends on a skilled hand at a charcoal bowl. Electric units with a fixed chip dose are the usual answer. What fails here: a batch whose oil flashes off in ten minutes, leaving staff to reload; a chip that leaves heavy soot on a white ceiling; anything that requires an open flame in a public area; and any product whose scent reads differently in a cool lobby than it did in a warm sample room.

**Retail.** The brief is shelf life and predictability. A pouch that sits in a shop for eight months in summer must still smell right when the customer opens it at home. What fails here: packaging that is not sealed, chips that clump in the bag, and a scent that is only impressive in the first thirty seconds. Retail buyers also care about the story on the pack — base wood, oil, origin — because that story is what the customer reads while deciding.

**Gift and occasion.** The brief is presentation and a first impression. The recipient will burn one piece, in a room they did not choose, probably on an electric burner. What fails here: a chip that needs a charcoal setup the recipient does not own; a scent that is too heavy for a small apartment; and a box that gives no guidance on how much to use.

| Buyer type | Buys on | Fails when | What to demand before ordering |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hotel | Consistency, low soot, staff safety | Batch drift; reload frequency; open flame | Sealed reference sample, batch matching, residue test on a white surface |
| Retail | Shelf life, packaging, margin | Clumping, faded scent, unsealed pouches | Moisture at packing, seal integrity, a stored sample aged in heat |
| Gift | Presentation, first burn | Needs equipment the recipient lacks; overpowering dose | Instructions in the box, a small format, a scent that suits small rooms |

*Editorial framework for trade buyers; it describes failure modes rather than setting requirements.*

## Questions to Ask a Supplier

**Direct answer:** Six questions separate a described product from a known one, and a supplier who cannot answer them is telling you something useful.

1. **What species is the base wood?** Agarwood is *Aquilaria*; a blend of unnamed "incense wood" is a different product with a different cost structure.
2. **Is the oil natural, synthetic, or a blend?** The answer changes the price, the scent behaviour, and what the label may honestly claim.
3. **Is the chip sweetened, and with what?** Sugar and honey change burn behaviour in a humid climate and are worth knowing about before a container ships.
4. **What is the moisture at packing?** A number matters less than a method: ask how it is checked and whether it is checked per batch.
5. **How is a batch identified?** A code that ties a pouch to a production run is the difference between a complaint you can investigate and one you cannot.
6. **What does the residue look like after a full burn?** Ash that is fine and grey behaves differently from a sticky black film, and the film is what ruins a display surface.

A sample should be burned, not sniffed. Ask for enough material to run a full session on the heat source the end user will actually use — an electric element test tells you little about charcoal behaviour, and the reverse is equally true.

## Three Things Called "Oud"

**Direct answer:** Raw agarwood, prepared bakhoor, and a synthetic oud accord share a word and almost nothing else, and the practical skill is telling them apart before money changes hands.

The word does a lot of misleading work on packaging. A side-by-side is the fastest screening tool a buyer can carry:

| Signal | Oud (raw agarwood) | Bakhoor (prepared chips) | Synthetic "oud" |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Resin-impregnated *Aquilaria* wood | Wood chips soaked in oils and resins | A manufactured fragrance accord |
| How it is used | Warmed as chips, or distilled to oil | Heated on charcoal or an element | Often in inexpensive sticks or oils |
| Scent | Deep, sweet, resinous, layered by nature | Smoky and warm, layered by the maker | Sweet-woody but flat, without resin depth |
| Price signal | High, and priced by grade | Mid-range, driven by base wood and oil load | Low, sold on the name |
| How to check | Look for resin veins and ask for grade | Ask about base wood and added oils | Ask whether agarwood is present at all |
| Behaviour on heat | Changes over the session | Opens with oil, then settles to wood | Smells roughly the same from start to finish |

The lesson is a boundary rather than a rule: "oud" printed on a box does not mean agarwood is inside. In inexpensive goods the term describes a scent family. Confirming which of the three you are holding matters because the three carry different prices, different burn behaviour, and different expectations.

## Mistakes That Cost Money or Ruin a Session

**Direct answer:** Most failures are category errors or heat errors, and a short checklist catches both before money is spent.


1. **Treating "oud" and "bakhoor" as one thing.** One is a material; the other is a prepared product built on it, sometimes without it.
2. **Lighting bakhoor like a stick.** Chips go on heat. A flame applied directly gives you char and a wasted piece.
3. **Overheating an expensive chip.** The best material suffers most from a coal that is too hot; give muattar a gentler setting.
4. **Burning in a closed room with no airflow.** Smoke that cannot leave stains fabric and irritates eyes and airways.
5. **Storing in a warm cupboard in a humid month.** Oils migrate, wood takes moisture, and the batch turns before it is finished.
6. **Believing health claims.** The practice is cultural and aromatic. No medical benefit is established here, and none is claimed.

## The Author's Take

**Direct answer:** This section states an editorial position and the reasoning behind it, not a verified fact.


**Position:** The most useful thing to understand about this tradition is that it is a hospitality practice first and a fragrance category second — and that the format, not the price, is what most often decides whether a session works.

**Reasoning:**

- The oud-versus-bakhoor distinction is the one that costs people money. Raw chips and infused chips are different goods, and a shopper who does not know which is in the box cannot judge whether the price is fair.
- A great deal of inexpensive "oud" is a fragrance accord. That is not a scandal, but buying it believing it is agarwood is a misunderstanding the buyer pays for.
- The social purpose explains the whole shape of the practice. A room is being prepared for other people, so volume, duration, and the ability to scent fabric matter more than the refinement of a single note. A stick is designed for a private timed pause; a chip is designed for a shared space.
- Heat is the variable most people under-manage. Two identical chips on a coal and on a low element are almost different products, and learning one heat source well is worth more than upgrading the material.

## FAQ

**Direct answer:** Five questions cover the decisions readers arrive with most often — what they are buying, how to burn it, how it keeps, and whether it fits their room.


### Q: What is the difference between oud and bakhoor?

Oud is the raw material — resin-loaded *Aquilaria* wood, warmed as chips or distilled into oil. Bakhoor is a finished product built on top of a material like that: wood chips, often though not always agarwood, soaked or blended with oils, resins, and sometimes sugar or floral essences, then dried for burning. Most bakhoor contains some oud, but it has been compounded into a ready incense with a maker's scent profile. The two sit at different price points for different reasons, which is why the label matters when you are paying.

### Q: How do you burn bakhoor?

Two routes. The traditional one puts a lit charcoal disc into a heatproof burner and lays the chips on top so the smoke rises. The gentler one uses an electric burner, which warms the chips without a flame and releases the aroma slowly with less of the sharp char. Either way, heat drives the infused oils and resins out of the wood, and the piece is never lit directly. Keep the burner on a stable, heatproof surface, clear of fabric and anything flammable, and ventilate the room — this format produces a lot of smoke, and indoor smoke is a real respiratory irritant.

### Q: Does bakhoor go bad, and how would I know?

Yes, and the signs are physical before they are aromatic. A chip that has taken moisture feels tacky rather than dry, may clump in the pouch, and can show a pale bloom on the surface. Oils oxidise in warmth, so an old batch smells dusty or flat in the jar instead of sweet. Sunlight and heat speed both processes. A batch that has merely dried out can sometimes be revived by storing it with a little fresh oil; one that has gone mouldy should be thrown away, because burning mouldy wood puts spores and irritant smoke into the room.

### Q: Can I burn bakhoor in a small apartment?

You can, with a smaller format and a lower heat setting. Two or three small chips on an electric burner will scent a single room without leaving a haze; a full charcoal session in the same space will coat the walls and curtains and take days to clear. Run the extraction fan or open a window while burning and for a while afterward, keep the burner away from soft furnishings, and stop when the room holds the scent rather than waiting for visible smoke. Anyone with asthma or other respiratory sensitivity should be cautious with any smoke indoors, this one included.

### Q: Is Middle Eastern incense the same as agarwood incense?

Only partly. Oud — agarwood — is the prestige centre of the tradition, but the practice also draws in frankincense and prepared bakhoor blends, so the category is broader than agarwood alone. Frankincense (*luban*) and prepared mixes that pull in rose, jasmine, sandalwood, benzoin, and other notes sit alongside oud. The phrase describes a home-and-hospitality practice built on pieces of material heated on a surface, with oud as its most prized element rather than its only one. For the botanical and grading detail on agarwood, see the [agarwood guide](/blog/agarwood-incense/); for the frankincense side, the [frankincense guide](/blog/frankincense-incense/).

## Summary

**Direct answer:** Keep the three products distinct, match the format to the room, and manage heat and moisture — that is the practical core of the tradition.


- Middle Eastern incense is a home-and-hospitality practice built on pieces of material heated on a surface, not on timed sticks.
- Oud is raw agarwood; bakhoor is prepared, infused chips; synthetic "oud" is a fragrance accord — three different goods at three price levels.
- Bakhoor's layering comes from the base wood plus what was soaked into it, released in sequence as heat moves inward.
- Regional markets differ in format and criteria: Gulf volume and grade, Levantine church and family resin, North African pressed forms, South Asian benzoin and loban.
- Heat delivery, moisture, and sugar content decide how a session actually behaves, which is why storage and burner choice matter as much as the material.
- Smoke is a genuine indoor hazard; ventilation and an attended burner are part of the method.
- No health or spiritual claims are made here; the tradition is treated as cultural and aromatic.

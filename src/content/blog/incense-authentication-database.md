**Direct answer:** The bulk of incense counterfeits are pieces of wood that have been stewed in oil, force-injected with oil, or steeped in dye and chemicals, then resold under the agarwood or sandalwood name. The practical giveaways are burn behavior (pale smoke with oil seepage versus dark smoke), aroma on low heat (complex and sweet versus "perfume" or "charred wood"), and the float-or-sink density check — and none of them works on its own.

**Key facts:** This is an authentication reference assembled from traditional Chinese market heuristics, not a laboratory method. Definitive authentication means a reputable seller and, where the value justifies it, laboratory testing.

---## The Four Roads to a Fake

**Direct answer:** Three treatment methods dominate the counterfeit trade — oil-boiling (煮油), high-pressure oil injection (高压灌油), and chemical/dye soaking (泡药水) — and a fourth route simply swaps in an unrelated wood under a borrowed name.

| Method | Chinese | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Oil-boiling | 煮油 | Stews ordinary wood in oil to add weight and smell |
| High-pressure oil injection | 高压灌油 | Rams oil into non-resinous wood |
| Chemical/dye soaking | 泡药水 | Dyes and scents non-resinous "white wood" |
| Substitution | — | Unrelated woods (Lauraceae, Burseraceae) dressed with synthetic fragrance |

Each method aims at a different check, which is the key to reading them. Oil injection targets the float-or-sink test; dye soaking targets the color grade; substitution plus synthetic fragrance targets the aroma. A forger selects the method that defeats the check a buyer is most likely to trust — so leaning on one check is exactly what the fake is built to exploit.

---## The Detection Signals, as a Field Guide

**Direct answer:** Real agarwood and sandalwood tend to give pale smoke with a slight oil seepage and a complex, sweet aroma on low heat, while fakes tend to give dark smoke and a flat "perfume" or "charred wood" note — but every signal is directional, not decisive.

| Signal | Real (tends to) | Fake (tends to) |
|---|---|---|
| Burn smoke | Pale smoke with oil seepage | Dark smoke |
| Low-heat aroma | Complex, sweet, layered | "Perfume" or "charred wood" |
| Room-temperature aroma | 奇楠 is fragrant even unheated | Synthetic has a one-note smell |
| Density | Resin-dense agarwood sinks (沉水) | Oiled wood may sink but smells wrong |

The table reads as a set of tendencies, not guarantees, and that is the honest framing. A skilled counterfeit can defeat any single signal; the value of the table is that the signals point in *different* directions, so a genuine piece tends to pass several at once while a fake trips on at least one.

## The Float-or-Sink Check, and Its Two Faces

**Direct answer:** The sink test is a real density signal — 沉香 means "sinking fragrance," and resin-dense agarwood genuinely sinks while low-grade wood floats — but it is weak against agarwood fakes because oil injection exists specifically to pass it.

Work the mechanism through. Resin is denser than water → wood saturated with resin becomes denser than water → it sinks → the test correctly identifies high-resin density. But oil is also denser than water → a forger injects oil to mimic that density → the fake sinks too → the test now measures "heavy" rather than "resinous." That is why the sink test is a useful *rule-out* (a very light piece is not resin-dense agarwood) but a useless *rule-in* (a sinking piece may still be oiled). See the [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) for the full 沉水 system.

## The Honest Limits

**Direct answer:** These are accumulated traditional heuristics, not laboratory methods — a skilled counterfeit can defeat any single one, so the reliable route is a reputable seller, laboratory testing where it matters, and price as the strongest single signal.

1. Buy from a **reputable seller** who names the species and origin.
2. Use **laboratory testing** where the value justifies it.
3. Treat **"too cheap for the claimed grade"** as the strongest single signal.

The ordering is deliberate. The seller question is free and filters the majority of fakes before any burn test is needed; the laboratory route is the only one that actually establishes species and resin content; the price signal is the veto that catches what the other two miss.

## Confusion vs: Agarwood, Sandalwood, and Their Common Substitutes

**Direct answer:** Both great woods are heavily substituted, but for opposite reasons — agarwood for its extreme price, sandalwood for its slow-growing scarcity — and the two substitutions are told apart by what the fake is trying to imitate.

| Signal | Agarwood (沉香) | Sandalwood (檀香) |
|---|---|---|
| Why it is faked | Extreme top-grade price (奇楠) | True *Santalum album* is scarce and slow-growing |
| Common fake | Oil-injected / dyed wood | Other sandalwood species, pale woods, synthetic blends |
| Real burn signal | Pale smoke, oil seepage | Mild, sustained creamy-woody aroma |
| Real low-heat aroma | Deep, resinous, sweet | Creamy, soft, sweet-woody |
| Defense | Sink test + aroma + seller | Species confirmation + accepting the higher price |

The table shows why a single "how to spot a fake" list is insufficient: the two materials fail in different ways. Agarwood fakery is about density and resin imitation; sandalwood fakery is about species substitution. A buyer who memorizes "check the smoke" for agarwood and then applies it to sandalwood is checking the wrong thing.

## The Judgment Boundary

**Direct answer:** The burn and aroma signals are a useful backup, but they are decisive only when the seller can also name the species and origin — a good burn test does not certify authenticity, it only reduces the odds against it.

My position cuts against the grain of most "authenticity" lists: the strongest signal is not a test at all, it is the seller. A vendor who cannot name the species, the origin, and the grade is already giving you all the information you need, before any smoke is involved. The burn and aroma signals are the backup — "what exactly is this, and where is it from?" is the honest shortcut.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Trusting a single signal.** Every fake is engineered to pass the one check a buyer is most likely to trust.
2. **Using the sink test as proof.** It rules out light wood but cannot rule in genuine agarwood, because oil injection passes it.
3. **Applying one material's test to another.** Agarwood fakes are about density; sandalwood fakes are about species.
4. **Ignoring the seller.** The cheapest and strongest filter is whether the vendor can name species, origin, and grade.

## FAQ

### Q: How can I tell if agarwood is real?
Stack several signals instead of trusting one. Burned, genuine agarwood tends to throw pale smoke with a touch of oil seepage, while many fakes go dark. Warmed gently, real agarwood opens into something complex, sweet, and layered, whereas a fake often reads "perfume" or plain "charred wood." Density helps too: resin-rich agarwood sinks in water — the origin of the name 沉香, "sinking fragrance" — though that check is not decisive. The strongest signal is the seller: buy from someone who names the species, origin, and grade. See the [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/).

### Q: What is "oil-injected" agarwood?
Low-grade, non-resinous wood that has had oil forced into it under pressure to fake weight and a faint scent, so it sails through the density checks a buyer might run. The oil used is usually cheap, and the result smells thin or cosmetic — nothing like the layered sweetness of real resin-rich wood. Because the oil is pushed into the surface rather than formed naturally, the piece tends to smell wrong on gentle heat and burn with a different smoke character. It is one of the most common fakes for exactly that reason: it defeats the sink test, so always pair density with burn and aroma checks. See the [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/).

### Q: Is the sink test reliable?
As a density clue, yes; as proof, no. Resin-dense agarwood really does sink — the name 沉香, "sinking fragrance," comes from that — and lower-grade wood floats, so the test quickly clears obvious low-resin pieces. But oil-injection fakes are built to pass weight and density checks, and some dyed or treated woods sink too. A sinking piece can still be fake, and a floating piece is not automatically worthless. Use the sink test as one data point and always combine it with the burn smoke and the warmed aroma before deciding.

### Q: Why is fake sandalwood common?
True *Santalum album* is scarce and costly: the tree grows slowly, needs years to develop its heartwood oil, and has been heavily overharvested, so real material commands a high price. That price gap invites substitution — sellers pass off other sandalwood species, unrelated pale woods, or synthetic fragrance blends under the sandalwood name because the margin is large. Buyers who shop by name or low price end up with a copy. Confirming the species and origin, and accepting that real sandalwood costs more, is the practical defense. See the [sandalwood guide](/blog/sandalwood-incense/).

### Q: What is the difference between an agarwood fake and a sandalwood fake?
They fail for opposite reasons, and that is the point of treating them separately. Agarwood is faked by engineering *density and resin appearance* — oil injection and dye — because its top grade (奇楠) commands extreme prices. Sandalwood is faked by *swapping the species* — passing off a cheaper *Santalum* or an unrelated pale wood — because true *Santalum album* is slow-growing and scarce. So the right defense differs: for agarwood, you probe density plus aroma plus seller; for sandalwood, you confirm the species and accept that real material costs more. Memorizing one "spot the fake" checklist and applying it to both is the most common mistake. See the [sandalwood guide](/blog/sandalwood-incense/) and the [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/).

## The Life Cycle of an Oiled Fake, End to End

**Direct answer:** An oil-injected fake is not a single deception but a chain of small steps, and knowing the whole chain is what lets a buyer attack the weakest link instead of the strongest.

Start at the factory. A forger takes cheap, pale, nearly scentless wood → the wood is loaded into a pressure vessel with cheap oil → pressure drives the oil into the surface and shallow pores → the piece gains weight and a faint, flat scent → it is dried and sometimes dyed darker → it is sold as "agarwood" at a fraction of the real price. Each link answers a specific check: the added weight defeats the sink test, the dye defeats the color grade, the faint scent defeats a quick sniff. The chain has one weak point that survives all of it: gentle heat. Warmed, a real resin-rich piece opens into layered sweetness, while the injected oil reads thin and cosmetic and often smokes dark. The buyer who knows the chain stops testing the strong links and probes the one link the forger could not reinforce.

## A Verification Ladder: From Free to Definitive

**Direct answer:** Authentication is a ladder, and the honest order runs from the free and fast to the expensive and final — each rung rules out a class of fake, and no single rung is enough on its own.

| Rung | Check | What it rules out | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Seller names species, origin, grade | Blatant substitution and vague sourcing | Free |
| 2 | Price sanity — too cheap for the grade | Most oiled and dyed wood | Free |
| 3 | Warm gently and smell | Surface oil, synthetic perfume, charred wood | Time |
| 4 | Burn a sliver and watch the smoke | Dye and chemical soak | A small sample |
| 5 | Laboratory testing | Everything, definitively | Money |

The ladder's lesson is that the free rungs do most of the work. A seller who cannot name the species has already failed the first rung, and no burn test downstream can rescue the purchase. Run the ladder top to bottom, and stop only when the value of the piece justifies the next rung.

## The "Sinking Fragrance" Name Is a Trap, Not a Shortcut

**Direct answer:** The name 沉香, "sinking fragrance," encodes a density test that forgers long ago learned to pass — so a sinking chip is evidence of weight, never evidence of resin.

The name invites a shortcut that no longer works. Originally, resin-dense agarwood really did sink, and the name captured a genuine signal. Today the signal has been polluted, because oil is also heavier than water → a forger who injects oil manufactures the very density the name promises → the chip sinks → the test reports "heavy" while the buyer hears "resinous." The honest reading is asymmetric: a very light chip that floats is a strong rule-out (it is not resin-dense agarwood), while a sinking chip is a weak rule-in that must be confirmed by heat and aroma. Treat the name as a historical clue, not a current certificate. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## More Questions Answered

### Q: Does a darker color mean higher-grade agarwood?
Not by itself. Dark color is one of the easiest signals to fake, because dye and chemical soaks (泡药水) are cheap and aimed exactly at the color grade. Genuine resin darkens wood as it accumulates, so color can correlate with quality in a verified piece — but color alone is meaningless when a forger can stain pale wood overnight. Pair color with the other rungs: warm the piece and smell for layered sweetness, watch the burn smoke, and above all confirm the species and origin with the seller before trusting any single visual cue.

## Why Two Materials Need Two Different Playbooks

**Direct answer:** Agarwood and sandalwood are both heavily faked, but the fake aims at different signals in each case, so the verification playbook must change with the material.

For agarwood the target is density and resin appearance → the forger injects oil and dyes the surface → your defense is heat, aroma, and a seller who names the species. For sandalwood the target is species identity → the forger swaps in a cheaper *Santalum* or a pale unrelated wood and adds synthetic fragrance → your defense is species confirmation and accepting that real *Santalum album* costs more. Running the agarwood playbook on sandalwood means checking smoke and density on a material where the fraud is about which tree it came from — the wrong test for the wrong crime. Match the playbook to the material and the whole exercise gets shorter and sharper.

## The Price Signal, Read Correctly

**Direct answer:** Price is the strongest single veto in authentication, but only as a rule-out — a low price cannot prove a fake, it can only make a fake overwhelmingly likely.

The logic runs one way. A genuine 奇楠-grade agarwood is scarce and expensive → a piece offered at a bargain fraction of that price is almost certainly not that grade → the price alone tells you to walk away. The reverse never holds: a high price does not prove authenticity, because the whole point of the fraud is to capture the high price. So price is a veto, not a verdict. Use it to refuse the too-cheap piece instantly, then spend the deeper rungs of the ladder only on the pieces that survive the price test.

## Keeping the Heuristics Honest

**Direct answer:** Every signal on this page — pale smoke, layered aroma, the sink test — is a traditional market heuristic, accumulated from practice rather than from a laboratory, and it deserves to be labeled that way.

A heuristic is a rule of thumb that usually points the right way, not a measurement that always decides. A skilled forger can defeat any one of them, which is why the page stacks several and leans on the seller and, where the value justifies it, the laboratory. The honest limit is worth stating plainly: these checks reduce the odds of being fooled, and they do not certify a piece the way a chemical analysis would. Use them as the accumulated wisdom they are — good filters, not proof. Where the stakes are high, the lab is the only rung that closes the case.

## The Three Treatment Fakes, Ranked by What They Defeat

**Direct answer:** Each of the three treatment methods is aimed at a specific check, and ranking them by which check they defeat reveals why no single test can ever be trusted alone.

Oil-boiling (煮油) targets the scale → it adds weight so a light piece feels dense → it defeats a hand-weigh or a casual sink check. High-pressure injection (高压灌油) targets the density test → it rams oil in deep enough that the piece sinks → it defeats the sink test outright. Dye and chemical soaking (泡药水) targets the eye → it darkens pale wood and adds a surface smell → it defeats the color grade and a quick sniff. Each method is a single-purpose tool aimed at one check, which is why a forger picks the method that beats the check a buyer is most likely to trust. Stacking checks is the only way to beat a strategy that is itself a stack of one-trick countermeasures.

## How a Buyer Should Actually Sequence the Checks

**Direct answer:** The checks have a natural order, and getting the order wrong wastes effort — free filters first, sensory tests second, the laboratory last.

Start with the seller → a vendor who cannot name species, origin, and grade has failed a check that costs nothing and rules out the bluntest fakes. Then the price → a too-cheap-for-the-grade tag is a veto that costs nothing either. Then the senses → warm the piece and smell, then burn a sliver and watch the smoke, because these cost time and a small sample. Then, and only then, the laboratory → the sole rung that establishes species and resin content, reserved for pieces valuable enough to justify it. This ordering is not fussiness; it is the same logic as the verification ladder — spend the cheap filters before the dear ones.

## The Sink Test's Two Jobs, Kept Separate

**Direct answer:** The sink test does two different jobs that keep getting fused into one — a rule-out for light wood and a rule-in for resin — and fusing them is the error that lets oiled fakes through.

As a rule-out the test is strong → a piece that floats is plainly not resin-dense agarwood, so it clears obvious low-grade wood instantly. As a rule-in the test is weak → a sinking piece only proves the piece is heavy, and oil is heavy too → an oiled fake sinks just as well as a genuine resin-dense chip. The test's honesty lives in keeping the two jobs separate: use it to reject, never to accept. A buyer who says "it sinks, so it is real" has promoted a rule-out into a rule-in, and that is the exact promotion the forger is counting on. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## More Questions Answered

### Q: Can a fake ever pass a laboratory test?
Not if the test is the right one and the lab is competent, but that is the point — most fakes never reach a lab. The treatments on this page are engineered to fool the cheap checks a buyer runs at home, not a gas-chromatography or species-identification test. A laboratory that confirms species and resin content will separate a genuine *Aquilaria* piece from an oiled substitute every time, which is why the page reserves the lab for pieces valuable enough to justify the cost. The fakes survive on the gap between what a buyer checks and what a lab could check, not on any ability to fool the lab itself.

## The Difference Between a Heuristic and a Test

**Direct answer:** The checks on this page are heuristics, not tests, and the difference is load-bearing — a test gives a definitive answer, a heuristic only shifts the odds, and mixing the two up is how a buyer walks away overconfident.

A test answers a single question with a yes or no → a laboratory species identification says "this is or is not *Aquilaria*" → there is no ambiguity. A heuristic says "this usually signals that" → pale smoke usually signals genuine material, dark smoke usually signals a fake → but a skilled forger can flip either signal. The practical consequence: heuristics are for filtering, not for certifying. Use them to shrink the field of likely fakes, then route anything valuable to a real test. A buyer who treats a passed sink test as a passed lab test has promoted a rule of thumb into a certificate, and that promotion is the forger's entire business model.

## Why the Seller Question Comes First

**Direct answer:** The seller is the first and cheapest check because it tests the only thing every other check ultimately relies on — information, not material.

Every physical check assumes you already know what the piece claims to be → the sink test assumes agarwood, the smoke test assumes agarwood, the aroma test assumes agarwood. But the claim itself is exactly what a fake disputes. Asking the seller to name species, origin, and grade tests the claim at its source → a vendor who cannot answer has already failed, before any material is burned → a vendor who can answer gives you a claim you can then verify against the physical checks. The seller question is not a substitute for the material checks; it is the prerequisite that makes them meaningful. Skip it and you are testing a material against a guess.

## The Smoke Color, Explained by What Burns

**Direct answer:** The pale-versus-dark smoke signal is not magic — it traces to what is actually combusting, and reading that chain is what makes the check reliable rather than folklore.

Walk the chain for a genuine piece. Resin-rich agarwood burns a mixture of wood fiber and hardened resin → the resin volatilizes cleanly and the wood smolders without excess soot → the smoke stays pale with a faint oily sheen. Now the fake. Oiled or dyed wood carries cheap oil, dye, and chemical residue → those impurities do not volatilize cleanly → they char and smolder with a dirtier, sootier plume → the smoke reads dark. The color is a byproduct of what is in the material, not a separate signal. That is why the check works as a tendency: cleaner material, cleaner smoke; loaded material, dirtier smoke. It is also why the check alone never decides — a skilled fake can be tuned to burn pale.

## A Judgment Boundary: A Clean Burn Does Not Certify Authenticity

**Direct answer:** A good burn test is a useful backup, but it is decisive only when the seller can also name the species and origin — a clean smoke does not certify a material, it only lowers the odds against it.

This is the boundary most authenticity lists blur. A pale-smoke, sweet-aroma burn is what a genuine piece *tends* to produce → but it is not a certificate → a well-made oiled fake can be tuned to pass the burn → the burn tells you the material is behaving like the real thing, not that it is the real thing. My position: treat the burn as evidence, never as proof. The strongest signal stays the seller — a vendor who names the species, origin, and grade before any smoke is involved has already answered the question the burn only gestures at. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The One-Question Sort for Any Suspect Piece

**Direct answer:** The whole authentication page reduces to a single question — can the seller name the species, origin, and grade? — and that one question filters the majority of fakes before any burn test is needed.

Ask the question and watch the answers sort the field. A seller who names the species, origin, and grade → you have a claim worth verifying → the physical checks now mean something. A seller who hesitates, gives a vague "agarwood" with no species, or dodges origin → you already know the piece is suspect → no burn, no sink test, no money. The question works because every physical check downstream assumes the claim is real; the question tests the claim itself, for free, before anything is burned. A buyer who skips it is testing material against a guess, and the forger is counting on exactly that.

## The Difference Between a Heuristic and a Test, in One Word

**Direct answer:** A heuristic shifts the odds, a test decides them — and the single word that separates the two is "definitive."

A burn smoke check, a sink test, a warmed sniff — these are heuristics → they point the same direction most of the time → they reduce the chance of being fooled → they do not settle the question. A laboratory species identification is a test → it answers yes or no → it settles it. The practical meaning is that a buyer should treat every home check as a filter and reserve the word "authentic" for what only the lab or a trustworthy, specific seller can establish. The page's honesty lives in this one distinction: heuristics reduce risk, they do not remove it.

## What the Sink Test Actually Measures, and What It Never Can

**Direct answer:** The sink test measures one thing — density — and density is only a proxy for resin content, which means the test can clear low-resin wood but can never certify high-resin wood.

The chain runs one way. Resin is denser than water → resin-rich wood is denser than water → it sinks → the test correctly identifies heavy, resin-dense pieces. But the test cannot run backward. Oil is also denser than water → oiled wood sinks too → the test reports "heavy," not "resinous." So the honest reading is asymmetric: a floating piece is a strong rule-out, a sinking piece is only a weak rule-in. A buyer who treats a sinking chip as proof has let a proxy stand in for the thing it only approximates — and that is the exact leap the oil-injection fake is engineered to exploit.

## The Faker's Single Weak Link, and Where to Press

**Direct answer:** Every fake is a chain of countermeasures, and the chain has one link the forger cannot reinforce — the warmed aroma — which is why gentle heat, not the sink test, is the check that keeps working.

The forger can add weight to pass the sink test → can dye the surface to pass the color grade → can add a faint scent to pass a cold sniff. What is far harder to fake is the layered, evolving sweetness that real resin-rich wood releases under gentle heat → the oiled fake reads thin and cosmetic on heat, the dyed fake reads flat, the substituted wood reads like something else entirely. Press the one link that resists the countermeasures. Warm the piece slowly and smell it in stages; the fake trips on the very test that is hardest to game.

## Why the Checks Should Be Run Before You Ever Open Your Wallet

**Direct answer:** The authentication checks are best run in a fixed order before any money moves — seller, price, senses, lab — and that order is the page's real deliverable, not any single test.

Set the order in advance and it becomes a script. Ask the seller to name species, origin, and grade → a vague answer ends the transaction for free. Check the price → a too-cheap tag is a veto. Warm the piece and smell, then burn a sliver → the senses filter what the first two did not. Only for pieces valuable enough to justify it, the lab → the one rung that certifies. Running the script in order means you spend the cheapest filters first and reserve the dearest for last. The checks, run this way, are a discipline rather than a checklist — and the discipline is what a forger cannot out-engineer.

## The Ember's Two Jobs: Fuel and Fragrance at Once

**Direct answer:** A burning piece does two jobs at the same time — it is both the fuel source and the fragrance release valve — and that double duty is why no single check can ever be trusted alone.

When a piece smolders, two things happen at once. The material burns as fuel → the combustion supplies the heat. That heat then volatilizes the fragrant resins and oils held in the material → the scent releases into the room. The two jobs overlap, and that overlap is what the checks read. A genuine piece burns its resin cleanly → pale smoke and a layered aroma. An oiled fake burns its cheap oil as fuel → the smoke turns dirty and the aroma reads thin. The checks are all, underneath, ways of reading how the two jobs are being done — clean fuel, clean fragrance, or not.

## The Ember's Two Jobs, and Why Both Get Read

**Direct answer:** A smoldering piece performs two tasks at once — it burns as fuel and it releases fragrance — and the two tasks leave separate traces that a buyer reads as smoke color and warmed aroma.

The fuel task: the material combusts → the combustion supplies heat → the heat drives the fragrance task → the resins and oils volatilize → the room gets the scent. The two traces separate cleanly. The fuel leaves its mark in the smoke → clean material, pale smoke; loaded material, dark smoke. The fragrance leaves its mark in the warmed aroma → genuine resin, layered sweetness; injected oil, a thin cosmetic note. A buyer who reads both traces — the smoke for the fuel, the aroma for the fragrance — has read the material's whole character. Read only one, and the fake has a seam to hide in.

## The Scent Itself Cannot Lie, the Label Can

**Direct answer:** A material's scent is the one thing a forger cannot rewrite — the label is a claim, the smoke and the warmed aroma are the evidence — and that split is why the nose, not the tin, is the final arbiter.

A label can say anything. A seller can repeat it. The material itself is the one party that cannot be coached. Burn it → the smoke reads pale or dark, clean or sooty. Warm it → the aroma reads layered or flat, sweet or cosmetic. The scent does not argue, does not hedge, does not mark itself up. That is why every check on this page, whatever its order, ends at the senses: the label is testimony, the material is the witness, and a buyer who trusts the testimony over the witness has already decided to be fooled.

## The Grading Vocabulary a Buyer Should Recognize

**Direct answer:** The trade grades agarwood with a handful of Chinese terms — 沉水 (sinking), 半沉 (half-sinking), 奇楠 (kyara) — and recognizing them on a label is the difference between reading a grade and reading a story.

These terms are not decoration. 沉水 (chénshuǐ) marks resin-dense wood that sinks → the highest density band → the most valuable. 半沉半浮 (bàn chén bàn fú) marks the middle band → partially resinous → mid value. Floating wood is the low band → little resin → the least valuable. 奇楠 (qínán) is the structural outlier → oil-rich to the point of softness → the top of the whole market. A label that uses these terms is speaking the trade's own grading language; a label that avoids them and says only "agarwood" is leaving the grade unsaid. Recognizing the vocabulary is the first step to knowing what a seller is and is not telling you.

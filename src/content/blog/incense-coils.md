> **Direct answer:** Among the common incense formats, the coil holds the record for the longest single uninterrupted burn — the same dough as a stick, rearranged into a spiral so it can smolder for many hours without relighting.

## Key facts

- **Format:** 盘香 (*pánxiāng*) is extruded incense dough wound into a flat spiral before it dries — a shape, not a separate recipe.
- **Burn time:** hours, and in the broadest coils most of a day, set by two independent dials — spiral span (path length) and ribbon gauge (consumption rate).
- **Setup:** hung from a stand threaded through the centre hole, or laid flat on a heatproof plate over ash or sand.
- **Ignition point:** the outer tip only; the ember then crawls inward ring by ring.
- **Failure mode:** the spiral is wound wet and dried flat → outer rings lose water and shrink first → tension concentrates at the inner radius → a crack. That is a manufacturing signal, not transit damage.
- **Not a mosquito coil:** identical spiral, different payload — insecticide rather than aromatic material.
- **Use conditions:** off drafts and flammables, with air moving through the room for the whole burn.

## What a Coil Actually Is

Strip away the spiral and a coil is a plain incense stick that has been bent, ring after ring, before the dough set. 盘香 packs the equivalent of a long straight rod into a footprint that fits a small dish. That single fact explains almost everything about how the format behaves.

**Direct answer:** The coil's shape is a packaging decision, not a fragrance decision — the scent depends on the same dough as a stick, while the spiral decides how long and how steadily that dough will smolder.

The material side of a coil tracks the same recipe logic as any stick: aromatic woods, resins, and botanicals ground and bound together with a combustible binder. What separates the coil is geometry. A straight stick burns from one end and releases fragrance at a steady but short-lived rate; a coil converts the same length into a path that turns back on itself, so the glowing tip travels a long distance inside a small area.

## Why a Spiral Burns So Much Longer

**Direct answer:** The spiral extends burn time by lengthening the smolder path without growing the footprint — more distance traveled, not more incense consumed per minute.

Think of the ember as a point moving along a line. A stick's line is short and straight, so the ember finishes quickly. A coil's line is the same physical length of dough, but it is folded into concentric rings; the ember walks the full length of that folded line, and because it moves inward ring by ring, the burn lasts for hours rather than minutes.

Two variables, not one, set the clock:

- **Diameter** sets the total path length. A wide spiral simply contains more linear distance of dough.
- **Thickness** sets how fast that path is consumed. A thicker ribbon smolders more slowly per unit length, so it also stretches the burn.

These two knobs are independent, which is the source of most beginner confusion. A small-but-thick coil can outlast a large-but-thin one, because burn time is the *product* of path length and per-length consumption rate, not a function of diameter alone.

One more detail keeps the burn steady as the ember walks inward: the ring it just left leaves a thin line of spent ash behind it, and that ash acts as a mild blanket that slows the heat from escaping sideways. The result is a slow, predictable crawl rather than a racing front — the same reason a coil feels "even" compared to a stick that gets noticeably hotter as the flame nears the base.

## The Wet-Dough Problem (Why Coils Crack)

**Direct answer:** Coils crack mainly because they are wound while wet and then dried in a flat spiral, a shape that dries unevenly and pulls against itself.

The spiral is formed from the same extruded paste as a straight stick, but the winding happens *before* drying instead of after. That reversal of sequence creates two failure points a straight stick never faces.

First, the dough must stay pliable enough to bend into tight rings without splitting. This pushes the formula toward a touch more moisture and a reliable binder — makko and similar natural binders are the common fix, covered in [makko & natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

Second, and more subtly, the flat spiral dries from the outside in. The outer rings expose more surface area than the inner core, so they lose water faster → they shrink faster → the shrinking outer rings pull on the still-damp center → tension concentrates at the inner radius → a crack opens. A coil that bows or splits is nearly always a drying or dough problem, not damage from shipping. That distinction matters to anyone buying coils by mail: a cracked ring is a manufacturing signal, not a transit accident.

## Coil vs Stick vs Mosquito Coil: A Field Guide

**Direct answer:** A coil, a stick, and a mosquito coil are three different objects that share ingredients only by accident — and a mosquito coil must never be burned as incense.

| Signal | Incense coil | Incense stick | Mosquito coil |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Flat spiral | Straight rod | Flat spiral |
| Purpose | Release fragrance for hours | A timed ~20–60 min scent session | Release insecticide to repel pests |
| Load | Aromatic woods, resins, botanicals | Aromatic woods, resins, botanicals | Pyrethroid or similar insecticide |
| Burn time | Hours to most of a day | ~20–60 minutes | Hours |
| Safe as room fragrance | Yes (with ventilation) | Yes (with ventilation) | No |

The shared spiral is where the confusion comes from. A mosquito coil uses the identical geometry for a completely unrelated job — slow, even release of an insecticide across several hours. Lighting one indoors as a "fragrance" is a genuine hazard, and the reverse mistake (expecting a fragrant incense coil to repel insects) does not work either. Read the label before lighting anything spiral-shaped.

## How to Burn One Without Regret

**Direct answer:** The coil needs one of two supports — a stand threaded through its center, or a heatproof plate with an ash bed — and it must be lit at the outer end so the ember crawls inward.

The center hole is the clue to the stand method: a wire or peg passes through it and lets the spiral hang clear of anything it could scorch. The plate method instead lays the coil flat over ash or sand, which both insulates the surface below and gives the falling ash somewhere to land. Start the fire at the outer tip, because that is the only end that feeds the ember a full inward path; lighting the middle just burns two short stubs.

Two habits keep a multi-hour burn from turning into a problem: keep the coil off drafts and flammables, and move air through the room. A coil that smolders for hours is a small open fire with a very long tail, so the setup deserves more forethought than a stick gets. See [incense holder & burner guide](/blog/incense-holder-burner-guide/) and the [safety guidance](/safety/).

## When the "Hours-Long" Label Stops Being a Benefit

**Direct answer:** A long burn is only a virtue when the space can take hours of continuous smoke — the moment a room is sealed or unattended, the coil's biggest selling point becomes its biggest liability.

The judgment here is conditional, and the condition is ventilation plus supervision. An all-day coil in a large, aired, supervised space is a reasonable way to hold a room scent steady without relighting. The same coil in a small closed bedroom, or left burning while the house is empty, is smoke accumulating with no one watching it. My position: coils are underrated as a background format and overrated as a beginner format — they reward the experienced user who respects the burn length, and they punish the newcomer who assumes "longer" means "better."

*The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## Reading a Coil Before You Light It

**Direct answer:** Three visible checks — the spacing of the rings, the evenness of the dough color, and the state of the center hole — expose most manufacturing faults before a single match is struck.

A coil telegraphs its drying history on its surface, which makes shelf inspection a genuinely useful shortcut. It all follows from the wet-dough reasoning above.

| Check | What you see | What it signals |
|---|---|---|
| Ring spacing | Even, concentric rings | Dough wound while properly pliable |
| Ring spacing | Rings that touch or pinch | Wound too wet, or rushed |
| Dough color | Uniform throughout | Even mixing and drying |
| Dough color | Darker outer rings, pale core | Uneven drying — the crack risk |
| Center hole | Clean and centered | Careful winding |
| Center hole | Torn or off-center | Weak dough or rough handling |

The rule that ties these rows together: **a coil that dried unevenly will look uneven before it ever fails.** Outer rings that read darker or shrunken are the same tension that eventually opens a crack at the inner radius, so the split is predictable rather than random. That is the practical payoff of the mechanism — you can spot a likely splitter on the shelf, not discover it after it arrives broken.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Judging burn time from diameter alone.** Thickness is the second, independent knob — a thick small coil can outlast a thin large one. Check the stated time.
2. **Burning without a stand or a heatproof bed.** The coil smolders for hours; the surface underneath matters the entire time.
3. **Skipping ventilation on a multi-hour burn.** Continuous smoke needs continuous air movement.
4. **Confusing a coil with a mosquito coil.** Same shape, different payload, different safety rules.

## Evidence status

The reasoning on this page — path length multiplied by consumption rate, spent ash acting as a mild heat blanket, differential drying pulling a crack open — is a physical account of how a wound dough spiral behaves. It is not a set of figures measured on a test bench. Burn-time ranges here are the ones manufacturers print, and they vary by product, so no independent test is being quoted. The safety points are precautions rather than findings: smoke from any burning incense adds particulate matter to indoor air, which is why ventilation and supervision are treated as conditions of use rather than optional habits. Nothing on this page is a health claim.

## Summary

- A coil is a stick rewound into a spiral; the spiral lengthens the burn without enlarging the footprint.
- Burn time is set by diameter (path length) and thickness (consumption rate) acting together.
- Cracking is a wet-dough and uneven-drying problem, not shipping damage.
- A mosquito coil is insecticide, not incense, despite the identical shape.
- The long burn is a benefit only with ventilation and supervision.

## FAQ

### Q: How long will a coil keep burning?
Plan on hours, with the spread set by the spiral's span and the ribbon's gauge acting together. A narrow, thin coil may finish in a couple of hours, a broad, thick one can carry most of a day, and manufacturers differ enough that the figure printed on the package is the only reliable number. That long steady run is exactly why temples, long ceremonies, and all-afternoon room scenting favor the coil. See [which incense format](/blog/which-incense-format/).

### Q: Why does my coil crack or come apart before I burn it?
Almost always a manufacturing issue, not transit damage. Because the coil is wound while the dough is still damp and then dried flat, the outer rings lose moisture before the center, and the differential shrinkage pulls the spiral apart at its inner radius. A formula that was too dry to wind, or a drying step that ran unevenly, is the usual culprit. See the section above on the wet-dough problem.

### Q: How is a coil different from a mosquito coil?
The shape is the only thing they share, and it hides a real danger. An incense coil is aromatic material — woods, resins, botanicals — made to release fragrance; a mosquito coil is loaded with an insecticide and exists to repel pests. Lighting a mosquito coil indoors as fragrance is harmful, and expecting a fragrance coil to drive off insects does not work in the other direction either. Check the label before you light anything spiral-shaped.

### Q: Coil or stick for a quick pause?
For a ten-minute pause, a stick or a cone is the better tool; a coil is a commitment to hours. A stick runs roughly 20 to 60 minutes and stops on its own, whereas a coil keeps going whether you are still in the room or not. Save the coil for the times you want a scent held steady across an afternoon without tending it. See [incense sticks](/blog/incense-sticks/).

**Direct answer:** 沉香 (agarwood) and 檀香 (sandalwood) are the two woods that hold up classical Chinese incense, yet the way each one becomes fragrant is a mirror image of the other. Agarwood is not a tree species but a *wound response* — an *Aquilaria* tree, once injured, saturates its own heartwood with resin until the wood turns aromatic. Sandalwood flips this: a *Santalum* tree simply carries its perfume in the mature core while standing perfectly healthy, no damage needed. Agarwood smells dark, resinous, sweet, and shifting; sandalwood smells warm, creamy, and steady. Both are faked on a large scale, though through entirely separate tricks.

## Key facts

Agarwood is heartwood changed by resin inside the *Aquilaria* genus (Thymelaeaceae); sandalwood is naturally scented heartwood of *Santalum* (Santalaceae). Neither is a tapped tear of resin. Both are warmed rather than flamed in practice — agarwood so a staged aroma can unfold, sandalwood so a slow cooked note can surface. Neither outranks the other; each fills a different seat in a blend.

## Key Takeaways

- Agarwood is **wood made fragrant by injury**; sandalwood is **wood fragrant in the healthy tree** — the two origin stories run in opposite directions.
- Agarwood's ceiling is **奇楠 (qínán / "kyara")**; sandalwood's benchmark is **老山檀 (lǎoshān tán, aged Indian *Santalum album*)** — two unlike ideas of "best."
- Agarwood is ranked by **resin density (the sink-or-float screen)**; sandalwood by **species, heartwood share, and oil content**.
- Both carry high substitution risk, and the door the deception comes through differs: agarwood is counterfeited as timber, sandalwood as species or as oil.
- Both prefer **gentle heat** over open flame.
- Nothing on this page is a health claim for either wood; the comparison is about aroma, grading, and buying.

## The Opposite Ways a Tree Turns Fragrant

**Direct answer:** Agarwood earns its scent through damage; sandalwood is born with it. One is a scar product, the other a birthright.

These two materials occupy opposite ends of the question "how does a tree become aromatic":

- **沉香 (agarwood)** is a *condition*, not a species. Undamaged *Aquilaria* heartwood is pale, light, and close to odorless. The fragrance appears only after the tree is hurt — by burrowing insects, storm damage, or a deliberate cut — and answers with resin. The causal sequence: bark breached → the tree secretes resin as a defense → the resin seeps into nearby heartwood → over years or decades the timber darkens, densifies, and turns aromatic → "agarwood" names that altered tissue, not the tree that made it. The botanical background sits on the [agarwood entity page](/ingredients/agarwood/).
- **檀香 (sandalwood)** is fragrant *heartwood* in a living tree. The scent is already present in the mature core of *Santalum*, with no wounding step. The tree is **hemiparasitic** — it attaches to host-plant roots to feed — which partly explains its slow growth and the high cost of establishing it. Species detail for the other wood is on the [sandalwood entity page](/ingredients/sandalwood/).

That single contrast explains nearly everything downstream. Grading, pricing, and the two separate counterfeiting channels all trace back to "scar product" versus "born-scented core."

## What the Names Reveal

**Direct answer:** Each Chinese name encodes the wood's defining trait — agarwood is "sinking fragrance" (a density hint), sandalwood is "sandalwood fragrance" — and one of them hides a naming trap.

- **沉香 (chénxiāng)** — "sinking fragrance." The name points at the density screen: top-grade agarwood is dense enough to sink in water, the traditional first filter for resin content.
- **檀香 (tánxiāng)** — "sandalwood fragrance," a plain label for the wood.

The trap is the character 檀, which does double duty. 檀香 (sandalwood) is easily confused with 紫檀 (*Pterocarpus santalinus*), "red sandalwood" — a crimson dye wood prized for color rather than aroma, and a completely different plant. The shared character keeps the two names tangled in English and Chinese alike. The dye wood has its own entry under [red sandalwood](/ingredients/red-sandalwood/).

Botanically the two sources part cleanly:

- **Agarwood** traces to *Aquilaria* species (Thymelaeaceae), roughly fifteen to twenty tree species spread across South and Southeast Asia. The two that matter commercially are *Aquilaria sinensis* (白木香, southern China) and *Aquilaria malaccensis*, the principal Southeast Asian source and a CITES Appendix II listing.
- **Sandalwood** traces to *Santalum* species (Santalaceae). The reference is *Santalum album*, Indian or "white" sandalwood; the chief legal commercial stand-in is Australian *S. spicatum*.

## How Each Wood Smells

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is a deep, layered, resinous scent that keeps changing as it warms; sandalwood is a warm, creamy, softly woody scent that holds one comforting line. One unfolds, the other persists.

| Aspect | Agarwood (沉香) | Sandalwood (檀香) |
|---|---|---|
| First impression | Dark and layered, with something underneath | Soft and warm, immediately legible |
| Words that fit | Resinous, sweet-bitter, changing | Creamy, buttery, gently woody |
| Under gentle heat | Arrives in waves, then settles | Holds a single line for a long time |
| Job in a formula | Floor and depth beneath the heart | Bridge between heart and base |

Because both live in the base register, they work as **partners rather than rivals**. Sandalwood's smoothness gives brighter notes a platform, while agarwood supplies the darker, rarer depth underneath. Classical 合香 blends often pair the two woods as a single twin anchor.

My editorial read: the "which is better" framing most people bring to this comparison is the wrong question. The right one is "which register do I need right now — depth or smoothness — and which one is more likely to be fake in the form I'm buying?"

## Warm It; Do Not Flame It

**Direct answer:** Both woods want gentle heat rather than open flame, but for opposite reasons — agarwood to avoid burning off its staged top notes, sandalwood to let its slow cooked note surface gradually.

- **Agarwood** is traditionally warmed over indirect heat (隔火焚香), chips resting on a plate above charcoal buried in ash so the aroma releases without combusting. A direct flame burns off the delicate top notes before they reach the nose.
- **Sandalwood** smolders steadily on its own — it *can* burn as a wood, unlike a resin — but still rewards slow heat over a harsh flame, which draws out a long, deep, slightly cooked wood character.

The mechanism behind the difference is structural. Sandalwood's fibers hold together under a slow burn → its volatile oils escape gradually → the scent stays even across the session. Agarwood's value is concentrated in volatiles destroyed at flame temperature → overheating collapses the aroma into char → the staged experience never happens. That is why the two are warmed for opposite-seeming reasons that both resolve to "keep the heat low."

## Two Different Grading Ladders

**Direct answer:** Agarwood and sandalwood are graded on separate scales — agarwood by resin density (the sink-or-float screen) plus a special top tier, sandalwood by species, heartwood share, oil content, and age.

The two "top grades" are not parallel ideas:

- **奇楠 (qínán / "kyara")** is the highest agarwood grade, a structurally distinct material where "oil wraps wood" (油包木) rather than "wood wraps oil" (木包油). It is soft enough to shave into curls, can be kneaded into pellets, and is fragrant even unheated. There is a dedicated treatment under [qínán / kyara](/blog/qinan-kyara/).
- **老山檀 (lǎoshān tán)**, literally old-mountain sandalwood, names the aged, oil-rich Indian *Santalum album* heartwood that the trade treats as its reference point. That makes it a tier inside a single species rather than a distinct material state, which is what 奇楠 is.

Side by side:

| What is ranked | Agarwood | Sandalwood |
|---|---|---|
| Primary axis | Resin density (sink test 沉水) | Species identity |
| Secondary axis | 奇楠 distinction | Heartwood share, oil content, age |
| Top reference | 奇楠 (qínán), "oil wraps wood" | 老山檀, aged Indian *S. album* |
| Bottom of the ladder | Pale, low-resin, near-scentless wood | Young, low-oil plantation wood |

The lesson: "top grade" means different work in each system — a density test for one, a species-and-age question for the other. Both ladders are set out in the [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/), and the sandalwood side in the [sandalwood entity page](/ingredients/sandalwood/).

## Why the Price Gap Opens Up

**Direct answer:** Agarwood's price climbs with resin density, the 奇楠 distinction, and scarcity; sandalwood's climbs with species (*S. album* versus substitutes), heartwood and oil content, and CITES pressure on wild stock. Read those as directional signals rather than as a price list.

The two woods sit in different price bands for structural reasons:

- **Agarwood** — low-grade medicinal chips are cheap, but burnable, gradeable material scales steeply with resin density, and 奇楠 sits at the very top of the whole market. The spread *within* "agarwood" is enormous.
- **Sandalwood** — genuine *S. album* powder is costly and 老山檀 carries a premium, while plantation and Australian *S. spicatum* material runs far cheaper. Very cheap "sandalwood" is almost always synthetic fragrance oil wearing the name.

What follows is a useful asymmetry: sandalwood's ceiling is capped because legal plantation substitutes exist, while agarwood's is not — nothing replaces a dense, old, resin-saturated chip. For a wider view, the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) sets both woods beside the other classical materials.

## Two Ways to Get Fooled

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is faked *as wood* — dyed timber, unrelated species, oil-injected material — while sandalwood is faked *as species or oil* — a substitute *Santalum* or synthetic fragrance sold under the name. The deception enters through different doors.

- **Agarwood adulteration** — pale wood dyed or soaked in oil to look resinous, foreign woods dressed up as agarwood, and low-grade material injected with oil so it sinks in the density screen. The screening routines for both are collected in the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).
- **Sandalwood substitution** — one *Santalum* species swapped for another (Australian or otherwise), oil sold where wood is claimed, and synthetic "sandalwood" fragrance replacing the real thing. Case-by-case swaps are catalogued in the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

The practical payoff of knowing this split: a collector needs **two different verification habits**. With agarwood you interrogate the *wood itself* — is it real, is it dense, is it injected? With sandalwood you interrogate the *label* — which species, wood or oil, and does the "buttery" note actually appear? One is a forensic question, the other a reading-comprehension question.

## The Sink-or-Float Screen, and Where It Breaks

**Direct answer:** The sink-or-float screen is a density check, not a quality proof — a chip that sinks is resin-heavy, but a chip can be made to sink by injection, which is exactly the cheat the test invites.

The physical logic is plain: resin is denser than the pale, airy wood it fills → the more resin a chip holds, the heavier it is for its volume → a sufficiently saturated chip displaces its own weight and drops → a thin, resin-poor chip floats. For centuries this was a quick bench test needing no equipment and no specialist.

The failure mode arrives with modern adulteration. Because the test reads weight per volume and nothing else, a low-grade chip can be loaded with injected oil until it sinks, and it will pass the screen while smelling flat. Density is therefore a *necessary* signal of resin content but not a *sufficient* signal of aroma quality. A buyer who leans on the sink test alone is measuring one axis and calling it the verdict. The aroma on gentle heat is the test that decides whether a specific piece is worth the price.

## The Substitution Matrix

**Direct answer:** If you cannot get — or cannot afford — one of these woods, the swap you reach for depends on which wood's *job* you are trying to fill, and the wrong swap fails in a specific, predictable way.

| You wanted | Common stand-in | Does it work? | Why it falls short |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agarwood depth | Sandalwood | Partly | Smooth and grounding, but lacks agarwood's dark, evolving resin |
| Agarwood depth | Cheap "agarwood" (dyed/injected) | No | May sink yet smell flat or artificial |
| Sandalwood creaminess | Australian *S. spicatum* | Mostly | A legitimate relative; drier and less buttery than *S. album* |
| Sandalwood creaminess | Synthetic "sandalwood" oil | No | Sweet but one-note; not the wood's layered warmth |
| Either, as a base note | The other wood | Only in a blend | They coexist as anchors, not as replacements |

The table's takeaway is that "substitute" is not one category. There are legitimate relatives (one *Santalum* for another), functional stand-ins (one base note for another in a blend), and outright fakes (dyed wood, synthetic oil). Only the last two are problems, and they fail in opposite ways — one smells wrong, the other is mislabeled.

## Reading the Form Before You Buy

**Direct answer:** The physical form you buy — whole chip, powder, or oil — changes what you can verify at home, so the form itself is part of the purchase decision, not just a packaging detail.

Agarwood is sold mainly as chips, powder, and occasionally oil; sandalwood as chips, powder, sticks, and oil. Each form hands the buyer a different set of signals:

| What you are holding | What it lets you confirm | What it conceals |
|---|---|---|
| A whole agarwood chip | Grain, resin banding, weight in water, behaviour on heat | Injection inside the piece stays invisible |
| Milled agarwood | Scent on gentle heat, grind consistency, water content | The density sort is gone and dust hides flaws |
| A sandalwood chip | Species grain, density, the buttery note when warmed | Oil content remains an estimate without testing |
| Milled sandalwood | Creaminess, persistence, general character | Species cannot be read from powder by eye |
| Anything sold as oil | The smell in the bottle | Species, dilution, and whether wood was involved |

The table's message is that form and fraud interact. Powder removes the sink screen but keeps the aroma test; an oil removes almost every check at once, which is why "agarwood oil" and "sandalwood oil" are the forms where mislabeling is easiest to hide. A whole chip is the most verifiable form for agarwood precisely because it retains the density screen.

## A Grading-Pitfall Table

**Direct answer:** Most mix-ups between these woods come from borrowing one wood's grading words for the other — a table of the traps makes the boundaries explicit.

| Phrase you might hear | Which wood it belongs to | The trap it hides |
|---|---|---|
| "Sinks in water" | Agarwood | Density test; not a sandalwood term |
| "Oil wraps wood" (油包木) | Agarwood (奇楠) | A material state, not a sandalwood tier |
| "Old-mountain" (老山) | Sandalwood | A quality tier, not an agarwood grade |
| "Australian" | Sandalwood | A legal substitute species, not agarwood |
| "Buttery" | Sandalwood | An authenticity marker, not an agarwood note |

Each row is a place where a beginner borrows one wood's vocabulary for the other and ends up comparing unlike things. The fix is to keep the two ladders apart: density language for agarwood, species-and-age language for sandalwood.

## The Species Behind the Labels

**Direct answer:** Both market names cover a genus rather than a single tree, and the practical differences inside each genus are large enough that a species name is worth more than a grade word.

Agarwood comes from the Thymelaeaceae family, genus *Aquilaria*. The species a buyer meets most often are *A. sinensis* in southern China, *A. malaccensis* across Malaysia and Indonesia, and *A. crassna* in Vietnam and Cambodia, with *A. filaria*, *A. microcarpa*, *A. beccariana*, *A. hirta*, and several others filling in the regional trade. Each has its own reputation, its own plantation history, and its own legal treatment under the CITES appendices.

Sandalwood comes from Santalaceae, genus *Santalum*. *S. album* is the Indian reference material, the wood behind the 老山檀 tier. *S. spicatum* is the Australian species that supplies most legal volume today. *S. austrocaledonicum* covers New Caledonia and Vanuatu, *S. yasi* Fiji, *S. insulare* across the Pacific islands, and *S. ellipticum* and *S. paniculatum* Hawaii. They are relatives, not equivalents: an oil distilled from one is not the oil of another, and the difference is detectable in the nose long before any certificate arrives.

| Genus | Family | Reference species | Common commercial alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Aquilaria* | Thymelaeaceae | *A. sinensis*, *A. malaccensis* | *A. crassna*, *A. filaria*, *A. microcarpa* |
| *Santalum* | Santalaceae | *S. album* | *S. spicatum*, *S. austrocaledonicum*, *S. yasi* |

A buyer who asks only "is it real agarwood" is asking a question that a genus-level answer satisfies. The useful question is narrower: which species, from which district, and can the seller name both without hesitating. The same discipline applies to sandalwood, where the genus is agreed and the species is exactly what the price is about.

## What Origin Actually Tells You

**Direct answer:** Origin is shorthand for a set of growing conditions, harvest practices, and trade reputations — it predicts aroma character loosely and legal status precisely, and the two predictions do not always agree.

In Chinese agarwood trade, Hainan and Vietnam have long carried the strongest aromatic reputation, with Cambodian material close behind in some periods of the market. Malay and Indonesian material supplies the bulk of commercial volume, and much of it reaches buyers through consolidation rather than direct farm supply. The reputation attached to a place is a social fact built up over generations of buying; it is not a chemical property, and a well-made plantation lot from a less glamorous district can outperform a poorly handled "famous" one.

Legal status is harder edged. Species listed on the CITES appendices require export permits, and the requirement attaches to the species, not to the reputation. A consignment described as "Vietnamese" tells you nothing about which *Aquilaria* it is, and the permit question follows the species. That is why a serious purchase file carries both facts — district and species — rather than treating one as a proxy for the other.

Sandalwood's geography is simpler and more heavily regulated. Indian *S. album* has been restricted for years, which is why Australian *S. spicatum* and Pacific island species carry so much of the legal trade. A seller who names "Indian sandalwood" without a species and a harvest basis is describing an aspiration rather than a supply.

## How Each Wood Behaves in a Formula

**Direct answer:** In a blend, both woods sit at the base, but they behave differently under heat and binder, and the difference decides how much of each a formula can carry.

Agarwood is a small-dose material. Its resin is intense, and a formula that leans on it heavily can flatten the middle notes beneath it; the usual practice is to use it as an anchor at low proportion and let brighter materials work above it. Sandalwood is a larger-dose material: creamy, forgiving, and structurally useful because it rounds the transition between the heart and the base.

The mechanism matters for the press. Resin-rich agarwood powder is dense and slightly oily; in a stick it can slow the burn and leave a heavier residue if overdosed. Sandalwood powder is drier and more fibrous, and it helps a stick hold shape and burn evenly. A blender who substitutes one for the other at the same weight is changing both the scent and the mechanical behaviour of the stick.

| Property | Agarwood in a blend | Sandalwood in a blend |
|---|---|---|
| Typical dose | Low; intense material | Higher; structural material |
| Effect on burn | Can slow it, leaves residue if heavy | Steadies it, helps shape retention |
| Function | Dark anchor beneath the heart | Creamy bridge and base |
| Failure if overdosed | Flattens the middle, smokes heavily | Turns powdery and dull |

None of this is a recipe. It is the reasoning a blender uses when deciding whether a formula needs depth or smoothness, and it explains why the two woods so often appear together instead of one replacing the other.

## Two Question Lists for Two Sellers

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is verified by interrogating the wood, sandalwood by interrogating the label — so the questions a buyer asks should differ, even when the same dealer sells both.

For agarwood, the questions run: which *Aquilaria* species; which district; plantation or forest; what density band; chips or powder; what moisture; and which documents follow the lot. A seller who can answer the first two without pausing has usually handled the material. A seller who answers "premium agarwood" to all seven has told you nothing.

For sandalwood, the questions run: which *Santalum* species; is this wood or oil; where was it grown; what is the harvest basis; and does the material show the buttery note on gentle heat. The species question is the load-bearing one, because the price difference between *S. album* and a plantation substitute is large and the visual difference is small.

| Ask about | Agarwood | Sandalwood |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Species, district | Species, wood versus oil |
| Condition | Density band, moisture, form | Heartwood share, oil content |
| Legal | CITES status, permits | Harvest basis, export rules |
| Sensory | Staged aroma on gentle heat | Buttery note, steady release |

The two lists converge at the end: whatever the material, the nose is the last check and the paperwork is the first. Between them sits the seller's willingness to answer specifics, which is often the most informative signal of all.

## Budgets, Small Quantities, and the Honest Compromise

**Direct answer:** A limited budget does not have to mean a fake purchase, but it does mean choosing which axis to give up.

The first thing to give up is size, not authenticity. A small quantity of verified material teaches more than a large quantity of doubtful material, and the small lot can be kept as a reference for years. The second thing to give up is grade, deliberately: a mid-band agarwood chip warmed gently will show the material's character, while a top-band chip of unknown origin shows only the seller's confidence.

For sandalwood, the honest compromise is species substitution with a clear label. Australian *S. spicatum* is a legitimate material with its own character; buying it as itself costs less than buying it as *S. album*, and the difference in the nose is smaller than the difference in the price. What is not a compromise is synthetic fragrance sold as wood, because that is not a lower tier of the same thing.

The pattern across both woods is the same. Spend the budget on verification first — a small verified lot, a second sample from another seller, a laboratory check on a high-value purchase — and let quantity follow later. Buyers who reverse that order end up with a cupboard of material they cannot use and cannot sell.


## Reading the Grain Before You Trust the Name

**Direct answer:** The two woods look nothing alike under a bright light, and learning the surface is the cheapest authentication tool a buyer owns — it costs a lens and a few minutes.

Agarwood is a soft, pale timber interrupted by darker bands. The pale portion is the host wood, light and fibrous; the dark portion is where resin has displaced air and hardened the fibre. Good material shows the resin as irregular streaks, patches, or a mottled field rather than as a uniform stain, because resin follows the grain and the wounds that produced it. A piece with an even, painted-looking darkness and no visible structure is a piece to put down.

Sandalwood is the opposite: fine, even, close-grained, with a heartwood that runs from pale yellow into a warm brown as it ages. The grain is dense and regular, the surface takes a polish, and there is no resin banding because the fragrance is distributed through the heartwood rather than deposited in wounds. A sandalwood chip with dramatic dark streaks is usually a different wood wearing the name.

| Feature | Agarwood | Sandalwood |
|---|---|---|
| Colour pattern | Pale host wood with dark resin bands | Even heartwood, pale to warm brown |
| Grain | Soft, irregular, resin follows wounds | Fine, dense, uniform |
| Weight | Highly variable; the good end sinks | Heavy but consistent |
| Surface | Matte where resin is heavy | Takes a polish, feels dense |

The surface does not settle authenticity on its own. Dyed agarwood can imitate banding, and a sandalwood substitute can be close-grained. What the surface does is sort the obvious from the plausible in seconds, which is the right job for a first filter.

## The Oil Question

**Direct answer:** Oil and wood are different products made from the same tree, and buying one while expecting the other is the most common category error in this corner of the trade.

Sandalwood oil is produced by distilling the heartwood, and the resulting liquid carries the creamy, woody signature in concentrated form. Agarwood oil is produced from resinous wood, usually by distillation of material that would otherwise be sold as chips, and the yield is small relative to the input — which is one reason the oil is expensive and frequently extended.

The practical differences matter to a buyer. Wood can be examined for grain, density, and resin distribution; oil can only be evaluated by nose, and a nose needs a reference to compare against. Oil is also easy to dilute and easy to blend from other aromatic sources, so a bottle that smells broadly right may contain very little of the tree named on the label.

| What you are buying | What you can check | What you cannot check |
|---|---|---|
| Chips | Grain, density, resin bands, aroma on heat | Whether the resin is natural or injected |
| Powder | Aroma on heat, moisture, particle size | Species, once the structure is gone |
| Oil | Aroma in the bottle and on a test strip | Species, dilution, or added isolates |

The rule that follows is blunt: buy oil from someone whose wood you could also inspect, or from a distiller who will discuss the feedstock. A seller who offers oil but cannot describe the material it came from is selling a smell, not a provenance.

## Heat Sources and What They Do to the Aroma

**Direct answer:** The heat source is not a detail; it sets the temperature curve, and the temperature curve decides which part of the material's aroma you actually get to smell.

Four arrangements are common. A charcoal bed buried under ash gives a slow, radiant heat that suits both woods and is the traditional method behind 隔火焚香. An electric heater with adjustable temperature gives repeatability, which is what a buyer needs when comparing lots. A direct flame destroys the volatile top notes of agarwood and scorches sandalwood into a bitter char. A resin burner designed for granulated resin often runs too hot for chip material and drives the oils off in a burst.

| Heat source | Temperature control | Suits | Fails at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charcoal under ash | Indirect, slow to change | Chips of both woods | Control; needs practice |
| Electric heater | Precise and repeatable | Comparison and daily use | Very high-resin chips needing higher heat |
| Direct flame | None | Nothing aromatic | Burns the volatiles, leaves char |
| Resin burner | Coarse | Granules and powders | Whole chips, which scorch outside and stay cold inside |

For comparison work, the electric heater is the honest instrument, because two lots can be warmed at the same setting one after the other. For appreciation, the charcoal bed gives a slower reveal and lets agarwood unfold in stages. What both share is the principle that heat should arrive gradually, since a fast ramp drives off the light fractions before the deeper material has warmed.

## Collecting, Using, and the Cost of the Habit

**Direct answer:** The two woods reward different behaviours — agarwood rewards patience and documentation, sandalwood rewards steady use — and buying against your own habit is how collections fill with material nobody touches.

Agarwood tends to attract accumulators. Pieces are small, the grades are many, and each lot has a story about where it came from. The habit that makes a collection useful is record keeping: a note of species, district, band, and date on every bag, kept with a sealed reference portion. Without that, a drawer of chips becomes a drawer of anonymous brown wood within a few years.

Sandalwood tends to attract users. It is bought to be burned, worn, or blended, and the material is consumed rather than stored. The habit that matters here is sourcing consistency: finding a supplier whose stock smells the same each time, and buying enough of one lot to last rather than chasing small parcels.

The economics differ accordingly. A collector's cost sits in the material and is realised only on resale, which requires the records that make a piece identifiable. A user's cost sits in consumption, where the relevant figure is how long a given piece actually scents a room rather than what it cost per gram. Both are legitimate ways to spend money on aroma; the mistake is buying like a collector while consuming like a user, or the reverse.


## Storage, Humidity, and the Slow Drift of Aroma

**Direct answer:** Both woods lose character in storage, but they lose it differently — agarwood to oxidation and drying, sandalwood to oil migration and surface loss — and the container matters more than the shelf.

Agarwood's aromatic content sits in resin that is chemically stable but physically exposed once the wood is cut or milled. A chip kept in a sealed jar in a cool, dark place will hold its character for years. The same chip left on an open dish in a dry room will slowly lose the lighter fractions, and the first thing to go is exactly the top note that makes a good piece interesting. Powder is far more vulnerable than chips because the exposed surface is so much larger.

Sandalwood holds up better in the short term and worse in one specific way: the oil migrates. A polished bead or a smooth chip will develop a duller surface as oil moves within the wood, and a piece stored next to paper or cloth will leave its scent on the neighbour. That migration is not damage, but it does mean sandalwood should be stored apart from materials whose smell you care about.

| Storage factor | Agarwood | Sandalwood |
|---|---|---|
| Enemy | Dry air, light, heat | Heat, absorbent neighbours |
| Best container | Sealed glass or metal, dark | Sealed wood or glass, away from textiles |
| Powder | Short-lived; use promptly | More stable than agarwood powder |
| Signs of decline | Flat note, brittle edges | Dull surface, weaker cold scent |

The practical rule for both is the same as for any aromatic store: keep it closed, keep it cool, keep it dark, and keep a labelled reference portion that never gets handled. A reference that has been opened a hundred times is no longer a reference.

## What a Laboratory Can and Cannot Tell You

**Direct answer:** Instrumental testing answers identity questions well and quality questions partially — which is why a certificate is a starting point, not a verdict.

For agarwood, the useful laboratory work is species identification and, where the stakes justify it, a check on whether the resin is natural or introduced. Chromatographic profiles can show the compound families associated with genuine resin and can flag material that looks chemically unlike the reference. What no instrument can do is tell you whether a specific piece smells good; that judgment belongs to a warmed sliver and a trained nose.

For sandalwood, the laboratory question is usually species and oil content. Distillation yield and the ratio of the characteristic sesquiterpene alcohols differ between *S. album* and its relatives, which makes species testing meaningful where the price gap is large. A certificate that says "sandalwood" without naming the species has answered the easy half of the question.

| Test | Answers | Leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Species identification | Which plant the sample came from | Whether the batch matches the sample |
| Resin or oil content | How much aromatic material is present | Whether that material is natural |
| Chromatographic profile | Compound families present | Subjective quality |
| Sensory panel | Aroma character and development | Nothing about identity |

The sequence that works is sensory first, laboratory second, and only for lots whose value makes the invoice sensible. A buyer who sends every sample to a lab is buying certainty at a price the material may not support; a buyer who never sends anything is trusting a nose that has no reference behind it.

## Building a Reference Set the Nose Can Use

**Direct answer:** Aroma judgment improves only against remembered standards, so the practical step for a buyer is a small, labelled reference collection of known material.

The set does not need to be expensive. One verified chip of each band, one sandalwood of known species, one sample of a substitute sold honestly as itself, and one obvious fake kept as a warning are enough to give the nose four fixed points. Each reference is stored sealed and labelled with what it is, where it came from, and when it was acquired.

The comparison method matters as much as the collection. Warm the reference and the unknown at the same setting, on the same heater, one after the other, and note the difference in the first thirty seconds and again after several minutes. Agarwood's character is in the development; sandalwood's is in the steady middle. A taster who only sniffs the first moment will miss the thing that distinguishes good agarwood from merely dense agarwood.

| Stage | What to notice | Why it discriminates |
|---|---|---|
| First warmth | Brightness, sweetness, any sharpness | Catches injected or oiled material |
| Middle | Body, complexity, woodiness | Separates bands and species |
| Dry-down | Residue, bitterness, persistence | Reveals filler and scorched notes |

Keeping notes at each stage turns a pleasant habit into usable knowledge. Two years of brief entries will tell a buyer more about a supplier than any brochure, because the entries record what arrived rather than what was promised.

## Terms That Travel Badly Between the Two Trades

**Direct answer:** Vocabulary borrowed from one wood's trade produces confident nonsense in the other's, and the confusion is a reliable sign that the speaker is selling rather than sourcing.

"Grade" is the clearest example. In agarwood it points at resin density and the 奇楠 distinction; in sandalwood it points at species, heartwood share, and age. A seller who says "top grade" without saying which ladder they are standing on has said nothing that can be checked.

"Old" is the second. Old agarwood means material that has aged in storage, which traders value; old sandalwood refers to 老山檀, the aged Indian heartwood tier, and the word carries a different weight in each trade. "Wild" applies meaningfully to agarwood, where forest collection is a legal category; for sandalwood the equivalent question is which species and which plantation or permit basis, not wild versus cultivated.

| Word | In agarwood trade | In sandalwood trade |
|---|---|---|
| Grade | Resin density band | Species, heartwood, age |
| Old | Long-stored material | The 老山檀 reference tier |
| Wild | Forest-collected, regulated | Not the operative distinction |
| Oil | Distilled from resinous wood | Distilled from heartwood |

A buyer who notices the borrowed vocabulary has learned something useful about the seller. The words are not evidence of fraud on their own; they are evidence that the seller is working from a script rather than from stock.


## Three Buyers, Three Different Answers

**Direct answer:** The same comparison resolves differently depending on what the buyer is doing with the wood, and naming the job first is what stops people from buying the wrong material expensively.

Take three cases. A reader who wants a room to smell of something warm in the evening, using a small electric heater, needs sandalwood: it releases steadily, forgives handling, and does not demand a reference collection to enjoy. A reader who wants a contemplative practice built around warmth and attention needs agarwood, because the unfolding is the point and a material that holds one line all evening defeats the purpose. A reader who wants to blend and sell needs both, plus the documentation that lets a batch be repeated.

| Buyer | Right first purchase | What would be a mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Evening room scent | Verified sandalwood chips | Expensive agarwood burned on a hot plate |
| Practice and attention | A mid-band agarwood chip | A single trophy piece that cannot be replaced |
| Blending for sale | Small lots of both, with records | One large lot of an unverified grade |

The pattern behind the three answers is that material follows use. A buyer who chooses by reputation rather than by job ends up with something precious they are afraid to warm, or something cheap they cannot build a habit around.

There is a fourth case worth naming, because it is common: the gift buyer. A gift benefits from a story and a container more than from rarity, and a well-documented mid-grade piece with a note explaining what it is will land better than an unidentified treasure. Rarity without explanation reads as a pebble in a box.

## What Changes When the Volume Rises

**Direct answer:** Moving from personal use to small-scale production changes which properties of the material matter, and the change is not mainly about price — it is about repeatability.

At personal scale, a single good chip is a complete purchase. At production scale, that chip becomes a reference, and the real question is whether a thousand like it can be obtained on a schedule. Consistency across lots matters more than peak quality, because a product that smells different every quarter loses the customers who came back for the smell.

Three practical consequences follow. The first is that documentation becomes load-bearing: lot codes, sealed references, and intake readings are what let a small producer explain a change instead of discovering it. The second is that a second approved material is worth having, because a single source that fails is a production stop. The third is that grade bands matter more than individual pieces, since a band can be specified and reordered while a beautiful anomaly cannot.

| Scale | What you optimise | What you accept |
|---|---|---|
| Personal | Character and pleasure | No repeatability |
| Small production | Repeatability within a band | Some loss of peak character |
| Larger production | Predictable supply and cost | Substitution within an agreed band |

The trade is visible in every growing incense business: the thing that made the early products distinctive is often the thing that cannot be bought twice. Planning for that transition early — keeping records from the first purchase, not the tenth — is what lets a maker move from one good batch to a line.


## Judging a Seller, Not Just a Sample

**Direct answer:** Two sellers can hand over identical chips and be very different suppliers, and the difference shows up in how they answer questions rather than in what they hand you.

A seller who has handled material answers specifics quickly. Ask which species and they name it without a pause; ask which district and they answer with a place rather than a country; ask about density band and they use the trade's own words. A seller working from a script answers the same question with adjectives: rare, premium, wild, ancient.

The second signal is what they volunteer. A supplier who mentions the moisture of a milled batch, or warns that the current lot is a little lighter than the last, is telling you they inspect their own stock. A supplier who never mentions a limitation is either very lucky or not looking.

| Signal | What it suggests | What to do with it |
|---|---|---|
| Names species and district unprompted | Works close to the material | Ask harder questions about consistency |
| Answers only with adjectives | Selling from a script | Ask for a lot-specific document |
| Volunteers a limitation | Inspects their own stock | Treat as a serious candidate |
| Refuses to discuss the last lot | Hides drift | Walk away |

The third signal is the willingness to lose a sale. A seller who tells you the material you asked for is not what you want, and offers something cheaper that suits the job, is a seller with a business rather than a transaction. That behaviour is worth more over years than any single price.

## What the First Purchase Should Actually Be

**Direct answer:** The first purchase should be small, verified, and slightly boring — because its real function is to establish a baseline, not to deliver a thrill.

Buy a quantity you can afford to keep as a reference rather than consume entirely. Buy the middle of a band rather than its edge, since a mid-band piece is easier to compare against future lots than a spectacular anomaly. Buy from a seller who will answer questions, even if the price is a little above the cheapest offer in the market, because the answers are part of what you are buying.

Then write down everything: species, district, band, form, weight, date, seller, and what the material did on gentle heat. That page becomes the standard against which the second purchase is judged, and the second purchase is where most buyers discover that "the same thing" from another seller is not the same thing.

The order matters. Buying small first is not caution for its own sake; it is the only way to build a reference before spending at a level where mistakes are expensive. A buyer with a reference can evaluate a large lot in an afternoon. A buyer without one is guessing at every scale.


## Where Each Wood Sits in the Aroma Families

**Direct answer:** Both woods belong to the resin-and-wood family rather than to the floral or citrus families, but they occupy different corners of it — agarwood at the dark, animalic edge, sandalwood at the creamy, balsamic one.

Mapping the two against the wider aroma wheel is useful because it tells a blender what will sit beside them. Agarwood pairs naturally with other deep materials — ambergris-like notes, dark resins, dry woods — and it overwhelms light florals if used heavily. Sandalwood sits closer to the centre of the wheel: it bridges florals and resins, which is why it appears in formulas that would otherwise have a gap between the top and the base.

| Family | Members it sits near | Relationship to the two woods |
|---|---|---|
| Resinous-woody | Agarwood, sandalwood, other heartwoods | Home territory for both |
| Balsamic-sweet | Benzoin, myrrh-adjacent materials | Sandalwood blends easily; agarwood can turn heavy |
| Floral | Osmanthus, jasmine, rose | Sandalwood bridges; agarwood usually buries them |
| Citrus-fresh | Bergamot, yuzu-type notes | Both outlast them; sandalwood is the gentler partner |

The practical reading is about proportion. A formula can carry a lot of sandalwood before it turns muddy, and very little agarwood before it dominates. That asymmetry, rather than any ranking of quality, is what most blending decisions actually turn on.

## Shipping, Permits, and the Border

**Direct answer:** The two materials carry different legal baggage — agarwood follows CITES species listings, sandalwood follows national export rules — and a purchase that ignores either becomes a warehouse problem rather than an aroma problem.

Agarwood's regulatory position attaches to the species. *Aquilaria malaccensis* and several relatives appear on the CITES appendices, which means an export permit must exist before the consignment travels, issued against a quota and tied to a specific shipment. A permit that names a different quantity, species, or consignee is not a substitute, and border inspectors are employed to notice precisely that.

Sandalwood's position is mostly national. India has long restricted export of *S. album*, which pushed legal supply toward Australian and Pacific species. Those species have their own rules, and the rules change. The buyer's protection is a supplier who names the species and the harvest basis without prompting, because a seller who cannot name the species cannot know which regulation applies.

| Material | Regulatory basis | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Agarwood (listed *Aquilaria*) | CITES appendices, export permits | Permit must precede shipment; lot-specific |
| Agarwood (unlisted species) | National forestry rules | Varies by country; ask for documentation |
| Indian *Santalum album* | National export restrictions | Legal volume is limited; substitutes dominate |
| Australian *S. spicatum* | National and state rules | Mainstream legal supply for most markets |

The timeline is where buyers get caught. Permits take time the shipping schedule does not account for, so the sensible order is species confirmation, permit application, permit number, freight booking, and only then a departure date. A container held at a border while paperwork is repaired is a container losing aroma in a hot terminal.

## What Changes When the Room Changes

**Direct answer:** The same chip behaves differently in a small room and a large one, and choosing between the two woods without accounting for the space is a common source of disappointment.

A small room concentrates everything. Sandalwood's steady warmth can become heavy in a closed bedroom, and agarwood's intensity can overwhelm a desk. In a large or well-ventilated space the opposite happens: sandalwood may barely register, and agarwood's development is lost because the aroma disperses before it can unfold.

| Space | Sandalwood | Agarwood |
|---|---|---|
| Small closed room | Use sparingly; can saturate | A sliver is enough |
| Medium living room | Comfortable at normal dose | Works well on a heater |
| Large or ventilated hall | Fades quickly | Needs more material or a warmer plate |

The practical rule is to buy for the room you actually have. A buyer who tests a chip in a shop's back office and then burns it in a cathedral will conclude the material was weak, when the material was fine and the space was wrong.


## Six Questions That Settle Most Confusion

**Direct answer:** Most arguments about these two woods collapse once six questions are answered in order, and the order matters because each answer narrows the next.

The first question is what the material is for: warmth in a room, attention in a practice, or a component in a formula. The second is what form is available to you, since a chip, a powder, and an oil are three different purchases. The third is which wood the seller claims, in species terms rather than adjectives. The fourth is what the piece does on gentle heat, judged against something you already know. The fifth is what paper follows the lot, which settles the legal layer. The sixth is what it costs per session rather than per gram, which is the only price that reflects how the material will actually be used.

| Question | Agarwood answer | Sandalwood answer |
|---|---|---|
| What is it for? | Depth, development, rarity | Warmth, steadiness, blending |
| What form? | Chips, powder, oil | Chips, powder, sticks, oil |
| Which species? | *Aquilaria*, named | *Santalum*, named |
| What does heat do? | Opens in stages | Holds one line |
| What paper? | CITES permit where listed | Harvest and export basis |
| What does it cost to use? | Small doses, long sessions | Larger doses, steady burn |

Answering them in that order prevents the most common error, which is starting with price. A figure arrived at before the first five questions is a figure about an unspecified object, and the cheapest unspecified object is never the one the buyer wanted.


## Traditional Use and Context

**Direct answer:** Both woods appear in classical materia medica as well as in incense — sandalwood the creamy staple, agarwood the deeper, rarer premium — but that language is traditional, not clinical.

- **Agarwood** appears in classical texts for "moving qi" — traditional language, not clinical evidence, and this page does not treat it as a treatment.
- **Sandalwood** appears as a standard aromatic wood in sticks, blended incense, and temple ritual.

Neither material is described here as having a therapeutic effect. The aroma characters are descriptive; any medicinal framing that floats around these woods online is not something this page repeats. A "medical grade" label on either wood does not mean it treats anything — it usually flags a grade or particle size, not a clinical use.

## Which Wood Do You Actually Need?

**Direct answer:** The useful choice is not "which wood is better" but "which role am I filling" — and that maps cleanly onto a few scenarios.

| If this is your case | Reach for | Because |
|---|---|---|
| You want the room to smell warm for an evening | Sandalwood | It releases slowly and evenly, with little drama |
| You want to sit with something that changes | Agarwood | The scent opens in stages as the plate warms |
| A formula needs a soft floor under bright notes | Sandalwood | Creamy material rounds the edges of sharper ingredients |
| A formula needs darkness at the base | Agarwood | Resin gives weight that nothing lighter can supply |
| You are buying without seeing the goods | Either, with more checking | Substitution risk is high for both, in different ways |

The table's real message is that the two woods are not competing for the same job. A buyer who picks sandalwood because it is "more affordable" and one who picks agarwood because it is "more rare" are both answering a different question than the one the wood itself asks.

## Verifying Before You Buy

**Direct answer:** With agarwood, settle species, district, density band, and authenticity before anyone mentions a figure; with sandalwood, settle which *Santalum* species, whether the goods are wood or oil, and where the harvest came from.

The agarwood list runs: species, district, plantation or forest, density band, form, moisture if milled, and the papers that follow the load. Aroma on gentle heat is the deciding test, because density can be engineered and paperwork can be copied, while a staged, changing scent is hard to fake convincingly.

The sandalwood list runs differently: species first, then wood versus oil, then harvest basis, then the buttery note on gentle heat. A seller who can name the species and explain why the price sits where it does is usually selling what the label says.

| Question | Agarwood | Sandalwood |
|---|---|---|
| Which plant? | *Aquilaria* species | *Santalum* species |
| Where from? | District and country | Country and harvest basis |
| What condition? | Density band, moisture | Heartwood share, oil content |
| What form? | Chip, powder, oil | Chip, powder, stick, oil |
| Legal layer? | CITES status and permits | Export restrictions by origin |

A label that names the species is the strongest single positive signal for either material. A label reading only "incense wood" or "natural fragrance" is the signal to slow down.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Treating the two woods as interchangeable.** They share the base register, which is not the same as sharing a job; one anchors with dark resin, the other with cream.
2. **Putting a flame to agarwood.** The staged aroma the material is bought for is destroyed at ignition temperature; it wants indirect heat.
3. **Mixing up 奇楠 and 老山檀.** The first is a material state inside agarwood; the second is a quality tier inside sandalwood.
4. **Skipping the identity check on either wood.** With agarwood you interrogate the timber itself; with sandalwood you interrogate the species claim and whether the goods are wood or oil.
5. **Confusing 檀香 with 紫檀.** Sandalwood and red sandalwood are unrelated plants that share a character, and the confusion is older than either of the English names.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** The single most useful fact in this comparison is not the price gap — it is that the two woods are faked *through different mechanisms*.

**Reasoning:**
- The counterfeiting channels do not overlap: one wood is faked as timber, the other as species or oil. A buyer who learns a single detection habit and applies it to both will miss about half of what is being done to them.
- The "which is more luxurious" framing is a trap. These are two base notes a single blend can carry side by side, not two fighters in a ring.

*Editorial view drawn from handling both materials, not a verified fact about any particular seller.*

## FAQ

### Q: Which one costs more — agarwood or sandalwood?
Agarwood at its ceiling, by a very wide margin. 奇楠 sits at the top of the whole aromatic market, while ordinary low-resin agarwood is genuinely cheap, which makes the spread inside the word enormous. Good *Santalum album* is expensive too, but its ceiling is held down by plantation supply and by Australian *S. spicatum* filling legal demand. So the honest answer is that the tops are far apart while the bottoms overlap.

### Q: Do they smell anything alike?
Not really. Agarwood is dark, resinous, and sweet, and it arrives in stages that change while you sit with it. Sandalwood is creamy, warm, and soft, and it stays where it starts. Both serve as base notes, which is where the confusion begins, but a base note is a position in a formula rather than a flavour.

### Q: What is the difference between 奇楠 and 老山檀?
They are not comparable categories. 奇楠 describes a state of agarwood so saturated with resin that the tissue is soft, sometimes pliable, and fragrant without heat. 老山檀 describes a tier of aged Indian *Santalum album* heartwood. One is a condition inside a wood; the other is a quality level inside a species.

### Q: How is each wood used in incense?
Both are warmed rather than burned directly. Agarwood chips sit on a plate above a heat source so the aroma can open in stages. Sandalwood can be warmed or burned slowly, and it holds a long, even, slightly cooked wood note that works well under brighter materials.

### Q: Which is faked more often?
Both are heavily faked, through different doors. Agarwood attracts dyed pale wood, unrelated timber, and oil-injected material built to sink. Sandalwood attracts species substitution, synthetic fragrance sold as wood, and oils that carry the name without the tree. Neither is safer; the verification habits simply differ.

### Q: Is sandalwood the same thing as red sandalwood?
No. 檀香 is the aromatic *Santalum*; 紫檀 is *Pterocarpus santalinus*, a red dye wood valued for colour. The shared character keeps the two names tangled in both languages, and the confusion predates the English names.

### Q: If the budget only stretches to one, which should come first?
Whichever job you actually have. A steady evening scent points to sandalwood, which is forgiving and does not need a reference collection to enjoy. A practice built around attention points to agarwood, where the unfolding is the point. Buy small, buy verified, and spend the remainder on checking what you bought.

### Q: Does a denser piece always mean better agarwood?
It means more resin by weight, which is one axis. A piece can sink and still smell flat, particularly if oil was forced into it, and a slightly lighter piece with a beautiful development can be the better purchase. Density is a useful first filter and a poor final verdict.

### Q: Can the two be swapped in a blend?
Only with the understanding that the result changes. Both anchor the base, but one anchors with dark resin and the other with cream. A formula written around agarwood's depth will read thinner on sandalwood, and a formula built on sandalwood's smoothness will read heavier on agarwood. They are partners, not equivalents.

## Summary

- Agarwood is resin-soaked heartwood formed by injury; sandalwood is fragrant heartwood present in the living tree — opposite origin stories.
- Agarwood's summit is 奇楠 (qínán); sandalwood's benchmark is 老山檀 (lǎoshān tán).
- Agarwood is graded by resin density, sandalwood by species, heartwood share, oil content, and age.
- Both are high substitution risk, but agarwood is faked as wood while sandalwood is faked as species or oil.
- Both reward gentle heat over flame, and neither is a medical treatment.

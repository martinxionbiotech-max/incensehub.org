**Direct answer:** Incense powder begins as dried plant matter — wood, gum, blossom or spice — which is cleaned, cut down to grains, passed through one screen, and then weighed against other ingredients. In that loose state it is used on its own as [loose incense powder](/blog/incense-powder-resin/); more often it is wetted and pressed into a stick, coil or cone, the paste route described in [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/).

**Key facts:** A formed stick, coil or cone is ground material first; its character was settled at the cutter, long before anyone rolled it. The sieve's choice then runs through everything — binding, paste behaviour, the way the piece burns.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Two careers from one pile.** Loose, it is the product; bound with water and makko, it becomes the body of a stick, coil or cone.
- **The order is fixed.** Sort, reduce, sift, weigh. No later stage can undo an earlier one.
- **Size is local, not universal.** The plant and the destination together decide the band.
- **Heat is the invisible cost.** Every cut adds warmth, and warmth is what carries the scent off.
- **A tight band beats a fine average.** Uniform grains burn predictably; flour mixed with grit does not, whatever the average says.

## First Stage: Sorting Settles the Material Question

**Direct answer:** Before anything is cut, the pile has to be named: which species, which part of the plant, and what condition it arrived in. Whatever the answer, the cutter will reproduce it faithfully.

No machine can repair a wrong label. Sapwood in gives sapwood out; a mix of two species in gives a mix out. Before the cutter starts, the material can still be disputed — afterward, only the jar remains. Three checks carry the weight:

- **Which plant.** A species name is an assertion, and assertions can be tested. The [incense authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) marks which claims yield to examination and which rest on the seller's word.
- **Which part.** Core wood against outer wood, bark against leaf, gum against the timber under it — one tree supplies four materials that behave in four ways.
- **What state it is in.** Wet, old, dusty, or kept beside something pungent: every one of those travels through the cutter and lands in the jar.

The commercial point follows directly: rejecting a bad lot is cheap before the mill and impossible after it. Powder removes the evidence that made the fraud visible — grain, colour, figure, the smell of a cut face. That is precisely why powder is the easier form to adulterate, and why the sorting bench is the buyer's real defence.

## Second Stage: Milling, and the Heat Nobody Budgets For

**Direct answer:** A cutter performs two operations, only one of them intended: it breaks material apart and it warms it. The unintended one decides how much scent survives.

Follow the warmth and the whole stage explains itself. **Metal bites into plant tissue → the bite converts motion into heat at the contact point → the material's lightest scent compounds turn to vapour before the powder is cool → what reaches the jar is a fraction lighter than what went in.** Nothing in that chain is visible. Two powders can look identical and differ entirely in what they retained.

The account is thermal before it is dimensional. A quick pass makes more cuts each second, banks warmth faster, and trades scent for minutes. Anything that pulls heat away is an ally: cold steel, a slack feed, a rest between passes, resin chilled before it meets the cutter. Reduce the material, yes — but reduce it while spending the least warmth you can. The same accounting reappears when [incense is dried and cured](/blog/how-incense-is-dried-and-cured/) further along the line.

Tissue type then dictates the setup, since no single setting suits four kinds of plant matter:

| Tissue | Behaviour at the cutter | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Hard core woods | Fibres stretch before they part, then fall as clean meal | Even feed; return the fibrous tail for another pass |
| Gums and resins | Heat makes brittle lumps sticky, then smeary | Chill first, pulse briefly, keep the head cold |
| Blossom and leaf | Fragile, scatters at once, loses scent as airborne dust | Gentle speed, short bursts, collect the flour |
| Fatty seeds and spice | Becomes a paste that jams the rotor | Very small loads; crush before reducing |

How far to reduce it is a judgment, not a specification. Dense, close-grained meal packs tight, keeps the ember low and runs cool; loose, gravelly meal gives more air, burns hotter and ends sooner. The right answer depends on the destination — a heater plate, a bound stick, a scattered trail — and one micron number cannot serve all three.

## Why Resins Fail in the Mill

**Direct answer:** Resin is the ingredient most likely to ruin a run, because the trait that gives it value — going soft when warm — is also the trait that gums the equipment.

The failure runs in one direction. **The chamber warms as it works → resin passes from glassy to tacky → the tacky mass coats the grinding surfaces → the chamber blocks and the batch leaves as paste.** At that point the loss is double: a spoiled lot and a machine that needs cleaning before anything else can be milled.

Cold breaks the chain at its first link. **Chilled resin stays glassy → it fractures instead of smearing → the surfaces stay clear → the meal stays free-running.** Many makers take a further step, reducing resin coarsely and then folding it through a dry wood meal that absorbs surface tack and keeps the fragments apart.

For a buyer, this yields one diagnostic and one warning. The diagnostic: a resin-rich jar that has clumped is usually evidence of a warm mill, not of a fake. The warning: a "resin" powder that flows like sand and shouts at room temperature may not be resin at all, but carrier with oil on it.

## Third Stage: Screening, and Why the Spread Matters More Than the Size

**Direct answer:** A sieve does not make powder finer; it makes the batch narrower. The point is to remove the outliers — chips that lag, dust that races — so the material inside a piece behaves the same from end to end.

Out of the cutter comes a crowd, not a grade: flour, grit and everything between in the same tray. What matters downstream is the width of that crowd, not its midpoint. Three failures follow from a wide range:

1. **The burn stumbles.** Grit holds an ember longer than flour; the line stalls on the coarse material and races through the fine.
2. **The blend drifts.** A large fragment carries a disproportionate share of whichever plant it came from, so the mixture is uneven before it is lit.
3. **The pack varies.** Different sizes compact to different densities along the same piece, which destabilises the burn all over again.

A sieve closes the gap. Grains under the chosen aperture drop through; the rest go back for another pass rather than into the jar. Which aperture is a judgment call, since a heater bed and a stick body are not built from the same band.

Two properties are routinely confused here, and separating them is the useful move:

| Property | What it governs | How it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Fineness | How fast and how hot the material burns | Too fine for loose use: the bed smothers itself and the scent flattens |
| Evenness | How steadily it burns | Wide band: the ember stalls and surges, and the blend separates |

A coarse lot, sieved hard, finishes quickly but evenly; an unsieved dust drags and stutters. Which is why a bad burn is rarely fixed by grinding smaller, and often fixed by making everything one size — a decision made at the screen, not the cutter.

## Moisture and Static Before the Mill

**Direct answer:** Two invisible conditions shape a grind before the cutting starts — how much water the material holds, and how much charge it carries — and both are cheaper to fix beforehand than to correct afterwards.

Damp tissue behaves badly under a mill. **Water makes fibres limp → limp fibre tears rather than cuts → the chamber clogs and the meal clumps → the spread of sizes widens and the burn inherits the unevenness.** Drying the material to a crisp, brittle state before milling is the standard remedy, and it also protects the scent fraction, since heat applied later to drive off water would carry scent with it.

Static is the second nuisance. **Particles rub against each other and the chamber → charge builds on the fines → fine material clings to walls, lid and sieve → the cut is uneven and the sieve blinds over.** Letting a batch rest, working in a slightly humid room, or earthing the equipment all reduce it. Neither problem is visible in the finished jar except as a signature: a powder that fights the sieve or clings in sheets has told you about the room it was milled in.

## Judging Uniformity in the Jar

**Direct answer:** Pour, look, rub and burn a pinch. Four checks, no instruments, and they say more than any claim printed on the container.

Tip a spoonful onto dark paper. A well-screened lot falls as a steady ribbon; a wide spread separates in the air, flour drifting while grit drops. Look at the heap: distinct grades show as bands of colour or texture. Rub a pinch — a narrow band feels the same throughout, a wide one alternates between silk and sand. Burn a small cone on a heat-safe surface and watch the ember line: steady advance means a tight spread, stalling and surging means the opposite.

No number comes out of this, and none is needed. The only question is whether the jar behaves as one material or as several.

## Fourth Stage: Combining, Where the Formula Actually Lives

**Direct answer:** Weighing the parts is the fourth step, and in Chinese practice it is also a design act: 合香 (hé xiāng) arranges materials under 君臣佐使, the chief, minister, assistant and guide, each with a job rather than a scent.

One ingredient is not a formula. A pure sandalwood or agarwood batch is finished by the sorting bench and the cutter; roles only appear when a second material joins it. The chief (君) decides what the blend is for. The minister (臣) supports that intention. The assistant (佐) softens or rounds it. The guide (使) points the result at a room or a time of day. [The combining system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) sets out that architecture in full.

Before it is creative, mixing is mechanical. **Densities and grain sizes differ between species → a quick stir leaves the heavy and fine at the bottom while the light and coarse rise → the jar stratifies → the spoon at the top and the spoon at the bottom are no longer the same blend.** So the fix is movement, not patience: tumble and turn the jar while filling it, and stir again before each use.

That yields a test a buyer can run without equipment. Tip the jar and look for bands of colour; stir before weighing out. Evenness within each ingredient says nothing about evenness across them, and a separated blend is a mixing failure, not a sign of age — resting will not bring it back.

Proportions remain the maker's private capital. What can be discussed is the structure: every material has an office, and the blend is balanced across offices instead of being led by whichever note shouts loudest.


## A Lexicon of Common Materials

**Direct answer:** The same eleven materials recur across Chinese incense work, and each has a name, a plant behind it, and a behaviour at the cutter.

| Chinese | Pinyin | Material | Working note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 檀香 | tán xiāng | Sandalwood heartwood | Fibrous, steady, keeps its scent well in storage |
| 沉香 | chén xiāng | Agarwood | Resinous and scarce; often blended with fibre to extend it |
| 乳香 | rǔ xiāng | Frankincense | Pale tears, brittle cold, sticky warm |
| 安息香 | ān xī xiāng | Benzoin | Softest of the gums; benefits from a dry meal in the chamber |
| 没药 | mò yào | Myrrh | Darker, denser, slower to soften than frankincense |
| 龙脑 | lóng nǎo | Borneol | Crystalline; volatile and easily lost to a hot pass |
| 桂皮 | guì pí | Cassia bark | Tough, slightly oily, grinds coarse before refining |
| 丁香 | dīng xiāng | Clove | Oil-heavy; behaves closer to resin than to wood |
| 藿香 | huò xiāng | Agastache | Leafy, light, quick to shed its note |
| 甘松 | gān sōng | Spikenard root | Earthy and dense; a little carries a long way |
| 艾草 | ài cǎo | Mugwort | Coarse leaf; often used for its smoke as much as its scent |

Read the table as a map of milling problems rather than a shopping list. The gums at the top soften; the leaf and root at the bottom scatter; the crystalline entry in the middle volatilises before the batch is cool. Each row implies a different feed rate, and none of them implies a number.

## Names for the Forms

**Direct answer:** Chinese practice separates the loose material from the shapes built out of it, and the vocabulary is worth knowing because catalogues use it untranslated.

Loose material is 香粉 (xiāng fěn), literally incense powder. Bound into a straight stick it becomes 线香 (xiàn xiāng), thread incense. Wound into a spiral it is 盘香 (pán xiāng), coil incense; pressed into a cone, 塔香 (tǎ xiāng), pagoda incense. A seal or character traced in loose material is 香篆 (xiāng zhuàn), and the gathering where material is judged and shared is 香席 (xiāng xí).

The distinction is commercial as well as cultural. A workshop tooled for 线香 may own no press for 塔香 and no winding frame for 盘香, so a quotation that names one form and a buyer who assumes another will produce a mismatch that looks like bad faith and is really a translation gap.

## Ground Wood or Scented Base: The Distinction That Follows the Powder

**Direct answer:** Two jars can carry the same word and hold different things: one ground from heartwood, the other a bland base sprayed with oil. Fingers struggle to tell them apart. A warm surface does not.

Here the material-versus-fragrance question sits one step further back, and it is harder to answer because a powder gives you almost nothing to inspect — no grain direction, no finish, no behaviour in the flame. The same comparison runs on a coarser material in [incense powder vs wood chips](/blog/incense-powder-vs-wood-chips/), and [what makes incense "natural"](/blog/what-makes-incense-natural/) looks at the vocabulary a careful label chooses.

| What you check | Milled plant material | Carrier plus oil |
|---|---|---|
| Smell in the jar | Subdued, dry, vegetal | Sharp, already at full volume |
| Smell over mild warmth | Unfolds, deepens, then recedes | Barely moves |
| Rubbed between fingers | Sandy, irregular, sometimes tacky | Slippery and even, like powder paint |
| Dropped into water | Clouds, sinks, keeps its plant smell | May film the surface |
| Cost against the plant | Follows scarcity | Follows the oil price |
| Printed detail | Species and part named | "Fragrance", or silence |

The second row is the test that matters. Plant tissue holds scent inside cells and gives it up as heat arrives; a dressed base has nothing to give up. Quiet when cold and expressive when warm is the signature of material. Loud when cold and flat when warm is the signature of something applied.

One caution on reading price alone: a very low price is a strong hint of a carrier, but a high price proves nothing, since expensive powder can be poorly milled material. Price narrows the question; only the label and the warm test answer it.

## Buying Powder or Milling Your Own

**Direct answer:** Most people should buy ready-milled powder from a supplier who names the species; milling at home is worth it when provenance or constant small-lot freshness matters more than time.

Doing it yourself has two genuine advantages. You see the material before it is reduced, so no substitution can hide behind a label, and you can mill a small quantity close to the moment of use, when the material is at its most expressive. Against that sits the cost: a cheap blade grinder runs hot and strips the top notes, a cool-running burr or stone mill costs more, and every batch needs cleaning and a little experimentation with feed rate.

| Your situation | Sensible choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You burn a little and want one dependable material | Buy, from a label naming species and part | The equipment never pays for itself |
| You blend and adjust often | Mill small lots | Iteration and freshness |
| You must know the material is what it claims | Buy whole material, reduce it yourself | Identity is checkable before the mill |
| You need a steady supply for years | Buy, but stay with one source | Continuity of grade matters more than novelty |

Put bluntly: buying buys convenience, milling buys control, and the two are not competing virtues. If you do mill, the binder question is a separate one, covered in [makko and natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

## What the Maker Writes Down

**Direct answer:** A usable batch note records six things — material, supplier, moisture, cutter setting, screen, and weight out — because without them a good result cannot be repeated and a bad one cannot be explained.

The list is short and unglamorous. **Material lot and supplier** make provenance traceable when a jar smells wrong months later. **Moisture at the start** explains why one batch ran hot and another did not. **Cutter setting and screen aperture** are the two numbers that fix the band, and therefore the burn. **Weight out against weight in** gives the yield, which is where a mill's losses become visible: fine dust that never reaches the jar, material left in the chamber, spillage during filling.

Written down, those six fields turn a craft into a process that can be corrected. Left in memory, they turn every batch into a fresh experiment and every fault into a mystery.

## Storage: Surface Area Is the Whole Story

**Direct answer:** Loose powder declines faster than a formed stick, simply because it exposes more surface to the air. A sealed jar, a dark cool shelf and no strong-smelling neighbours are the whole defence.

The arithmetic is simple. **Milling multiplies exposed surface → more surface means faster moisture exchange and faster oxidation → the scent thins and the powder grows reluctant to light → the change cannot be reversed.** A stick may hold its character for months; a powder can lose it in weeks.

The practice is plain. A screw-top jar or tin beats a folded bag; sunlight and warm equipment are both out; a desiccant helps in humid rooms and needs replacing once it is spent. Keep the jar clear of coffee, spices and cleaning products, since it takes on whatever odour sits next to it.

One habit is worth adopting: keep a small working jar and leave the reserve sealed. Every opening exchanges air with the entire contents, and the last spoonful from an often-opened jar is rarely the powder that was bought. [Incense storage and longevity](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) covers formed incense under the same conditions.

## Smoke, Ventilation and the Honest Caveat

**Direct answer:** Heat a pinch of powder and it still gives off fine particles and combustion gases — the loose form is not exempt. Air movement is therefore part of the process, not an afterthought to it.

Regulators treat indoor burning as a source worth managing. Both the [EPA's indoor air quality material](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq) and the [American Lung Association's pages on indoor pollutants](https://www.lung.org/clean-air/indoor-air/indoor-air-pollutants) discuss particulate levels and who is most exposed: people with asthma or other respiratory conditions, children, and anyone in a small closed room.

Practice follows: burn less at a time, keep a window or extractor open, do not fall asleep in a room where incense has just burned, and do not read a pleasant smell as clean air. A heater and loose powder generally produce less particulate than a smouldering stick, though "less" is not "none."

Nothing here is a health claim in either direction, and nothing here should be read as reassurance. The point is narrower: the craft and the air around it are one subject, and a page about milling should not leave the room out.

## The Mill Itself: Four Tools, Four Trade-offs

**Direct answer:** The device doing the cutting sets the heat, the spread and the loss, so choosing a mill is choosing which of those three you are willing to sacrifice.

| Tool | Heat produced | Size spread | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mortar and pestle | Almost none | Wide and coarse | Tiny lots, delicate petals, resins that must stay cold |
| Stone mill | Low to moderate | Fairly even | Traditional woods, steady small production |
| Burr grinder | Moderate | Narrow and repeatable | Consistent cuts across batches |
| Blade grinder | High | Widest, most erratic | Rough work only; it spends aroma for speed |

The pattern in that table is uncomfortable but reliable: the faster the tool, the more of the scent it costs. A mortar is tedious and preserves the most; a blade is quick and strips the lightest notes first. A burr sits between them, trading some heat for repeatability — which is often the right trade when the goal is a consistent blend rather than a single precious batch.

Scale changes the failure mode, not the principle: hand work drifts across a long session, while a line fights heat, because throughput rewards speed. So the question to ask of any powder is not "how fine" but "how cool did it run, and how tight is the spread."

## When a Burn Misbehaves

**Direct answer:** Most burn faults trace back to the powder, and the symptom usually names the cause.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| Ember keeps dying | Too coarse, or damp | Screen tighter; dry the material before milling |
| Burn races and finishes early | Too fine, or too much combustible binder | Open the cut; revisit the binder share |
| Smoke turns harsh and acrid | Overheated milling, or resin scorched in the chamber | Slow the feed; chill resin before grinding |
| Scent fades halfway down | Uneven blend, or fines burned off first | Re-mix; screen to one band |
| Stick splits or crumbles | Poorly sized powder packing unevenly | Narrow the spread before shaping |

Read the table as a diagnostic order rather than a list of equal options. Powder problems appear before shaping problems, and shaping problems appear before drying problems; working from the top down saves the effort of fixing the wrong stage.

## Three Chinese Terms for Processing

**Direct answer:** Chinese practice names three stages that English usually folds together: 炮制 (páo zhì), the preparation of raw material; 阴干 (yīn gān), drying in shade; and 陈化 (chén huà), deliberate ageing.

炮制 covers everything done to a material before it is used — cleaning, trimming, drying, sometimes gentle heating or soaking. The point is to make the material workable and predictable rather than to change what it is. 阴干 is the slower half of that: drying away from direct sun, so the material loses water without losing the fraction that sunlight and heat would carry off. 陈化 is the most contested. Makers describe aged material as rounder and less sharp, and the claim is traditional belief rather than a measured result; what is measurable is that storage conditions decide whether ageing helps or ruins a lot.

The three terms are useful even to a buyer who does not read Chinese, because they name three different promises. A supplier who says the material was shade-dried is making a claim about heat. One who says it was aged is making a claim about time. Each is a separate claim, and each deserves a separate question.

## Buying in Quantity: What the Packaging Has to Do

**Direct answer:** In bulk, packaging carries as much weight as contents. Powder that lands damp, smelling of its carton, or set into a brick lost its value on the way, not at the mill.

Three forces act on a shipment. **Humidity moves through paper and thin film → the meal takes on water → it clumps, dulls and burns badly → the loss is discovered only when the bag is opened.** **Odour migrates between materials → a strong cargo nearby leaves its mark → the jar smells of the warehouse rather than of the plant.** **Vibration compacts fine material → the load settles into a dense block → the top of the bag is dust and the bottom is stone.**

Good packing answers all three: a barrier layer rather than a single paper sack, an inner liner sealed before the outer bag is closed, and a carton that keeps its shape so the contents are not compressed by whatever sits above them. Headspace matters too, because air inside the package is water waiting to be absorbed.

A buyer can check the same things without opening every sack. Ask what the inner layer is made of. Ask whether the material is filled by weight or by volume, since a settled meal occupies less space for the same mass. Open one unit on arrival rather than the whole consignment, and judge before storage hides the evidence.

It is ordinary logistics, not an exotic problem — and it belongs beside the mill, because a good batch and a bad sack still end as bad powder.

## Specifying Powder to a Supplier

**Direct answer:** A powder order is only as good as its written terms, and five of them decide whether the jar that arrives matches the jar you approved.

Set out five things in writing: species and plant part, the band, what the powder is for, where the smell comes from, and the packing unit. A supplier can price against those terms, and a buyer can refuse a delivery that misses them. Vagueness buys a conversation; a written brief buys a specification.

Ask for a trial lot before a full run, and treat the trial as the only evidence that matters. A photograph of powder proves nothing, a sample chosen by hand proves less than a lot produced without special attention, and a certificate says nothing about the batch in front of you. What you want is a quantity made under normal conditions, then checked for band, smell and burn.

Expect the reply to include what cannot be promised. Powder varies with the harvest and the season, and a supplier who claims perfectly repeatable colour and aroma across years is either blending to hide variation or not paying attention. Consistency of behaviour is a reasonable ask; consistency of shade is not.

Keep the approved trial sealed and labelled. When a later delivery is disputed, that jar is the reference, and without it the argument becomes one opinion against another.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** Quality is settled at the mill, and the mill is the one stage a buyer almost never sees.

**Reasoning:**
- Everything after the sieve inherits from it. No blend rescues a scorched note, and no binder hides an uneven spread.
- The two questions worth asking a maker are not about fineness but about temperature and consistency: how warm did the cutter run, and how narrow is the band?

## FAQ

### Q: How is incense powder made?
Start by identifying the plant and rejecting what does not belong. Then cut it down, pass it through one screen, and weigh the parts against each other. From there the route splits: straight onto a heater, or into a dough with makko and water that is shaped and left to dry. Species and band width count for more than the machinery used.

### Q: How fine should the powder be?
There is no universal figure. A dense meal holds a low ember for a long time; a gravelly one runs hot and finishes early. Powder for a heater and powder for a stick are cut differently, and consistency inside a jar counts for more than the number on a spec sheet.

### Q: Why does resin need chilling before it is milled?
A cutter turns motion into heat, and heat is what turns resin from glassy to tacky. Once tacky it coats the working surfaces, blocks the chamber and turns the batch into paste. Cold keeps it brittle, so it fractures instead of smearing. A clumped resin powder points to a warm mill before it points to a fake.

### Q: Is the powder in the jar always the plant on the label?
No. Ground heartwood and an inert carrier dosed with oil can share a name and a look. Warmth separates them: real tissue stays quiet cold and opens as heat arrives, while a dressed base is loud from the jar and barely changes when warmed. If the label names a scent rather than a species, the claim is unproven.

### Q: How should I keep powder at home?
Sealed, dark and cool, with nothing fragrant nearby. A screw-top jar beats a folded bag, and a desiccant earns its place in humid weather. Keep the jar in use small and the reserve closed, because every opening trades air with the whole contents. Once the edge is gone, nothing brings it back.

## Summary

Powder follows a fixed order — **sort → mill → screen → combine** — and the second and third steps settle most of the finished quality. Heat is the variable no spec sheet records: friction warms the meal, warmth drives off the lightest scent compounds, and the loss only shows when the powder is warmed weeks later. Evenness is the second quiet variable; a narrow band burns smoothly, blends evenly and packs consistently, while a wide spread fails in three ways at once.

What a buyer needs is short: species and plant part on the label, a clear answer on whether the smell is botanical or added, a finger-and-warmth test, and a sealed jar afterward. No instruments required — only the assumption that the powder was made with equal care.

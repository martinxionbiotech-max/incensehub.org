**Technical answer:** Agarwood (沉香 chénxiāng) is the resin-laden heartwood of *Aquilaria* trees, produced when a tree answers injury or fungal attack by laying down fragrant resin. It holds the top seat in the classical incense tradition for its deep, sweet, resinous scent — and its price is so steep that it ranks among the most-counterfeited aromatics anywhere.

**Key facts:** 沉香 means "sinking fragrance" — drop a piece dense enough in resin into water and it goes under. The summit grade carries the name 奇楠 (qínán, a cognate of the Japanese "kyara"), and it parts ways with ordinary agarwood in a specific, under-documented manner.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Agarwood = resin-laden **Aquilaria** wood (Thymelaeaceae), a deposit built up over years to decades after wounding or infection.
- The name **沉香 means "sinking fragrance"** — wood dense with resin outweighs the water it displaces.
- **奇楠 (qínán)** is the summit grade, set apart from ordinary 沉香 by the **"oil-wraps-wood" vs "wood-wraps-oil"** split (unpacked below) — a distinction English-language writing largely skips.
- Agarwood is **heavily adulterated**; the ability to authenticate it is worth more than the ability to name it.
- Traditional Chinese medicinal wording about agarwood is **historical record, not modern clinical evidence.**

## How the Resin Forms: The Stress Story

**Direct answer:** Healthy *Aquilaria* wood is pale, light, and almost scentless; the resin that makes it agarwood is a defense response — the tree seals its own wounds with fragrant resin over years to decades.

The formation mechanism, as a chain:

tree is wounded (broken branch, insect boring, fungal infection, or deliberate cutting) → the tree deposits dark resin to seal and protect the wound → resin accumulates in the surrounding heartwood over years → the wood darkens, densifies, and becomes fragrant → the resin-bearing heartwood is harvested.

Because natural agarwood forms slowly and unpredictably, most of today's supply is cultivated — trees are deliberately wounded or inoculated to trigger resin, then harvested after a span of years. "Natural" versus "cultivated" therefore marks a real split in the market, not a cosmetic label.

## What Is Agarwood, Botanically

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is resin-saturated heartwood of *Aquilaria* trees (Thymelaeaceae), chiefly *Aquilaria sinensis* (Chinese agarwood, 白木香) and *Aquilaria malaccensis* (the classic import, now heavily depleted).

The prized material forms only when the tree is stressed. Two names dominate the Chinese tradition:

- ***Aquilaria sinensis*** (白木香, "white-wood fragrance") — the Chinese agarwood, a tree of southern China (Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, Yunnan) and, in earlier times, the homegrown source of 沉香.
- ***Aquilaria malaccensis*** — the classic import that traders brought out of mainland and maritime Southeast Asia, now badly depleted and on the CITES list.

Outside those two, further *Aquilaria* species and the sister genus *Gyrinops* also carry resin, all of them traded beneath overlapping names. The [Flora of China entry for Aquilaria](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=102387) sets down the botanical record; trade names and species names refuse to line up cleanly, and that looseness is one reason provenance matters to a careful buyer.

## Why It's Called "Sinking Fragrance"

**Direct answer:** 沉香 (chénxiāng) reads literally as "sinking fragrance," and the logic is physical — a piece dense enough in resin outweighs water and sinks, while lightly resinated wood stays on the surface.

The mechanism is plain density:

resin packs the spaces between the wood fibers → the piece grows denser than water → it sinks → it ranks highest.

Out of this comes the classical Chinese grading ladder (the 沉水 system), which English sources document surprisingly thinly:

| Grade | Behavior in water | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| 沉水 (sinks) | Fully submerged | Highest resin content, most valuable |
| 半沉半浮 (half-sinks) | Partially submerged | Medium |
| 浮 (floats) | Stays afloat | Lower (historically "黄熟香") |

The sink test measures resin *concentration* — a useful first screen, but only part of the picture, as the next section shows.

## qinan (奇楠) vs. Ordinary Agarwood: The Distinction English Misses

**Direct answer:** The single most useful thing to know about agarwood — and the point most English content skips — is the structural split between ordinary 沉香 and the summit grade, 奇楠 (qínán, Japanese "kyara").

- **Ordinary 沉香 runs as "wood wraps oil" (木包油).** Firm timber, with the resin showing only as veins inside.
- **奇楠 runs as "oil wraps wood" (油包木).** The resin saturates the fiber so thoroughly that the material turns soft and pliable. The traditional description is that it **"shaves into curls and kneads into pellets" (削之成卷、捏之成丸)** — and it smells even before any heat touches it.

This is a different state of matter, not a richer version of the same thing. The five traditional color grades of 奇楠 run 白 (white), 绿 (green), 紫 (purple), 黄 (yellow), 黑 (black). The sources are split over whether the purple and yellow tiers are genuine grades or merely the green tier at a later aging stage, so treat the color ladder as traditional rather than settled science.

**Etymology note:** the word 奇楠 resists a firm origin. The candidates floated over the years run from Sanskrit "Kāla" (black) to Sanskrit "tagara" (a fragrant plant) to a "temple" reading. Some scholarship argues "tagara" is a mistranslation and that the Sanskrit for agarwood is "agaru/aguru." The honest summary is "etymology uncertain; multiple theories."

## The Aroma: Three Stages on Heat

**Direct answer:** Agarwood's reputation rests on how its scent unfolds in stages as it warms, not on one burst of smell — an opening, a body, and a dry-down.

The classical vocabulary names three stages:

- **头香 (tóu xiāng, "head/opening")** — the first note to reach the nose as the piece starts to give off scent.
- **本香 (běn xiāng, "body")** — the long, steady middle character.
- **尾香 (wěi xiāng, "tail/dry-down")** — the deep, fading note that lingers last.

Because the release is staged, agarwood is traditionally warmed (隔火焚香) rather than burned: an open flame destroys the delicate top notes before the nose ever meets them. The mechanism runs — gentle heat releases the outer resin first → the warmth works inward → deeper resin releases later → the aroma opens in stages. High-grade 奇楠 is fragrant even cold; ordinary agarwood needs gentle warmth to open up.

## Adulteration: How to Spot Fakes

**Direct answer:** Because agarwood commands such a price, fakes are common — and the fakes aim squarely at the same signals the grading system leans on.

The common methods:

- **oil-boiling (煮油)** — simmering ordinary wood in oil to load it with extra weight and scent.
- **high-pressure oil injection (高压灌油)** — pumping oil into wood that has no resin of its own.
- **chemical/dye soaking (泡药水)** — dyeing and scenting plain "white wood" to darken it.
- **Substitution** — using unrelated woods (Lauraceae, Burseraceae) dressed with synthetic fragrance.

The attack chain is instructive:

oil or dye is added → the wood darkens and gains weight → it sinks like dense resin wood → the sink test passes → the fake reads as a high grade.

That is why a grading label alone is a claim, not a proof. Four traditional signals screen the material before any lab work:

- **Burn behavior:** a genuine piece burns to white smoke with oil weeping out; fakes tend to burn to black smoke.
- **Aroma on gentle heat:** warm a real chip and you get a complex, sweet profile; warm a fake and you get burnt wood or perfume.
- **The sink test:** a resin-heavy piece goes under (ordinary 沉香 grading).
- **No perfume note:** a one-note "perfume" smell points to synthetic fragrance.

None of these is decisive on its own; laboratory or a reputable seller is the reliable route for real confidence. ## Traditional Medicine Claims: Historical Record, Not Treatment

**Direct answer:** The classical materia medica links agarwood to phrases like "行气止痛，温中降逆，补五脏" — move qi, ease pain, warm the middle, tonify the organs. That is language from the old texts, not a finding from modern clinics.

IncenseHub treats these as historical notes, not medical guidance. They are preserved here under the label of traditional belief, keeping the cultural context intact without making any health claim.

## Sustainability and Conservation

**Direct answer:** Wild *Aquilaria* has been so heavily harvested that agarwood-producing species are listed under CITES, which is why cultivated plantation agarwood has grown — and why a reputable seller should be able to name the wood's origin and whether it was cultivated or wild.

Agarwood's source trees appear on the [CITES](https://checklist.cites.org/#/en/search/output_layout=alphabetical&level_of_listing=0&show_synonyms=1&show_author=1&show_english=1&show_spanish=1&show_french=1&scientific_name=Aquilaria) list, which governs cross-border movement of the wood. Scarcity, regulation, and demand each lift the price.

## Authenticating Agarwood: A Practical Checklist

**Direct answer:** For a first purchase, keep the checks small and concrete — species, cultivation status, warm aroma, and price.

1. **Ask the species and origin.** A seller who cannot name both is giving you a signal, not an answer.
2. **Cultivated or wild?** Both are legitimate; what differs sharply is the price and what you can expect.
3. **Smell it warm, not just cold.** The honest test is the aroma under gentle heat — a room-temperature sniff tells you little.
4. **Watch the price.** A very cheap "agarwood" or "oud" is virtually never genuine.
5. **Confirm before spending heavily.** For costly pieces, lean on a laboratory or a reputable seller's documented check.

---

## Common Mistakes

1. **Treating every agarwood as interchangeable.** 奇楠 and ordinary 沉香 are different materials (oil-wraps-wood vs wood-wraps-oil).
2. **Reading the TCM phrases as established medicine.** They are historical record, not clinical proof.
3. **Pricing as the only signal.** Cheap "agarwood" is almost always adulterated or substituted.
4. **Hoping for a big smoky room-fill.** The top grade is traditionally enjoyed by gentle, often flameless warming (隔火焚香), not by a large smoky burn.

## FAQ

### Q: How is agarwood different from sandalwood?
Two aromatics from two unrelated trees. Agarwood is *Aquilaria* heartwood that has taken on resin after an injury — the note runs deep, sweet, and resinous, and it opens in stages. Sandalwood is *Santalum* heartwood that is naturally scented — creamy, smooth, sweet-woody, and even. Agarwood also carries the heavier fakery risk and the more elaborate grading ladder (沉水 and 奇楠) built on resin density. See the [sandalwood guide](/blog/sandalwood-incense/), or the [agarwood vs frankincense](/blog/agarwood-vs-frankincense/) comparison.

### Q: What is kyara (奇楠)?
The summit rung of agarwood, and the tell is structural: "oil wraps wood" (油包木). Resin saturates the fiber so completely that the piece goes soft and pliable, curls under a blade, kneads into a pellet, and smells even before any heat is applied. Ordinary 沉香 works the other way — "wood wraps oil" (木包油) — firm timber threaded with resin veins. By color the grade divides (traditionally) into white, green, purple, yellow, and black, a scheme the sources hold as customary rather than firm taxonomy. "Kyara" is the Japanese term for the same summit.

### Q: Why does some agarwood sink and some float?
It comes down to how much resin the wood holds. 沉香 means "sinking fragrance" precisely because the most resin-saturated pieces are heavy enough to sink, while resin-poor wood stays afloat — that simple test is the spine of the classical 沉水 ladder. The reasoning is direct: resin is denser than wood. The same fact is why the sink test alone cannot catch oil-injected fakes, since those are built to sink on purpose.

### Q: How do I know agarwood is real?
Ask the seller to name species and origin before anything else, and for costly material get laboratory or documented confirmation. The traditional checks are heuristics, not proofs: a genuine piece gives white smoke with oil seepage when burned (fakes tend toward black smoke), a complex sweet aroma under gentle heat rather than a burnt-wood or single-note perfume smell, and no synthetic perfume note. A prestige grade selling at a bargain price is itself a red flag. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

## More Questions Answered

### Q: What does agarwood (沉香) smell like?
Picture a sweet, resinous note that sits deep rather than bright, carried on a woody undercurrent — and instead of arriving all at once, it climbs in stages as the heat rises: an opening (头香), a body (本香), and a dry-down (尾香). Most noses call the overall character warm and enclosing, not sharp. The top grade 奇楠 gives scent even cold, while ordinary agarwood needs a gentle warm before its full profile opens. That layered, time-released quality is the opposite of a flat single note, and it is much of why agarwood rewards slow, flameless appreciation.

### Q: Why is agarwood so expensive?
Three pressures converge on the price. The resin takes years to decades to form, and only after the tree is injured or infected; wild stands have been so heavily cut that the species now sit under CITES; and the material is hard to verify, so demand runs ahead of trust. Ordinary medicinal-grade chips stay comparatively cheap (a few RMB per gram), but dense 沉水 material, and above all 奇楠, climb to very high prices. The gulf between grades is vast, which is exactly why the market pulls in fakes.

### Q: Is agarwood the same as oud?
Mostly yes — one material wearing several names. "Oud" (also "oudh") is the Arabic and Middle-Eastern word for the same resin-laden *Aquilaria* wood that goes by 沉香 in Chinese and jinkō/kyara in Japanese. One plant family, many names, each with its own grading vocabulary. The caution travels with every name: a low-priced "oud," or one offered without species and origin, is a familiar hiding place for adulteration and synthetic substitutes.

### Q: How do I burn agarwood chips?
You warm them, you do not light them. The refined route is 隔火焚香, "separated-fire" heating: hot charcoal goes under a layer of ash, a thin mica or metal plate rests on top, and the chip sits on that plate, releasing its aroma well below the temperature where it would catch fire. An electric incense heater achieves the same thing with less fuss. A direct flame is the mistake to avoid — it burns away the delicate opening notes, the exact part of the experience you bought the wood for.

### Q: How can I tell if agarwood is fake?
Start with the commercial clues, then the sensory ones. The old checks are heuristics, not proofs: a genuine piece gives white smoke with oil seepage when burned (fakes lean toward black smoke), a complex sweet aroma on gentle heat rather than a burnt-wood or single-note perfume smell, and resin-dense pieces that sink. The one strongest clue is commercial — a prestige grade at a very low price, or a seller who cannot name species and origin. For anything costly, lean on laboratory or documented seller verification. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

### Q: Why is my agarwood chip barely fragrant when I light it?
A direct flame vaults past the very stage that makes good agarwood worth burning slowly. The appeal is a staged release — opening, body, dry-down — and that sequence needs gentle, rising heat to unfold. A flame skips the opening in an instant, burns the delicate top notes before they reach you, and leaves a harsher, flatter burn. The refined route is indirect heat (隔火焚香): warm the chip just below its ignition point, on a mica plate over buried charcoal or an electric heater, and the layers open in order. A chip that reads thin and one-note is usually a flame problem, not a material problem.

## The Resin Saturation Ladder: From Pale Wood to 奇楠

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is not one substance but a ladder of resin saturation, and every rung of the ladder — from pale, nearly scentless wood to the soft, room-temperature-fragrant summit — is the same tree at a different point in the same slow process.

Walk the ladder as a single continuous chain. A healthy, unstressed tree holds pale, light heartwood that is almost odorless → a wound or infection triggers the defense response → the tree deposits dark resin around the wound site → the resin spreads and accumulates through the surrounding fiber over years → the wood darkens, densifies, and begins to carry scent → at the low rungs it is ordinary 沉香 with resin veins in hard wood → at the summit the saturation runs so complete that the resin becomes the continuous phase and the wood turns soft, pliable, and fragrant at room temperature, which is 奇楠. The ladder is continuous, not stepwise; the named grades are markers placed by people on a curve the tree drew. That is why the grading vocabulary (沉水, 奇楠, the five colors) reads as a set of thresholds rather than distinct species — same tree, different saturation, different value.

## A Confusion Table: Agarwood, Sandalwood, and the Woods That Pretend

**Direct answer:** Agarwood gets confused with two different things — its legitimate cousin sandalwood, and the unrelated substitute woods that are sold *as* agarwood — and the two confusions have opposite causes and opposite cures.

| Signal | Agarwood (沉香) | Sandalwood (檀香) | Substitute "agarwood" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Botanical source | *Aquilaria* heartwood, resin-formed after stress | *Santalum* heartwood, naturally aromatic | Unrelated woods (Lauraceae, Burseraceae) |
| Aroma | Deep, sweet, resinous, staged | Creamy, sweet-woody, smooth | Flat, perfumed, or synthetic |
| How it forms | Wounding or infection over years–decades | Normal heartwood of a mature tree | N/A — dressed up, not formed |
| Grading | 沉水, 奇楠, the color ladder | Density and age | None genuine |
| Why it is confused | Shared warm woody register | Shared warm woody register | Deliberate substitution |

The table splits two problems that sound alike. Sandalwood is a real, honorable material that simply smells different; the fix is education, not suspicion. Substitute wood is a deliberate fraud dressed to pass as agarwood; the fix is authentication, not comparison. A collector who confuses the two problems will either distrust an innocent sandalwood stick or trust a dressed-up fake — both are mistakes, and they sit on opposite sides of the same word.

## A Misconception: "Burning Agarwood Is How You Appreciate It"

**Direct answer:** The common instinct to burn a good agarwood chip on a flame is backwards — a direct fire destroys the delicate staged opening that is the whole reason the material is prized, and the refined tradition warms it without burning it at all.

The misconception treats agarwood like any other wood: light it, enjoy the smoke. The material refuses the treatment. Its value is in a staged release — an opening, a body, a dry-down — and that sequence exists only under gentle, rising heat. A direct flame vaults past the opening stage in an instant → the delicate top notes are destroyed before they can be smelled → what remains is a harsher, flatter burn → the material that was bought for its layers delivers only one. The refined route (隔火焚香) holds the chip just below its ignition point so the stages unfold slowly. The boundary to hold: agarwood is appreciated by *heat*, not by *fire* — and a buyer who reaches for a lighter to "really smell it" is skipping the very experience they paid for.

## The Price Ladder as a Resin Fraction

**Direct answer:** The enormous price spread inside the agarwood market is not hype — it is a direct readout of how much resin a given piece carries, because the resin, not the wood, is the scarce and fragrant part being sold.

Trace the economics as a chain. Ordinary *Aquilaria* heartwood is cheap because every mature tree has some → the resin that turns it into 沉香 forms only after wounding or infection and takes years to accumulate → the more completely the resin saturates the fiber, the heavier, darker, and more fragrant the piece becomes → pieces fall onto a ladder where each step up in resin fraction multiplies the value → the summit, 奇楠, is so saturated the wood behaves like a different material → the price follows the resin fraction almost one-for-one. A few-RMB medicinal chip and a many-thousand-RMB summit piece are the same species at opposite ends of one fraction. That is why "how much resin" is the real question behind every agarwood price, and why a bargain on a high-fraction grade reads as a contradiction rather than a deal.

## A Use-vs-Grade Substitution Matrix

**Direct answer:** Different grades of agarwood suit different uses, and a table that maps each grade to what it is good for prevents both waste and disappointment.

| Grade (by resin fraction) | Typical use | What you should not do with it |
|---|---|---|
| Medicinal chips (low resin) | Herbal decoction, low-cost bulk | Expect a room-filling scent — it is near-scentless |
| Burnable mid-grade | Everyday stick or direct burn | Pay summit prices for it |
| 沉水 dense material | Gentle heating, careful appreciation | Waste it on a hot, smoky flame |
| 奇楠 summit | Slow, flameless appreciation; collection | Burn it casually as room fragrance |

The matrix is a budgeting tool as much as a tasting guide. A collector who buys summit 奇楠 and lights it like a cheap stick has destroyed the staged opening that justified the price; a beginner who buys low-resin chips expecting a deep aroma has bought the wrong grade for the wrong job. Match the grade to the use, and the price ladder stops feeling arbitrary and starts reading as a menu.

## A Misconception: "Darker and Heavier Always Means Better"

**Direct answer:** Dark color and heavy weight are real signals of resin — but they are also the two properties a forger engineers first, so treating them as proof mistakes an easy-to-fake signal for an honest one.

Genuine agarwood darkens and densifies as resin accumulates, so a buyer learns to reach for the dark, heavy piece. The faker reaches for the same two cues: oil-boiling and high-pressure injection both darken and weigh the wood down → the piece sinks and looks saturated → the two signals that once meant "resin-rich" now mean only "has been treated to look resin-rich." The boundary to hold: darkness and density are *consistent with* high grade, never *proof of* it. A collector who grades by eye and weight alone is grading the forger's craft. The reliable check is behavior — how the aroma opens on gentle heat — because that is the one property a surface treatment cannot convincingly fake.

## Regional Agarwood: Why Origin Changes the Scent

**Direct answer:** Agarwood from different growing regions carries different aromatic signatures, because soil, climate, and the local *Aquilaria* species steer how the tree builds its resin — which is why a named origin is a scent claim, not a brand flourish.

A regional cross-reference, kept as directional rather than definitive:

| Origin | Common species | Aromatic tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Hainan (海南) | *A. sinensis* | Clean, sweet, cool — the classical Chinese reference |
| Vietnam (Nha Trang, Hue) | *A. crassna* | Deep, sweet, complex — the classic import |
| Cambodia (Pursat) | *A. crassna* | Sweet, fruity, honeyed |
| Malaysia / Indonesia | *A. malaccensis*, *A. microcarpa* | Earthy, woody, sometimes smoky |

The honest caveat runs both ways: origin is a real aroma signal, and it is also a label a forger can print. The same region name on a cheap piece is a claim to verify against the seller's accountability, not a fact to accept at face value. Treat origin the way you treat the species name — as something a reputable seller should be able to state and stand behind, and as one more thread in the authentication web rather than a proof on its own.

## The Five-Flavor Reading: A Traditional Aroma Vocabulary

**Direct answer:** The Chinese tradition reads agarwood through a five-taste vocabulary — 甜 (sweet), 凉 (cool/menthol), 辛 (spicy), 酸 (sour), and 苦 (bitter) — and a piece is judged by which of these notes it carries and in what order they arrive.

This is a traditional descriptive framework, not a laboratory measurement, and it is best treated as a shared language rather than a fixed spec. A piece might open cool and minty → settle into a sweet, honeyed body → close with a faint bitter-woody residue. Another might read spicy and hot throughout. The five terms give collectors a way to compare pieces that the English word "sweet" alone cannot carry — a Hainan piece's cool-sweet and a Cambodian piece's fruity-sweet are two different sweets, and the five-flavor grid is what separates them. It is *traditional belief* as a grading aid, not a settled sensory science, and it works best as a lens for your own nose rather than a score someone else assigned.

## Anatomy of the Resin: What the Wood Is Doing Underneath

**Direct answer:** The resin does not sit on the wood like a coating — it infiltrates the tree's transport tissue, filling the spaces the living tree once used to move water and nutrients, and that infiltration is what changes both the density and the scent.

Look at the biology in causal order. A living *Aquilaria* tree moves water through hollow vessels and fibers in its heartwood and sapwood → when the tree is wounded or infected, it secretes resin into those very spaces → the resin plugs the vessels and hardens there → the plugged wood grows denser and heavier → the trapped resin is what carries the aroma, released only when heat or solvent frees it. This is why agarwood is described as resin-*saturated* rather than resin-*coated*: the oil is inside the structure, not on top of it. It is also why a surface treatment can never fully imitate the real thing — an injected fake puts oil into the outer zones only, while genuine saturation runs through the fiber, which is the property gentle heat and time reveal.

## Cultivated vs Wild: Two Routes to the Same Resin

**Direct answer:** Agarwood reaches the market by two different paths — wild harvest from stressed trees, and plantation cultivation where trees are deliberately wounded or inoculated — and the two differ in supply, price, and what a seller can honestly claim, not in the resin's basic chemistry.

Lay the two routes side by side as cause-and-effect. A wild tree meets injury or infection by chance → the resin forms unpredictably over years to decades → the piece is found and harvested, often old and deeply saturated, and is scarce and costly. A plantation tree is wounded on purpose → a maker drills, nails, or inoculates it with a fungal culture → the tree responds with resin over a few years → the piece is harvested on a schedule, younger and usually lighter in saturation, at a far lower price. The resin the two trees produce is the same class of material; what changes is age, saturation depth, and cost. For a buyer the honest question is not "wild or cultivated?" as a value judgment, but "does the seller state which, and is the price consistent with it?" A cultivated piece sold as wild, or a young piece sold as old, is a provenance claim to hold the seller to.

## Reading a Piece Before You Buy: Grain, Weight, and Sheen

**Direct answer:** A physical piece telegraphs three useful things before any test — the evenness of its grain, its weight in the hand, and whether its surface reads as oily or dry — and reading all three together is a better first filter than any single rule.

| What to look at | What you see | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Grain | Fine, even, interlacing fibers | Wood that took resin throughout |
| Grain | Coarse, open, uniform pale fiber | Low-resin, younger, or treated white wood |
| Weight | Unusually heavy for its size | Dense — consistent with high resin, or with oil-injection |
| Weight | Light and airy | Low resin fraction |
| Sheen | A dull, natural, waxy luster | Genuine resin in the fiber |
| Sheen | A glossy, wet, uniform shine | Possible surface oil or polish treatment |

The table's central lesson is that no single row decides anything — it is the *agreement* between grain, weight, and sheen that matters. A piece that is heavy, even-grained, and dully waxy reads as genuinely saturated; a piece that is heavy but pale and glossy reads as treated. Weight without sheen, or sheen without weight, is where the forger's hand shows. Read the three together and the piece mostly tells you which test to trust next.

## The Naming Cross-Reference: Agarwood Across Traditions

**Direct answer:** The same resin-bearing wood carries several names across traditions — 沉香 in Chinese, oud or oudh in Arabic, kyara and jinkō in Japanese — and the names index different grading vocabularies more than different plants.

| Tradition | Term | What it refers to |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese | 沉香 (chénxiāng) | The resin-saturated wood, graded by sinking and by 奇楠 |
| Arabic / Middle Eastern | oud / oudh | The same material, central to bakhoor and attar |
| Japanese | jinkō (沈香) | The general term, in the kōdō incense ceremony |
| Japanese | kyara (伽罗) | The summit grade, cognate with 奇楠 |

The cross-reference matters because a buyer who shops "oud" in one market and "agarwood" in another is buying the same family under two vocabularies that do not map one-to-one. "Oud" and "agarwood" are the broad term; "kyara" and "奇楠" are the summit grade, and the step from one to the other is the price gap the fakes exploit. The same caution follows every name: a low price on a prestige term, or a seller who will not name species and origin, is the warning sign regardless of which language the label is printed in.

## A Misconception: "All Agarwood Smells the Same"

**Direct answer:** The belief that agarwood is one smell flattens a material whose whole appeal is its range — and a buyer who holds it will treat a Hainan piece and a Cambodian piece as interchangeable when they are almost opposite experiences.

Agarwood is not a single note but a family of profiles: cool and sweet from one region, fruity and honeyed from another, earthy and smoky from a third, with the summit grade fragrant even unheated and the low grade almost scentless until warmed. The misconception survives because most people meet the material only once, in a single stick or chip, and generalize from that one encounter. The practical consequence is a missed purchase or a wrong one: someone who loved a fruity Cambodian piece and then buys a smoky Malaysian one expecting the same scent will be disappointed, and will blame the grade rather than the region. The boundary to hold: agarwood is a *range*, not a *flavor*, and the first question before buying is not "is it good agarwood" but "which agarwood."

## Distillation and the Aromatic Chemistry Underneath

**Direct answer:** The scent of agarwood is not one molecule but a blend of volatile and semi-volatile compounds — sesquiterpenes and chromone derivatives — and it is the balance among these that separates a flat, cheap piece from a layered, expensive one.

The chemistry reads as a chain from stress to smell. When the tree is wounded, its defense response produces dark, fragrant resin → that resin is rich in compounds the wood itself did not hold before → the most abundant classes are sesquiterpenes (woody, balsamic) and chromones (sweet, resinous) → a piece's character depends on which of these dominate → a high-grade piece holds a wide, balanced spread, while a low-grade or treated piece leans thin and one-note. This is why distillation of agarwood into oil (oud oil) is a different craft from burning the wood: heat or steam separates the volatile fraction into a liquid, while burning releases the same compounds into smoke, and the two routes favor different parts of the profile. The chemistry is recorded in specialist reviews rather than settled consumer knowledge, and the honest framing is that the *profile* — not any single molecule — is what the nose grades.

## A Misconception: "Sinking Grade Is the Only Grade Worth Buying"

**Direct answer:** The sink test sorts resin density, not enjoyability, and the belief that only sinking-grade material is worth owning confuses a density rank with a quality verdict — a lighter, floating piece can smell better than a dense one.

The misconception follows from the name 沉香, "sinking fragrance," which sounds like a definition of quality. It is a definition of *density*, not of aroma. Resin packs the fiber → the piece grows heavy → it sinks → the sink test ranks it high. But aroma quality rides on the *composition* of the resin, not its weight, and a denser piece can carry a flat profile while a lighter one carries a complex, sweet one. The boundary to hold: sinking is a useful signal of resin *concentration*, never a guarantee of a better *smell*. A collector who buys only sinking grade is buying density and hoping the aroma follows — it often does, but the two are not the same measurement, and treating them as one is how overpriced, dense-but-dull pieces get sold.

## The Extraction Craft: How Distillers Separate the Aromatic Fraction

**Direct answer:** Turning agarwood into oil is a different discipline from burning the wood — distillers use heat and steam to strip the volatile aromatic fraction off the fiber, and the route they choose decides whether the oil reads bright, dark, or scorched.

Trace one standard run as a sequence. The harvested, dried wood is ground to a coarse meal → the meal is loaded into a still and heated, often under steam → the steam carries the volatile compounds out of the fiber → the vapor is condensed and the oil floats or settles out of the water → the aromatic fraction is captured while the spent, now scent-poor wood is discarded. The variables that matter are the cut of the wood, the temperature, and the duration: a low, slow run preserves the delicate sweet notes, a hard, hot run pushes heavier, smokier compounds out but can scorch the profile. This is why oud oil and burned agarwood smell related but not identical — the still favors different compounds than the ember, and a buyer shopping oil is reading a different product than a buyer shopping chips.

## A Regional Trade Reference

**Direct answer:** The agarwood market names pieces by origin because provenance is a shorthand for a scent profile, and the same species grown elsewhere does not smell the same.

| Trade name / origin | Species often cited | The profile it signals |
|---|---|---|
| Hainan 海南 | *Aquilaria sinensis* | Cool, clean, sweet — the classical Chinese reference |
| Vietnam (Nha Trang) | *A. crassna* | Deep, sweet, complex |
| Cambodia (Pursat) | *A. crassna* | Sweet, fruity, honeyed |
| Borneo / Malaysia | *A. microcarpa*, *A. malaccensis* | Earthy, woody, sometimes smoky |
| Indonesia (Kalimantan) | *A. microcarpa* | Woody, less sweet |

The table is directional, not a specification — individual lots vary more than regions do, and origin names are among the most counterfeited labels in the trade. Its honest use is as a *starting vocabulary* for describing a piece, not as a claim you can accept without the seller naming species and standing behind it. A named origin is a hypothesis to verify, not a fact to read off the label.

## Storing Agarwood Without Wrecking It

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is more forgiving than most aromatics, but three storage habits preserve it — keep it away from strong smells, out of direct sunlight and moisture, and sealed from air when you are not using it.

The wood is porous, so it drinks in whatever surrounds it. A chip left beside soap, spices, or a box of perfumed sticks will slowly take on those notes → the stored piece no longer reads as clean agarwood → the collector has, in effect, diluted the very material they paid to preserve. Sunlight and damp are the other two enemies → ultraviolet light fades the surface resin over months → trapped moisture invites mould in a material prized for being dry. A dark, cool, sealed jar is the answer, and a collector who stores agarwood the way they would a good tea loses almost nothing over years.

The storage rule has a second, quieter payoff: a well-stored piece holds its staged release intact. A chip that has absorbed kitchen smells, or one that has been bleached by a windowsill, will open flat when warmed — the top note is already gone. Storage is therefore not housekeeping; it is part of the authentication and appreciation chain.

## The Sinking Test, Done Properly

**Direct answer:** The 沉水 sink test is only meaningful if the piece is tested as a whole, in clean water, with no surface tension cheating — and it answers one narrow question, which is resin density, not quality or authenticity.

Run it correctly and it tells you one thing reliably. Fill a cup with clean water → lower the piece in gently, not dropped → a resin-dense piece sinks to the bottom → a lightly resinated piece floats → the result places the piece on the density ladder. The common mistakes are the interesting part: a piece cut into small shavings floats regardless because surface area spreads the weight → a piece with trapped air bubbles reads falsely light → a piece coated in oil floats differently from one that is saturated through. The test is a first screen, never a verdict, and the spec page's warnings about oil-injected fakes engineered to sink apply here unchanged.

The deeper point is that "sinking" measures one dimension of the material. A sinking piece can still be low quality if the resin is flat and dull; a floating piece can still smell beautiful. Use the sink test to sort density, then let the nose — on gentle heat — decide the rest.

## Buying Agarwood: The Questions a Careful Buyer Asks

**Direct answer:** Before any price is discussed, a careful buyer asks four questions — species, origin, wild-versus-cultivated, and what happens when it is warmed — because each answer is checkable and each silence is a warning.

Work the four in order. Species pins the botanical identity, and a seller who cannot name *Aquilaria sinensis* versus *A. malaccensis* has already failed the first test → origin names the region, which is a scent claim and a legal claim at once → wild-versus-cultivated tells you whether the wood is old, slow-grown, and regulated, or young plantation stock → and the warm-aroma test is the one that cuts through all the labels, because a fake can carry a name but cannot carry the staged release. The four questions cost nothing and together they eliminate most of the market's fakes before a single gram is weighed.

The honest framing: these are screening questions, not guarantees. A sophisticated fake can survive the first three and only fail on the fourth. That is why the warm-aroma test is the anchor — it is the one check that reads the material itself rather than the story told about it.

## The Beginner's Timeline: What to Buy First, Second, Third

**Direct answer:** A careful beginner buys in this order — first a small, honestly labelled cultivated piece to calibrate the nose, then a slightly better piece to hear the difference, and only then any prestige grade — because each step builds on the last.

The sequence is a calibration ladder, not a shopping list. Step one is a small quantity of cultivated agarwood from a seller who states species and origin plainly → it is genuine but modest → it teaches the nose what resin-saturated *Aquilaria* smells like warmed. Step two is a step up in density or age → the difference between the two teaches what "more resin" does to the aroma → the nose learns to hear the ladder. Only at step three, with a calibrated nose, does a prestige grade make sense → by then the buyer can hear whether the expensive piece actually delivers, rather than trusting a label.

The reasoning is that most disappointment in this category comes from buying the summit before the base. A beginner who starts with a "rare" piece has no reference point → cannot tell real from fake, or good from great → and has spent summit money on a lesson that a modest chip would have taught for far less. The modest piece first is not a compromise; it is the instrument you use to judge everything after it.

## Reading a Certificate Without Being Fooled by It

**Direct answer:** A certificate is only as good as the lab or seller behind it, and a careful buyer reads three things — who issued it, what exactly it claims, and whether the claim is even checkable.

The document is a claim wearing a lab coat. Ask who issued it → a named, verifiable laboratory or an anonymous "certificate of authenticity" printed by the seller → the first is evidence, the second is decoration. Ask what it claims → does it name a species and an origin, or does it only say "genuine agarwood," a phrase that is true of almost anything resin-bearing → the specific claim is checkable, the vague one is not. Ask whether the claim can be verified independently → a certificate number traceable to a lab database is worth something; a signed piece of paper is worth the paper.

The honest position is that no document replaces the warm-aroma test. A certificate can be forged as easily as a label, and the one thing a forger cannot print is the material's staged release on gentle heat. Treat the document as a filter that narrows the field, and let the nose render the verdict.

## Agarwood and Moisture: Does It Need a Humidor?

**Direct answer:** No — agarwood is a dry, resin-laden material, not a fresh cigar, and it wants the opposite of a humidor: a dry, dark, sealed container with no added moisture.

A humidor is the wrong tool because it solves a problem agarwood does not have. Cigars dry out and crack, so they want humidity; agarwood is already dry and resin-dense, and added moisture invites mould → a damp chip grows fungus long before it benefits from anything → the water does nothing for the aroma and everything for the spoilage. The correct container is a dry, dark, sealed jar or tin, ideally with a little desiccant in humid climates, kept out of sunlight and away from strong smells.

The one moisture-adjacent caution is the reverse of the humidor instinct: do not store agarwood where steam or condensation reaches it, because a resin-laden wood that takes up water will not smell better — it will spoil. Dry, dark, sealed, and quiet is the whole of it.

## Why Region Names Persuade (and Why They Can Mislead)

**Direct answer:** A region name is persuasive because it bundles scent, history, and scarcity into one word — and it can mislead for exactly the same reason, since a forger can print a famous origin on a cheap piece without changing the wood.

The name works on three levels at once. It carries a scent profile → Hainan reads cool and clean, Vietnam deep and sweet → so the name promises a smell. It carries history → these origins built their reputations over centuries of trade → so the name borrows authority. It carries scarcity → famous origins are harder to find → so the name implies value. All three are real, which is why the name persuades.

And all three are faked by a printed label. A cheap piece from an unnamed plantation can be sold as "Hainan" with no change to the wood → the buyer pays the famous-origin premium → and only the nose, or a seller who can document the chain of custody, reveals the gap. The rule that holds: treat a region name as a hypothesis about the scent, to be confirmed on gentle heat, never as a fact to accept from the packaging.

## Burning Versus Heating: The Physics of What You Lose

**Direct answer:** Burning and gentle heating release the same resin, but they spend it differently — a flame burns through the volatile top notes in seconds, while gentle heat stretches the same compounds across minutes, and that single difference is why the refined tradition insists on warmth, not fire.

Think of the resin as a stack of compounds that each evaporate at their own temperature. The lightest, sweetest ones — the "top notes" — leave first, and they are the ones a buyer pays most to smell. A direct flame sits far above every evaporation point → the light compounds flash off in an instant → what the nose catches is mostly the heavier, smokier, harsher end of the profile → the delicate opening is gone before it reaches the nose. Gentle heat, kept just below ignition, releases the same stack slowly → the light notes arrive first, the body follows, the dry-down last → the full arc survives.

The practical rule that falls out: the method is not a ritual detail, it is the difference between spending and saving the aromatic capital of the piece. A chip warmed correctly gives its whole story; the same chip burned gives only the last chapter, and a harsher one at that.

## The Market's Vocabulary: A Short Field Glossary

**Direct answer:** The agarwood trade runs on a handful of terms that compress whole concepts into single words — 沉香, 奇楠, 沉水, 隔火焚香, 合香, 木包油, 油包木 — and a buyer who learns the seven can read most of what a seller says.

A plain-language key:

| Term | Reading | What it compresses |
|---|---|---|
| 沉香 (chénxiāng) | "Sinking fragrance" | The material itself, graded by density |
| 奇楠 (qínán) | The summit grade | "Oil wraps wood" — resin is the continuous phase |
| 沉水 (chénshuǐ) | "Sinks in water" | The density ladder's top rung |
| 隔火焚香 (géhuǒ fénxiāng) | "Separated-fire incense" | Gentle, flameless warming |
| 合香 (héxiāng) | "Blended incense" | Compounding several aromatics into one formula |
| 木包油 (mù bāo yóu) | "Wood wraps oil" | Ordinary agarwood's structure |
| 油包木 (yóu bāo mù) | "Oil wraps wood" | 奇楠's structure |

The glossary matters because the trade's vocabulary is not decoration — each term is a compressed instruction about structure, density, or method. A seller who uses 沉水 and 奇楠 correctly is describing two different facts; one who uses them interchangeably is revealing that the words are not doing work for them.

## Why Price and Density Do Not Track Perfectly

**Direct answer:** Density and price move together in the broad sense, but they are not the same number — a dense piece can smell dull and a light piece can smell vivid, so a buyer who reads price as density, or density as quality, is reading two different dials.

The confusion comes from a real correlation that is not a law. More resin → more weight → a higher rung on the sink ladder → and usually a higher price. So far the chain holds. But the nose grades the *composition* of the resin, and composition does not scale with weight. A heavy, fully saturated piece can carry a flat, one-dimensional profile → it sinks and sells high while smelling ordinary → the price reflects its density, not its charm. A lighter piece can carry a complex, sweet profile → it floats and sells lower while smelling better → the price understates its appeal.

The practical read: use the sink test for density, the nose for quality, and the price for neither. The three are separate instruments, and a collector who runs them together ends up paying for weight while chasing a smell.

## A Field Check Sequence for a Single Piece

**Direct answer:** A single piece can be screened in a fixed order — look at the grain, weigh it in the hand, read the surface, then warm a fragment — and the order matters because each step narrows what the next must answer.

Run the sequence in this order:

1. **Grain** → even, interlacing fibers suggest resin ran through the wood; coarse, open, pale fiber suggests low resin or young stock.
2. **Weight** → heavy for its size is consistent with resin, but also with oil injection, so weight alone decides nothing.
3. **Surface** → a dull, waxy sheen reads genuine; a glossy, wet, uniform shine reads treated.
4. **Warmth** → warm a small fragment on gentle heat and read the release; this is the step that overrides the other three.

The sequence is a funnel: each early check is cheap and fast, and each only narrows the field. The warmth test is last because it is the one that can contradict the earlier three, and it is the one that reads the material rather than its presentation. A piece that passes grain, weight, and surface but fails the warm test is a dressed fake; a piece that fails the first three but passes the warm test is simply honest low-grade wood.

## How the Two Reference Woods Differ in Use

**Direct answer:** Agarwood and sandalwood are both "reference woods" of Chinese incense, but they are used for opposite effects — agarwood for a slow, evolving, resinous depth, sandalwood for a steady, creamy, even sweetness — and a blender reaches for one or the other by what the blend is missing.

The split is a matter of timing and texture. Agarwood unfolds → its scent arrives in stages over minutes → it rewards patience and gentle heat → it anchors a blend with a deep, changing base. Sandalwood holds steady → its creamy, sweet-woody note arrives at once and stays → it rewards consistency → it fills the middle of a blend with an even, dependable sweetness. One is a slow-moving anchor, the other a steady filler, and the two are complements rather than rivals.

The working rule for a blender: reach for sandalwood when the blend needs a smooth, even middle, and for agarwood when it needs a deep, evolving base. Mixing them is common and sensible — the sandalwood carries the steady middle while the agarwood does the slow, layered work — and a formula built on both is the classical pattern for a reason.

## Agarwood in Modern Perfumery and Oud Oil

**Direct answer:** Outside the incense burner, agarwood reaches modern buyers as distilled oud oil — a concentrated liquid whose production is a different craft from burning the wood — and the oil has its own grading, its own fakes, and its own reasons to be sceptical.

The oil is not burned wood in a bottle. Distillers load ground wood into a still → heat and steam strip the volatile aromatic fraction off the fiber → the vapour condenses and the oil separates from the water → what remains is a concentrated liquid, while the spent, now scent-poor wood is discarded. The route favours different compounds than the ember, which is why oud oil and warmed agarwood smell related but not identical. The oil is graded by origin, age, and how its scent evolves on skin or on a heater — a language parallel to, but not the same as, the wood's density ladder.

And the oil has its own counterfeit problem. Cheap fractions are blended into a thin base, or a synthetic note is layered over something that was never *Aquilaria* → the result reads "oud" on the label and little else on the nose → the buyer of oil faces the same verification gap the buyer of chips faces, refracted through a different product. The lesson carries over: ask for the species and origin, and judge by how the scent behaves, not by the word on the front.

## How the Two Grades Are Actually Told Apart in Hand

**Direct answer:** In the hand, ordinary 沉香 and 奇楠 are told apart by three physical cues — hardness, behaviour under a knife, and whether the piece smells before any heat — and the third is the decisive one.

The three cues line up with the structure. Ordinary 沉香 is hard → it resists a blade → it must be warmed to release scent → it is wood holding resin. 奇楠 is soft → a knife shaves it into curls → a warm hand can knead a pellet from it → it is fragrant at room temperature → it is resin holding wood. The first two cues are the same fact observed from different angles; the third is the one that cannot be faked by surface treatment, because it is the whole material announcing itself before any heat is applied.

The practical read for a buyer: if a piece is hard, needs heat to smell, and shaves into splinters, it is ordinary agarwood, no matter how good. If it is soft, curls under a blade, kneads, and smells on its own, it is 奇楠 — and that is the profile that justifies the summit price. The distinction is physical and checkable, not a matter of taste or of trusting the label.

## The Trade-Off No One States: Verification Effort vs. Material Quality

**Direct answer:** There is a quiet trade-off at the heart of buying agarwood — a mediocre piece that is easy to verify is often the better purchase than an outstanding piece you cannot verify — and the spec's whole grading ladder exists to make that trade-off visible.

Think of verification as a cost paid in time and attention, and quality as a reward paid in scent. A beginner faces a spectrum: at one end, an honestly labelled cultivated chip that is easy to confirm → low reward, near-zero verification cost → a clean purchase. At the other end, a "rare" summit piece whose origin no one can state → high potential reward, enormous verification cost → a gamble dressed as a purchase. The mistake most newcomers make is choosing the high-reward end while paying nothing on the verification side → the result is a faked story, not a faked scent.

The working rule that falls out: never pay for reward you cannot verify. If the seller cannot name species and origin, the "outstanding" piece is only outstanding as a story, and the honest purchase is the modest piece whose facts you can actually check. Verification is not a chore tacked onto buying — it is the price of admission to the higher rungs of the ladder.

## A Grading Decision Table: What Each Signal Actually Decides

**Direct answer:** The agarwood market's many checks each answer one question and one question only, and a table that maps signal to question stops a buyer from asking a density test to prove authenticity.

| Signal | The one question it answers | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| Sink test (沉水) | How dense is the resin? | Prove the wood is genuine |
| Burn test | Does it give white smoke and oil? | Prove the grade |
| Gentle-heat aroma | Does it release in stages? | Name the species |
| Grain and weight | Is it resin-laden through the fiber? | Rule out oil injection |
| Species + origin | Which tree, which place? | Guarantee the scent |
| Price | What does the market ask? | Decide quality |

The table's lesson is that no single signal is a master key. Each is a single-purpose instrument, and a buyer who runs the sink test expecting an authenticity verdict is using a ruler to weigh a bag. The checks work as a set — density for the sink test, behaviour for the burn, release for the nose, provenance for the species — and the verification job is to run the right instrument for the question you actually have.

## Why "Genuine" Is Not the Same as "Good"

**Direct answer:** A piece can be one hundred percent genuine *Aquilaria* and still be a poor purchase, because genuineness and quality are two separate axes — one is about what the wood is, the other about how it smells — and collapsing them is a common and costly error.

The two axes point in different directions. Genuineness is a yes-or-no fact about the material → is it really resin-bearing *Aquilaria* → it is answered by species, origin, and the burn and sink tests. Quality is a graded judgment about the scent → how layered, sweet, and long is the release → it is answered only by the nose on gentle heat. A low-resin cultivated chip is fully genuine and fully ordinary → a buyer who paid a premium for it has not been defrauded, only disappointed. A summit 奇楠 that is genuine but flat has the same problem in reverse.

The practical correction: buy genuineness first, quality second, and keep the two purchases separate in your head. The first question every purchase must survive is "is it really agarwood," and the second is "is it agarwood worth this price." A buyer who merges the two will either overpay for genuine-but-dull material or chase a quality story that a fake is telling.

## The Honest Limits of This Page

**Direct answer:** Everything on this page is a screening framework, not a laboratory method — the sink test, the burn test, and the warm-aroma check are useful filters, but none of them, alone or together, replaces a documented chain of custody for an expensive purchase.

The limits are worth stating plainly because the page spends most of its length on heuristics. A heuristics-based screen catches the crude fakes → dyed wood, oil-soaked chips, substituted species → and it does that job well. It does not catch a sophisticated counterfeit → a genuine species name printed on a treated piece, a lab-style certificate with no lab behind it → because those attacks target the parts of the material a bench test cannot reach. The gap between what the tests catch and what a buyer pays for is exactly where a documented chain of custody earns its keep.

The practical boundary: use these checks to reject the obvious, and use a verifiable seller or lab for the rest. A buyer who treats the sink test as a verdict has asked a density measurement to prove provenance, and that is the one thing it cannot do.

## A Note on Terminology That English Blurs

**Direct answer:** English flattens several distinct Chinese terms into the single word "agarwood," and that flattening hides real distinctions — 沉香 names the material, 奇楠 names the summit grade, and 沉水 names a density rung, not the material itself.

Three terms, three different jobs. 沉香 (chénxiāng) is the material → the resin-laden heartwood → the broad name everything else nests under. 奇楠 (qínán) is a specific grade → the soft, oil-wrapped summit → not a synonym for 沉香 but a subset of it. 沉水 (chénshuǐ) is a test result → "sinks in water" → a density band within the material, not a separate substance. When a seller says "sinking-grade agarwood," they are naming a density rung; when they say "奇楠," they are naming a structural grade; when they say "沉香," they are naming the material. The three are nested, not interchangeable.

The blur matters because English speakers often use "agarwood" for all three at once → a buyer then treats "sinking grade" and "奇楠" as synonyms for the same thing → and pays a summit price for a density rung that is nothing of the kind. Keeping the three terms separate is a cheap way to read a label correctly.

## A Misconception: "If It Is Real, It Must Smell Strong"

**Direct answer:** The assumption that a genuine agarwood piece should fill a room with scent is backwards — most real agarwood is quiet, and the strongest-smelling pieces are often the fakes engineered to be loud.

The misconception comes from how most people first meet incense: as a room-filling stick. That model does not transfer to agarwood. A genuine chip, warmed gently, gives a modest, layered release meant for the nose close to the heat → not a plume that scents a whole room → the material is subtle by design. A fake, by contrast, is often doused with fragrance oil precisely so it *does* read strong at a distance → the loudness is the tell of the treatment, not the proof of the material.

The boundary to hold: strength is a measure of the dose, not the grade. A buyer who judges agarwood by how far the smell carries is measuring the perfume, not the wood. The honest test is the staged release on gentle heat — weak at first, then opening — not a single loud burst.

*The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## A Confusion Table: The Senses a Fake Attacks

**Direct answer:** Fakes attack one sense at a time — sight, weight, smell, or the sink test — and a table of which sense each fraud targets shows why no single check is enough.

| Fraud method | Sense it attacks | Why it works | The check that defeats it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dyeing | Sight | Dark wood reads as resin-rich | Burn test (smoke color) |
| Oil-boiling / injection | Weight + sink test | Heavier wood sinks | Gentle-heat aroma |
| Perfume soak | Smell | Strong note reads as quality | Burn test (one-note perfume) |
| Substitution | Sight + smell | Unrelated wood dressed up | Species + origin, then nose |

The table's message is that each fraud is aimed at one sense and defeated by a different one. A dye is a sight fraud → the eye is fooled, the burn test is not. An oil injection is a weight fraud → the hand and the sink test are fooled, the nose on gentle heat is not. This is why the verification protocol on this page runs several checks in sequence: a single check is always attacking a single sense, and a forger only needs to beat the one sense they aimed at.

## The Supply Chain, End to End

**Direct answer:** A piece of agarwood passes through four hands before it reaches a buyer — the harvester, the grader, the trader, and the seller — and each hand is a point where the material can be mislabeled, so a careful buyer reads provenance as a chain, not a label.

Walk the chain. The harvester takes resin-laden wood from a wild tree or a plantation → they sell it raw, often with little documentation → the grader sorts it by density, color, and aroma, and this is where a grade name is first attached → the trader moves it across borders, where CITES paperwork and local names enter → the seller presents it to the end buyer, sometimes honestly, sometimes not. Four hands, four chances for a story to drift from the wood.

The practical lesson is that "provenance" is not a single fact but a chain of custody, and every link is either documented or assumed. A buyer who can only see the last link — the seller — is trusting three invisible links on faith. A seller who can show species, origin, and a documented path has shortened that faith to something checkable. The longer the chain is hidden, the more the price is paying for a story rather than for wood.

## A Decision Table: Wild, Cultivated, or "Who Knows"

**Direct answer:** The wild-versus-cultivated question is not a moral one — it is a legal and price question, and a table that maps each answer to what it implies prevents a buyer from paying wild prices for cultivated wood.

| What the seller says | What it implies | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Wild, documented | Old, slow-grown, CITES-regulated | Permit and chain of custody |
| Cultivated, documented | Younger, lighter, far cheaper | Species and inoculation method |
| "Natural" (no detail) | Probably cultivated, or evasive | Ask for the species and origin |
| No answer | A red flag | Treat the price as unverified |

The table's point is that the wild-versus-cultivated label carries a price gap of an order of magnitude, and the label is exactly where the misrepresentation happens. A cultivated piece sold as wild is the single most common provenance inflation in the trade → the buyer pays scarcity money for plantation wood → and the only defense is asking the seller to state the answer in a way that is checkable. A vague "natural" is not an answer; it is the absence of one.

## The Difference Between a Grade and a Claim

**Direct answer:** A grade is a physical measurement — density, color, aroma — while a claim is a statement about origin or age — wild, old, rare — and the two are often sold as if they were the same thing.

The distinction is load-bearing because it is where buyers are separated from their money. Density is measured → a piece sinks or floats, and that is a fact. Age is claimed → a piece is "old" because the seller says so, and that is a story. The two get fused in phrases like "old sinking-grade agarwood," which bundles a measurement (sinking) with a claim (old) → the measurement lends its credibility to the claim → and the buyer pays for both while only one is checkable.

The correction is to unbundle. Ask which part of the phrase is measured and which is asserted → hold the measured part to the test that measures it (the sink test, the burn, the nose) → and hold the asserted part to documentation or treat it as unproven. A grade and a claim are different instruments, and a buyer who keeps them separate cannot be sold a story at the price of a fact.

## A Practical Workflow for a First-Time Buyer

**Direct answer:** A first-time buyer should run a five-step workflow — decide the use, set the budget, name the species, test the aroma, and check the chain of custody — because the workflow forces each decision to rest on a checkable fact rather than a label.

Work the five steps in order:

1. **Decide the use.** Appreciation by gentle heat, blending into a formula, or a decorative piece — each use points at a different grade and form.
2. **Set the budget.** The grade ladder spans an enormous range, so a budget is a filter, not a detail.
3. **Name the species and origin.** If the seller cannot say which *Aquilaria* and where, stop here.
4. **Test the aroma.** Warm a fragment on gentle heat and read the staged release; this is the step that overrides the label.
5. **Check the chain of custody.** For anything costly, ask for the documentation that ties the piece to a species, origin, and legal status.

The workflow's value is that it front-loads the two checks that cannot be faked — species identity and the warm-aroma test — and leaves the price until the facts are in. A buyer who skips straight to the price is negotiating over a story, not a material.

## Why the Grade Names Do Not Transfer Across Sellers

**Direct answer:** The grade names — 沉水, 奇楠, the color ladder — are not standardized units, and the same word can mean different things from different sellers, which is why a buyer must treat a grade name as a claim to verify, not a fact to accept.

The reason is that grading is descriptive and local, not calibrated. One seller's "sinking grade" may rest on a careful density test, another's on a glance → two pieces both labeled 沉水 can carry different resin loads → the word has done no work → the buyer has paid for a category rather than a measurement. The same looseness afflicts the color grades of 奇楠 → whether purple and yellow are distinct grades or aging stages is genuinely disputed → the label carries the dispute, not a resolution.

The correction is to convert each grade name into a question. "Sinking" → how was density tested? "奇楠" → oil-wraps-wood, and does it shave into curls? "Aged" → aged how long, and who documented it? A grade name is the start of the conversation, never the end of it.

## The Two Questions That Matter Most at the Counter

**Direct answer:** Every agarwood purchase reduces to two questions — what tree, and what happens when it warms — because a seller who can answer the first and let you test the second has already covered the two things a fake cannot survive.

The first question is botanical and checkable. Which *Aquilaria* species, and from where → the answer pins the material to a place and a legal status → a seller who hesitates here has not yet started. The second question is sensory and decisive. Warm a fragment and read the release → a genuine piece opens in stages, a fake reads flat or perfumed → the nose overrides every label in the room. Everything else on this page — the sink test, the burn test, the grade ladder, the price — is a filter that narrows toward these two.

The reason the two questions matter most is that they are the two the forger cannot answer at once. A dyed piece can pass the eye, an oil-injected piece can pass the sink test, but neither can name a true species and then deliver a staged, layered release on gentle heat. Two questions, asked in order, do more work than a dozen checks asked at random.

## A Field Guide to the Common Seller Excuses

**Direct answer:** Certain phrases recur at the counter, and each one is a red flag in disguise — learning to hear them is a faster defense than any test, because they signal that the two questions above will not be answered.

A short phrasebook of what to listen for:

| What the seller says | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "It is all just agarwood." | The species and grade are being collapsed | Ask for the species and origin anyway |
| "The grade names are just marketing." | The density and structural distinctions are being flattened | Ask how density was tested |
| "You can tell by the price." | Price is being offered as proof | Treat price as a claim, not a fact |
| "Trust me, I have been doing this for years." | Provenance is being asserted, not shown | Ask for the documentation |
| "The certificate is in the other shop." | The paper trail does not exist | Walk away or wait for the paper |

The phrasebook's value is that it converts vague discomfort into a concrete question. Each phrase is a signal that the seller would rather assert than document, and the correct response is always the same: ask for the species, the origin, and the warm-aroma test — and treat any refusal to provide them as the answer.

## The Honest Limits of This Page

**Direct answer:** Everything on this page is a screening framework, not a laboratory method — the sink test, the burn test, and the warm-aroma check are useful filters, but none of them, alone or together, replaces a documented chain of custody for an expensive purchase.

The limits are worth stating plainly because the page spends most of its length on heuristics. A heuristics-based screen catches the crude fakes → dyed wood, oil-soaked chips, substituted species → and it does that job well. It does not catch a sophisticated counterfeit → a genuine species name printed on a treated piece, a lab-style certificate with no lab behind it → because those attacks target the parts of the material a bench test cannot reach. The gap between what the tests catch and what a buyer pays for is exactly where a documented chain of custody earns its keep.

The practical boundary: use these checks to reject the obvious, and use a verifiable seller or lab for the rest. A buyer who treats the sink test as a verdict has asked a density measurement to prove provenance, and that is the one thing it cannot do.

## A Note on the Economics of Scarcity

**Direct answer:** Agarwood's price is not driven by marketing — it is a direct consequence of three coupled facts: slow formation, depleted wild stock, and hard verification — and understanding that coupling is what lets a buyer read a price as a signal instead of a mystery.

Each of the three facts tightens the others. Slow formation means supply cannot be hurried → a tree needs years to decades to build resin → no amount of demand shortens that clock. Depleted wild stock means the historical source is largely gone → what remains is regulated and scarce → the legal supply is capped. Hard verification means buyers cannot easily tell good from fake → trust becomes a scarce good → sellers who can document provenance charge for it. The three facts multiply, which is why the price spread is a staircase rather than a smooth ramp.

The reader's reward is a working rule: a price is a claim about how slowly it formed, how scarce it is, and how verifiable it is. When a price is low, one of the three has usually broken — young stock, unverified origin, or a fake. When a price is high, ask which of the three is doing the work, and whether the seller can show it.

## Why a Buyer Should Care About the Species Name

**Direct answer:** The species name is not botanical pedantry — it is the first checkable fact a purchase stands on, because *Aquilaria sinensis*, *A. malaccensis*, and the related *Gyrinops* all answer to "agarwood" but carry different prices, legal status, and scent profiles.

The name is a switch that turns a vague category into a specific object. Saying "agarwood" tells you almost nothing → the word covers several trees, several regions, and every grade from medicinal chips to 奇楠 → a buyer who stops at the broad word has stopped before the facts begin. Saying "*Aquilaria sinensis* from Hainan" pins the material to a tree and a place → the price, the legal status, and the scent profile all become checkable → the purchase stops being a leap of faith.

The practical move is to treat the species name as the first test of the seller. A seller who can name the species is making a falsifiable claim; one who cannot is making a hope. Ask for the species before you ask about the grade, and you have already filtered out most of the market's vagueness before a single price is discussed.

## How Heat and Time Change What You Smell

**Direct answer:** The same piece of agarwood smells different at different temperatures and different ages — gentle heat opens the sweet top, higher heat pushes the smoky base, and oxidation over years dulls the bright notes — so a buyer should judge a piece the way it will actually be used.

The temperature ladder is the first lever. At the lowest warmth, only the lightest, sweetest compounds leave the wood → the nose gets a bright, honeyed opening → this is the 头香 stage. Raise the heat and the middle-weight compounds join in → the body fills out → this is 本香. Push toward scorching and the heavy, smoky fractions dominate → the dry-down turns darker and harsher → 尾香. The same chip is three different smells across that range, which is why a buyer who only ever smells it hot has never smelled the piece they paid for.

Time is the second lever, and it works against the bright notes. Resin left in open air oxidizes → the lightest compounds break down first → the aged piece loses its honeyed top and keeps its darker base → a piece that once opened sweet now opens flat. Storage slows this but does not stop it, which is one reason a buyer should prefer well-stored, recently cut material over an old jar of unknown history.

## A Table of the Three Stages, Mapped to Use

**Direct answer:** The three classical stages — 头香, 本香, 尾香 — are not just vocabulary; they are a map of how a piece will perform at different temperatures, and a table that links each stage to a use case turns the vocabulary into a decision tool.

| Stage | What you smell | What it suits |
|---|---|---|
| 头香 (opening) | Sweet, bright, honeyed | Appreciation at gentle heat |
| 本香 (body) | The main, sustained character | Longer, steady warming |
| 尾香 (dry-down) | Darker, deeper, smoky | The end of a long session |

The table's message is that the stages are not a defect — they are the point. A piece that opens sweet and closes dark is not inconsistent; it is doing what layered resin does. A buyer who understands the map can choose a piece for its opening, its body, or its dry-down, and can heat it to the stage they actually want to spend time with.

## A Misconception: "The Sink Test Is the Whole Story"

**Direct answer:** The sink test is a density measurement, not a quality verdict, and treating it as the whole story is how buyers end up paying for weight while chasing a smell.

The test answers one question — how dense is the resin? — and it answers it well. A piece that sinks carries more resin by volume; a piece that floats carries less. But the test says nothing about what that resin smells like → a dense piece can be flat and dull, a light piece can be vivid and sweet → the density is a fact, the quality is a judgment, and the two live on different axes. A buyer who buys only sinking grade is buying the fact and hoping the judgment follows.

The correction is to treat the sink test as one filter among several. Density first, then the burn test, then the nose on gentle heat — three checks, three questions, one verdict that no single test can reach alone. The sink test is a good first screen and a terrible final answer.

## A Quick Reference: What Each Check Is Actually For

**Direct answer:** The agarwood checks each do one job, and a one-line map of which check answers which question prevents the most common buyer error — running the wrong test for the question they actually have.

| Check | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Sink test (沉水) | How dense is the resin? |
| Burn test | White smoke and oil seepage, or black smoke? |
| Gentle-heat aroma | Does it release in stages? |
| Grain and weight | Is the resin through the fiber? |
| Species + origin | Which tree, which place? |
| Price | What does the market ask? |

The map's value is that it stops a buyer from asking a density test to prove authenticity, or a price tag to prove quality. Each row is a single-purpose instrument, and the verification job is to run the right instrument for the question in hand. The checks work as a set, never as a single key.

## A Note on What "Natural" and "Cultivated" Actually Mean

**Direct answer:** In the agarwood market, "natural" and "cultivated" are claims about how the resin formed, not about whether the wood is genuine — both are real agarwood, but they sit at different prices and different legal statuses, and the label is exactly where the two get confused.

Both routes end in genuine resin-bearing *Aquilaria*. A natural piece forms when a wild tree is wounded by chance → the resin builds over years to decades → the wood is harvested old and often deeply saturated → it is scarce and CITES-regulated. A cultivated piece forms when a plantation tree is deliberately drilled or inoculated → the resin builds over a few years → the wood is harvested younger and lighter → it is cheaper and legal. The resin chemistry is the same class of material; what differs is age, saturation, price, and paperwork.

The confusion that costs money is treating "natural" as a quality claim and "cultivated" as a fake. Neither is correct → a cultivated piece is genuine agarwood, and a "natural" label on a young, lightly resinated piece is a story, not a grade. The honest question a buyer asks is not "is it natural?" but "can the seller state the origin and the legal status, and is the price consistent with the answer?"

## A Practical Note on Buying Powder Versus Chips

**Direct answer:** Agarwood reaches a buyer as chips or as powder, and the two forms change what you can verify — a chip keeps its resin veins visible, while a powder hides fillers behind a uniform grind — so the form you choose should follow what you plan to do with it.

The split is a matter of inspection. A chip is a solid piece → you can see the resin veins, feel the density, and run the sink test → the material's identity is on display. A powder is a uniform grind → the veins, the color contrast, and the heft all vanish into the dust → the only check left is a sample burn against a known reference. The chip is the form for appreciation and for verification; the powder is the form for blending into a stick or cone formula.

The practical rule: buy chips when you want to see and verify the material, and buy powder only when you need it for a formula — and in the latter case, treat the particle size, moisture, and a burn test as your only protection. A buyer who cannot say which form they need is buying the wrong form for the wrong job.

## A Note on Why Size and Moisture Matter for Powder

**Direct answer:** For agarwood powder, the two specifications that decide how it behaves are particle size and moisture — they set how the powder packs, draws air, burns, and stores — and neither is printed on most labels, so the maker must read them by feel.

Particle size controls the burn. A fine, even grind disperses through a paste and lets the binder hold it → the finished stick burns evenly. A coarse, mixed grain packs unevenly → the stick burns in patches. Moisture controls the paste. A damp powder clumps and cakes → the paste packs unevenly → the burn stalls at the wet spots. A bone-dry powder dusts and floats → it is hard to wet evenly → the binder must work harder. The window between "cakes" and "dusts" is the working moisture, and it is set by the grind's surface area.

The practical move: press a pinch between your fingers and watch whether it clumps, and burn a small amount against a known reference before committing to a batch. Moisture and mesh are specs you measure by hand, and they decide more about the finished stick than the label's word does.

## A Final Note on Trust and Verification

**Direct answer:** The whole agarwood question reduces to trust — either you trust a seller's word, or you trust a check you can run yourself — and every test on this page exists to move a purchase from the first kind of trust to the second.

The trade runs on two kinds of confidence. The first is the seller's word: "this is genuine Hainan agarwood." The second is a check you can hold: a piece that sinks, burns to white smoke, and opens in stages on gentle heat. The first is cheap to offer and easy to fake; the second is slow but hard to fake. The gap between them is where the fakes live.

The practical endpoint of everything here: for a modest, first purchase, run the checks and accept the small risk. For anything costly, shift the trust to a laboratory or a documented chain of custody, because at the summit of the price ladder the seller's word is exactly what you are paying to escape. Verification is not a chore added to buying agarwood — it is the purchase.

## A Glossary of the Trade's Most Abused Words

**Direct answer:** Certain words in the agarwood trade are loaded — "natural," "old," "wild," "rare," "premium" — and each one is a claim the seller should be able to defend, because each one is also the cheapest thing to print on a label.

Run through the vocabulary:

| Word | The question it should trigger |
|---|---|
| "Natural" | Formed how, and can you document it? |
| "Old" | Old how long, and who measured it? |
| "Wild" | Harvested where, and under what permit? |
| "Rare" | Rare by what standard — species, grade, or origin? |
| "Premium" | Premium compared to what, and by what test? |

The table's lesson is that these words are adjectives without a noun to anchor them until the seller supplies one. "Natural" and "old" and "rare" each name a property the buyer cannot see on the surface → the property can only be supported by documentation or a test → a label that uses them without support is using the cheapest material in the shop: ink. Treat every such word as a prompt to ask one concrete question, and treat silence as the answer.

## A Field Note on the Two Grading Philosophies

**Direct answer:** The agarwood world runs two grading philosophies side by side — one that ranks by density (the 沉水 ladder) and one that ranks by aroma character (the five-flavor and stage system) — and a buyer needs both, because density is measurable and aroma is judged, and neither substitutes for the other.

The density philosophy is quantitative. A piece sinks, half-sinks, or floats → the result places it on a ladder → the ladder is objective and reproducible → anyone with a cup of water can run it. Its limit is that it says nothing about smell → a dense piece can be dull, a light piece vivid → the ladder is precise about the wrong thing, or rather about one of two things. The aroma philosophy is qualitative. A piece opens cool or sweet, its body is honeyed or earthy, its dry-down is deep or faint → the judgment lives in the nose → it is subjective and hard to standardize → but it is the thing the buyer is actually paying to experience.

The working synthesis: use density to sort the field, use aroma to choose within it. Density is the fast, objective first cut; aroma is the slow, subjective final cut. A buyer who runs only one of the two is grading with half the instruments.

## Three Appreciation Traditions, Three Rituals

**Direct answer:** The same resin-bearing wood is consumed three very different ways in three traditions — gently warmed on a mica plate in China, formally savored in the Japanese kōdō ceremony, and smoldered on charcoal as bakhoor across the Middle East — and the ritual, not the material, is what separates them.

China's refined route is 隔火焚香 (separated-fire heating): a buried coal, a bed of ash, a thin plate, and a chip held just below its ignition point so the staged aroma unfolds in order. It is quiet, slow, and aimed at the nose alone. The Japanese kōdō ceremony pushes this further into a codified practice — participants pass a censer and identify named woods by their scent alone, treating the material as a connoisseur's discipline rather than a room fragrance. The Middle Eastern bakhoor tradition runs the other way: agarwood or agarwood-laced compounds are laid onto hot charcoal, releasing thick, sweet smoke meant to fill a room and scent clothing and hands.

The three share one material and split on everything else. Heating favors the delicate opening notes → the flame-free route preserves the top of the profile → the experience is private and subtle. Smoldering favors volume → the smoke saturates a space → the experience is social and communal. Neither is wrong; they are different uses of the same wood, and a buyer shopping across traditions is really shopping across three different ideas of what agarwood is *for*.

## Why Low-Grade Chips Dominate the Volume Market

**Direct answer:** The agarwood you can buy cheaply and in bulk is almost always the low-resin end of the ladder — not because the good material is absent, but because the market's volume sits exactly where the resin fraction is thinnest, and that is where the margins and the fakes concentrate.

Walk the distribution. A mature *Aquilaria* tree that was never wounded holds pale, near-scentless heartwood → this is abundant and cheap → most of what is cut and sold as "agarwood" at the low end is this material, or lightly resinated wood → the deep, fully saturated grades are the rare tail of a slow, decade-scale process → the rare tail is what the price ladder rewards. The result is a market shaped like a wedge: enormous volume at the base, a thin sliver at the summit.

This wedge is why price does most of the sorting for a buyer. A cheap "agarwood" chip is not a bargain on a rare material — it is the base of the wedge, where the resin is faint and the value is mostly in the name. The summit grades are scarce because the biology that produces them is slow, and no amount of demand can hurry it.

## A Field Checklist for Reading Any Listing

**Direct answer:** Twenty quick checks, run in order, catch most of what a listing is trying to say — and, more usefully, what it is trying not to say.

1. Species named? If not, ask.
2. Origin named? If not, ask.
3. Cultivated or wild stated? If not, ask.
4. Grade named in a scheme you recognize (沉水, 奇楠, a color tier)?
5. Price consistent with the grade claimed?
6. Sink test described or shown?
7. Warm-aroma described, or only cold sniff?
8. Smoke color mentioned (white vs black)?
9. Oil seepage mentioned?
10. Perfume note disclaimed?
11. Certificate present, and issued by a named lab?
12. Certificate claims specific (species, origin) or vague ("genuine")?
13. Documentation of chain of custody?
14. Seller willing to state provenance without hesitation?
15. "Natural," "old," "wild," "rare," "premium" used — and each backed by a concrete noun?
16. Return or inspection terms clear?
17. Batch or lot number?
18. Unit clearly defined (gram, chip count, stick count)?
19. Harvest or age date?
20. Any contradiction between the price and the adjectives?

The checklist's value is that most of it costs nothing to run and each "no" narrows the field. A listing that names species, origin, cultivation status, and a recognized grade, and that prices them consistently, has survived the first nineteen questions; a listing that leans on adjectives and refuses specifics trips on the second. Treat the checklist as a screen, not a certificate — the warm-aroma test still renders the final verdict.

## The Perfumery Bridge: Agarwood as an Oil and a Note

**Direct answer:** The same wood that is warmed on a mica plate also ends up inside attars and high-end perfumes as a distilled oil, and the two forms — solid and liquid — are different products with different rules, even though they come from the same tree.

Perfume houses want a liquid they can dose by the drop, so distillers steam or hydrodistill ground agarwood to strip the volatile fraction off the fiber. The result is an oil that smells related to, but never identical with, the wood that produced it — the still favors a narrower band of the profile, and a hard run can scorch the delicate opening the way a flame does. A buyer shopping an oud oil is reading a different chapter than a buyer shopping a chip, and the price logic differs too: an oil's value rides on yield (how much oil a given weight of wood surrenders) as much as on the wood's grade.

The bridge matters because the two markets leak into each other. A cheap "oud" perfume built on synthetic or adulterated oil borrows the prestige of the wood without the cost → the label rides on the name, not the material → the buyer pays a story. The same authentication habit travels across the bridge: ask what the material is, where it came from, and whether the claim is checkable — whether the product is a chip, a powder, or a liquid in a bottle.

## Storing Agarwood for Years Without Losing It

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is forgiving to store — it is dry, resin-dense, and stable — but it needs three simple protections: keep it out of strong smells, out of direct sun and damp, and sealed when not in use, because the wood is porous and drinks in whatever surrounds it.

The porosity is the whole story. A chip left beside soap, spices, or a box of scented sticks slowly absorbs those notes → the stored piece no longer reads as clean agarwood → the collector has diluted the very material they paid to preserve. Sunlight is the second enemy → ultraviolet light fades the surface resin over months → a windowsill-aged piece opens flat. Damp is the third → trapped moisture invites mould in a material prized for being dry → a humid spot turns a stable aromatic into a spoiled one.

The rule that follows is a dark, cool, sealed jar, and a collector who stores agarwood the way they would a good tea loses almost nothing over years. There is a quiet second payoff: a well-kept piece holds its staged release intact, because the top note — the first and most delicate stage — is the first thing a poor storage spot destroys. Storage, then, is not housekeeping; it is part of the appreciation chain.

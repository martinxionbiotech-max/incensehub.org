**Direct answer:** Agarwood is what an *Aquilaria* tree grows around a wound: a dark, fragrant infiltration of the tissue that can take decades to develop and cannot be produced on demand. Every piece is therefore a record of injury, which explains both its scarcity and its price. It is warmed rather than set alight, sorted by how much of the tissue has converted, and imitated more often than almost any other aromatic material.

**Key facts:** The Chinese name translates as "sinking fragrance," a reference to how the densest pieces behave in water. Most of the trade is hard timber carrying resin channels; the apex grade, 奇楠 (qínán, known in Japan as kyara), is the inverse — a material in which the resin has softened the fibre itself. Wild sources are limited and controlled by international agreement, so plantation material now supplies the bulk of the market.

## Key Takeaways

- **The tree's defence is the product.** Without an injury there is nothing to harvest.
- **Density is the oldest grade scale** and the easiest one to fake.
- **The apex grade describes a structure,** not a species — resin dominant over softened fibre — and it attracts the most mislabelling.
- **Flame wastes the material.** The opening notes, which are the reason to buy good wood, are the first thing a flame destroys.
- **Adulteration has four standard shapes:** boiling in oil, forcing oil in under pressure, dyeing and scenting pale wood, and selling an unrelated timber under the name.
- **Classical medical language belongs to history.** It is quoted here as context, not as evidence.

## What the Material Is, and Is Not

The genus is *Aquilaria*, in the family Thymelaeaceae. Sound timber from those trees is pale, light, and nearly scentless — good for little in the incense trade. The portion that matters is a dark, resin-charged region around an old injury, and it may be a small fraction of the trunk.

That detail explains most of the market's economics. A plantation of healthy *Aquilaria* produces timber, not incense. The incense fraction is a pathological by-product, unpredictable in location and quantity, and a tree may yield very little of it after years of growth.

Confusion usually involves one of three neighbours. Sandalwood is a different genus entirely, with a creamy scent that does not depend on injury. Oud oil is not a uniform commodity either, since it is distilled from wood as variable as the chips. And "agarwood" is not one species: several in the genus, plus a related one, yield material sold under identical labels.

The [sandalwood guide](/blog/sandalwood-incense/) covers the nearest alternative, and the comparison matters because the two are confused constantly in listings.

## The Wound That Makes the Wood

The formation story is a chain, and each link has consequences for what a buyer receives.

a branch breaks, an insect bores, or a fungus enters → the tree isolates the injury with a resinous response → the resin soaks outward into surrounding tissue → the process repeats over years, layer on layer → the wood around the wound darkens and hardens → the tree is felled, or dies and ages in place → the resin-rich portion is separated from pale timber → what remains is graded, traded, and heated

The first consequence is that the resin is not evenly distributed. A single piece can be dark on one face and pale on the other, and cutting it changes what you have. Sellers who photograph one beautiful face are not necessarily dishonest, but the photograph is not the piece.

The second is that time is the dominant input. Resin accumulates slowly, which is why plantation material harvested after a few years tends to be lighter and less complex than old wild pieces. Cultivation shortens the clock; it does not abolish it.

The third is that "wild" and "cultivated" describe a process, not a quality. A well-managed plantation with a long grow-out and good post-harvest handling can produce material that outperforms poorly stored wild wood. What wild material offers, when it is legal and documented, is age — and age is what the nose usually recognizes.

## "Sinking Fragrance": Density as a Grade

Three bands survive in the trade vocabulary. Sinking material, 沉水, goes down. Half-sinking material, 半沉半浮, hangs in the water. Floating material, 浮, stays up and appears in older texts as 黄熟香.

Use the bands as a rough index of how much tissue has been converted. They are not a quality ranking: a floating fragment can be excellent on a plate, and a sinking block can be dull. They are also easy to defeat, because injection makes pale wood heavy. A buoyancy result is a screening step, not a verdict.

## The 奇楠 Distinction: Oil-Wrapped vs Wood-Wrapped

The apex grade differs from ordinary material in structure, not degree. Ordinary agarwood is timber carrying resin channels — split it and pale fibre shows between dark veins, an arrangement the trade calls 木包油, wood wrapping oil. 奇楠 is the inverse, 油包木: resin so pervasive that the fibre softens, so the piece can be shaved into curls and pressed into pellets between the fingers (削之成卷、捏之成丸), and it carries a smell before any heat arrives.

That gives a buyer a cheap first check. Hard resistance suggests ordinary wood; slight give plus a cold scent makes the claim worth investigating. Neither observation proves the grade, but together they eliminate most optimistic labels.

Colour names — white, green, purple, yellow, black — add another layer. Whether the middle two are separate categories or stages in the darkening of green material is disputed, so price behaviour and documentation rather than the adjective.

## Four Ways the Resin Forms

Four labels describe circumstances, not qualities: 熟结 for resin in a tree that died and aged in place, 生结 for resin formed while the tree lived, 虫漏 for resin around insect damage, 脱落 for resin at an external break. None is visible in a finished piece, so each is a seller's account of provenance rather than an observable property.

## How the Scent Unfolds

The material is valued for change rather than intensity, and heating exists to serve that change. Warmth reaches the surface, the lightest compounds leave first, the surface dries, heavier fractions take over, and the residue finally reads woody.

The classical names for those phases are 头香 for the opening, 本香 for the body, and 尾香 for the tail. A flame collapses the sequence: everything leaves at once, some of it degrades, and the result is a crude version of an expensive smell. A piece that smells identical at minute one and minute twenty is either very simple or not what the label claims.

## Species and Geography

Several species produce the material, and the trade names do not map cleanly onto the botany.

The Chinese species, 白木香, is the historical domestic source from the southern provinces. The old Southeast Asian trade wood, *A. malaccensis*, is now scarce and regulated. Indochinese plantations lean on *A. crassna*. A related genus from eastern Indonesia and New Guinea yields comparable material and enters the market under the same words.

Botanical detail sits in the [Flora of China account](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=102387) for the genus. What matters commercially is that species names and trade names diverge: a listing that says "Cambodian" may describe a port, a style, or a marketing story rather than a species. When provenance is priced, provenance should be documented.

Region affects character in the way soil and climate affect wine — broadly, not absolutely. Buyers who chase region names before they can describe a scent are buying narrative, not material.

## Cultivated vs Wild

The distinction is meaningful and frequently misused.

Wild agarwood comes from trees that were injured naturally and left to accumulate resin, often for decades. Legal wild material requires harvest documentation, and in practice the supply is small. Cultivated agarwood comes from planted trees that are wounded deliberately — by boring, cutting, or inoculation — and harvested after a grow-out period.

Cultivation has changed the market in two ways. It has made genuine agarwood available at prices that ordinary buyers can reach, and it has flooded the market with material whose quality varies enormously. A plantation that wounds trees early and harvests fast produces light, resin-poor wood. A plantation that waits produces something closer to the old material.

Neither category is a quality guarantee. The useful questions are about the grow-out period, the handling after harvest, and whether the seller will state the category at all. A seller who says "wild" without documentation is selling an adjective.

## Adulteration: The Four Methods

Agarwood's price attracts fraud, and the methods are consistent enough to learn.

| Method | What is done | What it produces | How it betrays itself |
|---|---|---|---|
| 煮油 (oil boiling) | Ordinary wood boiled in oil | Weight and a surface smell | Greasy feel; smell fades fast; smoke is dark |
| 高压灌油 (pressure injection) | Oil forced into pale wood under pressure | Density that passes a sink test | Oil weeps in heat; scent is flat and one-dimensional |
| 泡药水 (dye soaking) | Non-resinous wood dyed and scented | Colour and a perfume note | Uniform colour; synthetic sharpness; stained water |
| Substitution | Unrelated woods with fragrance compounds | A convincing look and an inexpensive price | Wrong grain; no resin lines; smell of perfume rather than resin |

The common thread is that all four add something to the surface and none of them create resin inside the tissue. That is why the most reliable bench observations are about structure rather than smell: resin lines wandering through pale fibre are difficult to fake, and a uniformly dark block with no pale inclusions deserves suspicion.

## Testing at the Bench

For a buyer without a laboratory, the useful tests are cheap, quick, and cumulative. No single one is proof.

1. **Look at the cut face.** Natural resin follows grain and leaves pale wood visible between veins. Uniform darkness across a fresh face is a warning.
2. **Warm a sliver.** Gentle heat should produce a layered smell that changes over minutes. A flat, perfumed, or burnt-wood smell is a failure signal.
3. **Watch the smoke.** Traditional accounts describe white smoke with oil seepage from genuine material; dark, sooty smoke suggests oil or additives. This is a heuristic, not a test.
4. **Feel the surface.** Oil-treated wood feels slick or tacky. Genuine aged wood feels dry and slightly waxy.
5. **Weigh and measure.** Density is informative when compared with a known piece of similar size, and meaningless in isolation.
6. **Float it.** A sink test stratifies a batch, and proves little about any single piece.
7. **Ask for paper.** Species, origin, and category, in writing. A seller who cannot supply them is telling you where the risk sits.

The [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) collects the reference material for these checks, and the [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) covers the grade vocabulary in more depth.

## Names Across Traditions

Each tradition named the material after what it noticed. Chinese chose density, calling it 沉香. Japanese kept that reading and added 伽羅 for the finest grade, rendered in English as kyara. Arabic trade gave the world oud, which drifted from wood to oil to a whole perfumery family.

None of the words is a specification. "Oud" may mean a fragment, a distillate, or a fragrance compound. "Kyara" is a claim about the apex grade and should carry an apex price and documentation. "Agarwood" is the safest English term and still says nothing about species.

## Traditional Claims, Historical Record

Chinese formularies describe the wood in a language of movement and warmth — 行气止痛，温中降逆，补五脏, roughly "move the qi, ease pain, warm the middle, support the organs." The phrases appear in texts including the 本草纲目 and belong to the history of materia medica.

They are recorded here because product copy repeats them. They are not clinical evidence, and no treatment claim is made on this page. A listing that markets the wood as medicine is making a regulatory claim in many markets, and the presence of such copy says something about the seller.

## Conservation and CITES

Wild agarwood has been harvested far faster than it regenerates, and the response has been regulation. Several *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* species appear on the CITES appendices, which means cross-border trade requires permits from the exporting country and a finding that the trade is not detrimental to wild populations.

For a buyer, this has three practical effects. International shipments may need documentation that a casual seller cannot supply. "Wild" material without paperwork is a legal risk that lands on the importer. And plantation material has become the mainstream because it is easier to document and to sustain.

Listing status is checked at the [CITES database](https://checklist.cites.org/#/en/search/output_layout=alphabetical&level_of_listing=0&show_synonyms=1&show_author=1&show_english=1&show_spanish=1&show_french=1&scientific_name=Aquilaria). Conservation is not a marketing angle here; it is the reason the legal supply looks the way it does.

## Price Without a Price List

Agarwood has no published price list, and any page that offers one is either quoting a single grade or guessing. What can be described is the set of factors that move a quotation.

**Resin fraction.** The dominant variable. A piece that sinks is in a different market from a piece that floats, and the gap is large.

**Grade category.** 奇楠 sits in a category of its own, and within it colour and behaviour move the number further.

**Formation and age.** Old 熟结 material commands more than fresh cultivated wood of similar density, when either can be documented.

**Provenance and legality.** Documented, permit-supported material costs more than material of uncertain origin, because the buyer is paying for the absence of a legal problem.

**Size and shape.** Large, intact pieces with good figure are worth more per gram than splinters, because they can be carved, displayed, or cut to order.

**Seller tier.** A specialist with a reputation to protect prices differently from a marketplace listing with no history. Part of what you pay for is the recourse if the piece is wrong.

The practical rule is to compare quotations only within a grade and a form. A price per gram for chips and a price per gram for a sink-grade block are not comparable numbers, and treating them as comparable is how buyers talk themselves into a bargain that is not one.

## A Buying Sequence

The order below puts cheap checks first and expensive commitments last.

1. **Decide what the piece is for.** Heating, carving, display, or oil distillation are four different purchases with different grade requirements.
2. **Fix the budget and the form.** Chips for a heater, a small block for handling, a larger piece for display.
3. **Ask for species, origin, and category in writing.** Vague answers are information.
4. **Request photographs of both faces and a scale reference.** One flattering face is not a description.
5. **Buy a small sample first.** Heat it, smell it over twenty minutes, and compare it with a reference if you have one.
6. **Escalate only with a seller who passed the small test.** The second purchase is where trust is actually built.
7. **Keep the paperwork.** Species, origin, permits, and the seller's description, stored with the piece.

The [natural vs synthetic](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/) page is useful background for the substitution problem, since the same logic applies to fragrance compounds across the trade.

## Storage and Handling

Agarwood is stable, and most damage comes from handling rather than time.

Keep pieces out of sunlight, away from heat sources, and away from anything strongly scented. Resin-rich wood absorbs odours from its surroundings, and a piece stored beside a scented candle will carry that smell permanently. A wooden box or a sealed glass jar works; plastic is acceptable only if the piece is fully dry and the bag is clean.

Handle with tweezers or clean fingers, and avoid touching the face you intend to heat, because skin oil oxidizes and turns rancid on the surface. Do not wash agarwood. Water raises the grain, can mobilize resin, and encourages mould in the softer tissue.

Do not refrigerate or freeze. Condensation cycles do more harm than room temperature ever will. If a piece arrives damp, let it dry slowly in a shaded, ventilated space before sealing it away.

## What Heat Does to the Resin

Heating agarwood is not burning, and the difference explains why the same chip can smell ordinary on one setup and remarkable on another.

Resin is a mixture. It contains light, mobile molecules that leave at low temperatures, heavier sesquiterpenoid compounds that need more energy, and non-volatile material that stays behind as residue. Warm the chip gently and the fractions leave in order of volatility, which is what the three-stage experience of 头香, 本香, and 尾香 actually is. Push the temperature and the order collapses: everything leaves at once, some of it degrades on the way, and the result is a flat, smoky approximation.

The physical chain is worth holding in mind:

plate temperature rises → the lightest fraction vaporises first → the nose receives the opening → the surface dries and the release slows → heavier compounds begin to dominate → the body forms → the resin closest to the plate is exhausted → the tail thins toward the woody residue

Two practical rules follow from that chain. The plate should reach the wood's working temperature slowly, because a fast rise overshoots before the nose has caught the opening. And the session should be allowed to run down, because the tail is where a good piece separates itself from a merely strong one.

An electric heater makes the first rule easy and the second rule tempting to break. The dial invites turning up the heat when the aroma thins, which accelerates exhaustion and shortens the session. The wood rewards patience in a way that is easy to state and hard to practise.

## Working with Chips

Most buyers encounter agarwood as small chips rather than as a block, and chips behave differently from the piece they came from.

A chip has more surface per gram, so it releases faster and depletes sooner. That makes chips ideal for tasting and poor for long sessions. A single chip the size of a grain of rice is enough for a first evaluation; a piece the size of a pea will carry a longer sitting.

Cutting matters. A saw leaves dust, and dust burns rather than warms, producing smoke and a harsh note. A sharp blade that shaves produces cleaner faces and less waste. Traditional descriptions of 奇楠 emphasize that it shaves into curls, which is both a handling property and a diagnostic.

Portioning is the discipline most beginners skip. Buy a modest quantity, divide it into single-session portions, and keep the portions separate so that a mistake with one does not consume the whole supply. A tiny glass vial per portion is enough, labelled with the date and the seller's description.

Do not grind agarwood for a heater. Powder is for sticks and cones, where it is bound with other materials; on a plate, powder scorches almost immediately.

## The Distillation Route and Oud Oil

A large share of the world's agarwood becomes oil rather than incense, and the logic of that trade is worth understanding because it explains much of what happens to the wood.

Distillers use hydrodistillation or steam distillation, sometimes after soaking or fermenting the wood. The yield is low, the process is slow, and the resulting oil is graded by scent rather than by the density scales used for solid material. Because the yield is low, distillers are motivated to buy wood that would be unremarkable on a heater: resin-bearing but not beautiful, small, or irregular.

This creates a market split. The finest solid pieces become collector material and are heated. The middle grades are distilled. The lower grades are ground, blended, or treated. A buyer who understands the split can read a price list more accurately, because a cheap chip offered at a premium price is competing with the distillery's willingness to pay for the same material.

Oud oil and heated agarwood are related experiences, not identical ones. The oil concentrates and transforms the volatile fraction; the heated wood presents it in sequence, with the woody substrate contributing to the tail. A person who knows the oil does not automatically know the wood, and the reverse is also true.

## Reading a Listing and a Photograph

A listing is a sales document, and its structure reveals what the seller knows.

Look for the fields that a knowledgeable seller fills without being asked: species, country or region, wild or cultivated, form (chip, block, powder), and weight. A listing with a poetic description and no species is selling atmosphere rather than material.

Look at the photographs for scale and for both faces. A hand, a ruler, or a coin gives size; a single face gives the best angle. A photograph taken under warm light flatters resin colour, and a photograph with heavy contrast hides pale inclusions. Neither is proof of intent, but a seller who shows the pale side is showing you something a faker would hide.

Look at the language of the grade claim. "Sinking" is a test result that can be checked. "Super" and "king" are adjectives. "Wild" is a category that requires paperwork. "Kyara-grade" is a comparison that means little without a reference point.

Look at the return policy last, because it is the only clause that costs the seller anything if the piece is wrong.

## When the Session Fails

Four failures account for most disappointing sessions, and none of them is about the wood's reputation.

**Too hot.** The chip scorches at the edge within seconds and the smell turns acrid. The cure is distance and time, not a different chip.

**Too much material.** A generous pile produces a strong opening and a fast collapse, because the surface area to mass ratio changes. Small is better.

**Dirty plate.** Residue from a previous session carbonizes and adds a burnt note that contaminates the whole run. Clean the plate before every session.

**Stale room.** A room that smells of cooking, cleaning products, or perfume masks the opening. Air the room an hour before, not during.

The pattern behind all four is that agarwood is a low-signal material being asked to perform in a high-noise environment. The fix is almost always environmental rather than material.

## A Note on Substitutes

The market offers several materials that stand in for agarwood, and knowing what they are prevents both confusion and overpayment.

Sandalwood is the classical alternative: a different genus, a different scent, a consistent and much more affordable material. It cannot imitate agarwood's staged release, and it is not sold as a substitute so much as a sibling.

"Gaharu" is a trade term used across Southeast Asia for agarwood-type material, sometimes from *Aquilaria*, sometimes from *Gyrinops*, sometimes from unrelated trees with aromatic heartwood. The word describes a trade category, not a species.

Synthetic oud compounds reproduce recognizable parts of the scent and are used legitimately in perfumery and in inexpensive incense. They are not agarwood, and the problem is never their existence — it is a label that implies wood.

The [substitution question](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/) is worth reading before any large purchase, because the same vocabulary appears across the whole trade.

## What a Certificate Proves

Certificates circulate in this market, and their value varies enormously.

A CITES permit proves that a shipment was declared and approved for export. It does not prove that the material inside the box is what the invoice says, and it does not grade it. What it does is establish legality, which is the part a buyer cannot verify independently.

A laboratory report may identify species from a sample, and may report resin content or volatile profile. Its value depends on whether the sample was drawn from the piece you are buying, whether the laboratory is identifiable, and whether the report is addressed to a shipment rather than to a single object.

A seller's own certificate proves that the seller can print a certificate. That is not nothing — it establishes a name and an address — but it is not evidence about the wood.

The practical hierarchy is documentation of legality first, independent species identification second, and everything else as supporting material.

## A Plantation, in Sequence

Cultivated agarwood is made, and the making follows a schedule that a buyer can ask about.

Trees are planted and grown until the trunk is large enough to survive injury. Then the wounding begins: holes are bored, cuts are made, or a culture is introduced into the trunk to provoke the defence response. The tree responds by walling off the injury with resin, and the resin spreads into the surrounding tissue over the following years.

Harvest comes later. The tree is felled, and the trunk is cut into sections and examined. Resin-rich regions are separated from pale wood by eye, by weight, and sometimes by a quick smell. The pale material is not waste — it is the substrate for distillation, powder, and the lower grades — but it is not what the buyer of a chip is paying for.

Three questions follow from that sequence, and each has a real answer that a serious supplier can give.

**How long was the grow-out?** A short cycle produces lighter, less complex material. A longer one costs the grower money and produces something closer to the old material. The answer is usually expressed in years, and a supplier who does not know it is not close to production.

**What was the induction method?** Boring, cutting, and inoculation produce different resin distributions. None is dishonest; all are worth naming.

**How was the harvest sorted?** Hand-sorted material is more consistent than machine-graded bulk, and the difference shows up in the proportion of pale chips in a lot.

A plantation is not a guarantee of quality. It is a guarantee of traceability, which is a different and useful thing. The traceability is what makes the legal trade work, and it is what a buyer can actually check.

## Learning to Smell Blind

The fastest way to learn agarwood is to remove the labels.

Take three pieces of known but different material, code them, and have someone else present them in random order. Smell each on the same heater, at the same temperature, with a minute between. Write one line per piece before checking the code. Repeat across sessions with the same three, then swap one out.

The exercise teaches two things that reading cannot. The first is that memory for smells is weak and needs anchoring — the written line is the anchor. The second is that expectation dominates early judgments, which is why blind presentation is the only way to find out what you actually notice.

A useful variation is the pair test. Two pieces, one variable: same region different grade, same grade different region, or same wood heated at two temperatures. A single variable is easier to detect than a complex set, and the detection is what builds the vocabulary.

Keep the notes. After a few months, the notebook becomes a reference that no description on a listing can replace, and it will show you which of your early judgments were guesses.

## Packing and Shipping

Agarwood is light, valuable, and regulated, which makes it a difficult parcel.

Pack it dry. Wrap the piece in acid-free paper or clean cloth, then in a rigid container, then in an outer box with padding. Do not use scented packing materials, and do not use a plastic bag as the primary container unless the wood is fully dry, because trapped moisture is worse than air.

Declare it accurately. Material that requires CITES documentation needs the paperwork with the shipment, not in an email afterwards. Under-declaring a high-value wood to avoid scrutiny is a customs problem, not a saving.

Insure it. Small parcels of high-value material are lost at a rate that makes insurance rational, and a tracked service with signature is the minimum.

Photograph the packing. A photograph of the piece before it goes into the box, with a scale, resolves most disputes about what was sent.

## Heater Setups Compared

The heating method changes the experience more than most buyers expect, and the choice is practical rather than ceremonial.

| Setup | Control | Smoke | Cost of entry | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Charcoal under ash with a mica plate | Manual, learned | Almost none | Moderate | Traditional practice, long sessions |
| Electric heater with a dial | Precise, repeatable | None | Moderate | Learning a piece, comparing grades |
| Tealight sub-burner | Coarse | Low | Low | Beginners, small chips |
| Direct coal or ember | Poor | Heavy | Low | Cheap blends, not fine wood |
| Resin warmer with a metal dish | Easy to overshoot | Low | Low | Oud oil and resins, not chips |

The electric heater deserves a recommendation that tradition does not require: it removes the two variables that ruin beginner sessions, namely heat and ash. A person who learns a piece on a dial learns what the piece smells like. A person who learns on charcoal learns the piece and the setup at the same time, which is slower and, for some temperaments, more satisfying.

The one thing no setup fixes is a bad piece. Equipment controls temperature; it does not create resin.

## Building a Reference Collection on a Budget

The people who learn agarwood fastest are not the ones who buy the most expensive pieces. They are the ones who buy a range.

A useful starting set is five samples, each small, each from a different grade or origin, each labelled with what the seller claimed and what you paid. The set does not need to include 奇楠. It needs to include a float-grade chip, a mid-grade chip, one cultivated piece, and one piece whose origin you doubt, because doubt is part of the education.

Store the samples separately, in labelled vials, and keep a notebook that records the date, the seller, the claim, and your impression on first heating and again a month later. The second impression matters: palate memory changes, and the notes will show you how.

Replace samples slowly. A collection of twenty pieces that you have smelled repeatedly is worth more than a collection of two hundred that you have handled once. The point is familiarity, not inventory.

## The First Year, Realistically

Most people who take up agarwood go through a predictable sequence, and knowing it in advance makes the sequence less discouraging.

The first month is enthusiasm without discrimination. Everything smells interesting, and the vocabulary feels unnecessary.

The third month brings the first disappointment: a piece that seemed wonderful in the shop reads flat at home, usually because the shop was warm and the home is cold, or because the piece was at the top of its range and is now exhausted.

The sixth month brings the first useful comparison. Two pieces, heated side by side, teach more than any description, and the vocabulary suddenly has referents.

The first year ends with a smaller collection than expected and a better nose than expected. The pieces that survive the year are usually the ones bought slowly, and the money saved by not chasing grades is usually the money that paid for the education.

None of this requires a school, a certificate, or an expensive heater. It requires a few grams of material, a way to warm it gently, and the willingness to write down what happened.

## A Catalogue of Defects

Agarwood fails in a small number of ways, and knowing them makes a first purchase less anxious.

**Pale chips in a dark lot.** Resin distribution is uneven, so a lot described as high grade may contain a proportion of pale material. Ask what proportion, and ask to see the worst piece rather than the best.

**Surface oil.** A slick or tacky feel means something has been applied. Genuine aged wood feels dry. The oil may be benign — some sellers oil a piece to protect it — but it changes what you are buying.

**Scorch marks.** Brown or blackened edges suggest the piece was tested on a flame rather than a plate. It still smells, but the opening is gone.

**Cracks along the grain.** Drying too fast produces splits, and splits concentrate heat at the edges. Cosmetic in a display piece, annoying on a heater.

**Mould in the soft tissue.** Damp storage leaves a musty note that survives drying. Reject it; the smell does not come out.

**Dust and grit.** Chips cut with a saw carry powder that burns rather than warms. Brush them before the first session.

**Uniform colour with no grain.** The single strongest visual warning. Natural material has variation.

**A perfect photograph.** Not a defect in the wood, but in the listing: heavy contrast, warm light, and a single angle are how ordinary material is made to look remarkable.

## What Sellers Mean by Region

Region names sell agarwood, and their meaning is looser than the price suggests.

Vietnam carries the strongest association because the historical 奇楠 trade ran through it, and the name now functions as a quality claim as much as a location. Cambodia, Malaysia, Indonesia, and India all appear on listings with their own reputations, some inherited from old trade routes and some manufactured recently.

Three problems complicate the picture. Species ranges cross modern borders, so a tree grown in one country may be sold under a neighbouring country's name. Processing often happens in a different country from harvest, which splits "origin" into two separate facts. And the most valuable names attract the most mislabelling, because the premium is in the word rather than the wood.

A buyer can respond in two ways. Ask for the country of harvest and the country of processing separately, which at least reveals whether the seller has thought about the difference. Or ignore region entirely for a first purchase and buy on the nose, using region as a refinement once a reference set exists.

## Agarwood and the Perfume Industry

Most agarwood does not become incense. It becomes oil, and the oil becomes perfume, and that market shapes what reaches the incense buyer.

Distillers buy material that is resin-bearing but not beautiful: irregular pieces, offcuts, and wood that would be awkward on a heater. The oil they produce is graded by scent, blended, aged, and sold into perfumery at prices that reflect scarcity. The best oud oils are treated as collectibles in their own right, with vintages and regional styles.

Synthetic oud compounds changed the economics. They reproduce the woody-resinous-barnyard impression at a fraction of the cost, and they are used legitimately in most mass-market fragrances. Their existence does not devalue natural oud; it simply widens the market and makes labelling more important.

For an incense buyer, the practical consequence is that chips and oil compete for the same raw material. A price for chips that looks unreasonable may simply reflect what a distiller is willing to pay for the same grade. Knowing that makes it easier to judge whether a bargain is a bargain.

## Carved Pieces, Beads, and Wearables

Agarwood is worn as often as it is heated, and the wearable market has its own logic.

Beads are usually made from material that is not quite good enough for display: small, irregular, or with pale sections that can be hidden inside the drill line. A bead strand is therefore a way to use the middle of the harvest, and the scent is a secondary pleasure rather than a session.

Carving is the opposite end. A carver wants a piece with figure, density, and enough size to hold a form, and will often prefer material with visible grain over material that is uniformly dark. Carving also exposes fresh surfaces, which release scent for a while and then settle.

Wearables need different care from chips. Skin oils, sweat, and friction all change the surface, and a bead worn daily will darken and polish over years. That patina is part of the object's life, not damage. Cleaning should be limited to a dry wipe; water and detergent will strip the surface and can lift the resin.

## Reading a Laboratory Report

When a seller provides an analytical report, the useful questions are about scope rather than about the result.

**What was tested?** A species identification from a small sample tells you about the sample, not the shipment. A report that names the piece, the weight, and the date of sampling is worth more than one that names only the species.

**Which method?** Microscopic wood anatomy, DNA barcoding, and chemical profiling answer different questions. Anatomy can identify genus and sometimes species; DNA is more specific but needs a clean sample; chemical profiling describes the volatile and semi-volatile content, which is closer to what a buyer cares about but does not establish species.

**Who issued it?** A commercial laboratory with an accreditation number is checkable. A document with a logo and no identifier is decoration.

**What does it not say?** Most reports do not grade the material, do not certify origin, and do not test for treatment. Read the limitations section before the conclusion.

## Keeping a Session Log

A log turns smelling into a practice, and it takes less effort than people expect.

Record five fields for every session: the piece, its claimed identity, the setup, the temperature or heat setting, and three lines of sensory notes at the start, middle, and end. Date each entry. That is enough to compare sessions months later, and it is far more useful than a paragraph of impressions written from memory.

The log also protects against a common self-deception. Memory for smell is poor and easily overwritten by the most recent experience, so a person who does not write things down will find that every piece they liked last year has become indistinguishable.

Review the log every few months. Patterns appear: a preference for a region, a sensitivity to a particular note, a setup that consistently disappoints. Those patterns are the beginning of a palate, and they are more valuable than any single purchase.

## Three Pieces on the Table

Abstract rules are hard to use. A worked comparison is easier, so here are three pieces of the kind a first buyer meets, described as they would present themselves.

**Piece A** is a small block, dark on one face, pale on the other, weighing more than its size suggests. The seller calls it sinking grade. On the plate it opens sharply, holds a sweet middle for ten minutes, then goes thin. The pale face is a third of the block. Reading: probably genuine, probably mid-grade, certainly not uniform. The sink test may have been run on the dark end only.

**Piece B** is a set of chips, uniformly dark, faintly glossy, smelling strongly of resin in the bag. On the plate the smell arrives immediately and does not change; after eight minutes it is gone. The chips feel slightly slick. Reading: treated. The gloss and the flat development are the tells, and the rapid exhaustion is the consequence.

**Piece C** is a light-coloured chip with visible dark veins, no smell at room temperature, and a modest price. On the plate it is quiet for two minutes, then opens into something resinous and slightly sour, holds for twenty, and ends woody. Reading: ordinary agarwood, honestly described, and the best of the three for learning, because it behaves like the material is supposed to behave.

The exercise is worth repeating with real pieces. The point is not to memorize the answers but to notice that the diagnostic information arrived in the first minutes and in the surface, not in the description. A buyer who learns to look at the pale face, feel the gloss, and wait through the quiet opening will avoid most of the market's traps without learning a single grade name.

## The Retail Label

A seller who ships across borders owes the buyer information, and the useful label is short.

Species or genus. Origin and, separately, the country of processing. Form: chip, block, powder, stick. Weight. Whether the material is wild or cultivated. Any permit reference for regulated trade. A batch or lot number so that a complaint can be matched to a shipment.

That list is unglamorous and it is exactly what resolves disputes. A label that says "premium wild sinking agarwood" and nothing else has told the buyer nothing that can be checked.

For buyers, the same list works as a request. Ask for those seven fields before paying, and treat the ability to supply them as a qualification. A seller with a good product and no paperwork is a harder relationship than a seller with a good product and a system.

## When a Purchase Goes Wrong

Disputes over agarwood follow a pattern, and the pattern favours the buyer who prepared.

The first question is always evidential: what was agreed, in writing, and what was delivered. A specification with weight, form, and category turns an argument about impressions into an argument about facts. A photograph taken before packing, with a scale, does the same work in one image.

The second question is remedy. A refund is clean; a replacement is slower; a partial credit keeps a relationship alive but leaves the buyer holding material they did not want. Decide which remedy you want before opening the conversation, because the negotiation is easier when the outcome is already clear.

The third question is timing. Most marketplaces and payment systems have windows for claims, and a dispute raised after the window closes is a conversation rather than a claim. Check the window when the parcel arrives, not when the problem appears.

None of this is specific to agarwood, and all of it matters more here because the material is expensive and the claims are hard to verify. The buyer who documents the transaction is not being suspicious; they are making the seller's honesty cheap to demonstrate.

## The Chemistry of the Scent

Analytical work on agarwood volatiles gives a concrete picture of why the material behaves as it does.

The aromatic fraction is dominated by sesquiterpenoids and their oxygenated derivatives, together with chromone compounds that are less volatile and contribute to the long tail. Named constituents reported in the literature include agarospirol, several agarofuran-type structures, and benzylacetone, the last of which is associated with a sweet, floral, slightly balsamic note. The exact profile varies by species, by injury history, and by how the wood was dried and stored.

Two practical points follow. The light, sweet notes come from the more volatile fraction, which is why they are the first to leave and the first to be destroyed by flame. The heavier chromones and oxidized sesquiterpenoids persist, which is why the tail of a good piece reads woody and slightly bitter rather than sweet.

The chemistry also explains a common disappointment. Wood that has been stored warm and exposed to light loses its light fraction over months, so a piece that was vivid when bought can be muted a year later even though nothing was done wrong in handling. Storage is not housekeeping; it is preservation of the volatile fraction the buyer paid for.

## Plantation Forestry, in Plain Terms

Cultivated agarwood is an agricultural product, and the agricultural details predict quality.

Trees are planted at a spacing that allows the trunk to grow straight and large enough to survive wounding. Pruning shapes the stem. Once the trunk is thick enough, the induction begins: holes are bored at intervals, cuts are made, or a culture is introduced to provoke the defence response. The tree then needs years to wall off the injuries and spread resin into the surrounding tissue.

Harvest and sorting decide what the buyer sees. Felled trunks are cut into sections, examined under good light, and separated by eye and weight. The resin-rich pieces go to the incense and carving trade; the pale material goes to distillation, powder, or the lower grades. A well-run operation sorts into more categories than a small one, which is why consistency differs between suppliers.

The questions a buyer can ask map directly onto that sequence: how old were the trees at harvest, how many years passed between induction and felling, and how many grades does the operation sort into. A supplier who answers all three is close to the production line. A supplier who answers none is a reseller.

## Regulation in Practice

Rules about agarwood are easy to state and tedious to follow, and the tedium is where shipments fail.

The core mechanism is a permit system attached to species listings. Exports require documentation issued by the exporting country, and imports may require corresponding paperwork at the destination. The documents travel with the goods, not with the email thread, and a customs officer works from the paper in front of them.

Three failures recur. A shipment arrives with a permit that names a different species from the invoice. A permit covers a weight that does not match the parcel. A seller ships "samples" to avoid the paperwork and the parcel is held. Each failure costs time, storage fees, and sometimes the material.

For a small buyer, the safe pattern is to buy domestically from a seller who has already imported, and to ask for the permit reference as part of the transaction record. For anyone importing directly, the paperwork should be verified before payment rather than after dispatch, because the leverage disappears once the parcel is in transit.

## Agarwood in Japanese Practice

Japanese incense culture gave agarwood a second vocabulary and a different use.

The wood is 沈香 there, jinkō, and the finest grade is 伽羅, kyara — the cognate of the Chinese 奇楠. Rather than blending it into sticks, the classical practice warms a small piece on a mica plate over buried charcoal, a method called 隔火焚香, and treats the resulting aroma as an object of attention. The verb is 聞, to listen, and the session is 聞香, monkō.

Two consequences follow for anyone buying the material in the West. First, Japanese grade names circulate internationally and are often used loosely; a "kyara" listing in an English shop may be describing a scent profile rather than a verified grade. Second, the Japanese market has its own house styles — the old incense houses of Kyoto have blended and sold these materials for centuries — and their product lines are a useful reference for what a well-made stick of a given material actually smells like.

The practical lesson is about form. Japanese practice favours small pieces heated gently, which rewards material with complexity rather than volume. A chip that seems underwhelming when burned can be remarkable on a plate, and a buyer who only ever burns will misjudge a large part of the market.

## In Arab Hospitality

The same wood appears in Gulf hospitality under a different set of words, and the usage explains a different slice of the market.

Bakhoor is the general term for the fragrant material burned in a mabkhara, the small censer passed among guests so that clothing and hair take on the scent. Oud chips and oud oil both enter that practice, and the majlis — the sitting room where guests are received — is the setting. The aroma is expected to linger on fabric for hours, which places a premium on materials with a heavy, persistent tail rather than a delicate opening.

That preference shapes demand. Wood that performs poorly on a mica plate may still be prized for bakhoor, because the burning method and the social function are different. Consumers in that market often prefer a strong, resinous, slightly smoky profile, and the trade supplies it.

For a buyer, the lesson is that "quality" is not one scale. A piece judged by the standards of Japanese listening may be unremarkable in a majlis, and the reverse is equally true. Understanding which tradition a seller is speaking to explains a great deal about how a piece is described and priced.

## The Old Trade Routes

Agarwood moved along the same sea lanes as spices, and the geography of those lanes explains why so many place names attach to the material.

The overland and maritime routes that connected the South China Sea to the Mediterranean carried aromatics westward for centuries. Ports on the Malay coast, the straits between Sumatra and the peninsula, and the harbours of the Indian Ocean served as collection points where material from several forests was pooled, sorted, and renamed. By the time a parcel reached Alexandria or Venice, its origin had usually been replaced by a quality description, because a description sold better than a coordinate.

European traders entered the same network and added their own vocabulary. The words that appear in older European herbals and travel accounts — aloeswood, lign aloes, eaglewood, agalloch — are attempts to render an unfamiliar material into a familiar frame. None of them implies a species, and several of them were applied to more than one wood.

The pattern repeats in the modern market. A famous port becomes a style name; a style name becomes a price band; the price band attracts material from everywhere. Understanding that history is the best defence against a romantic origin story, because the story has been told for five hundred years and the material has always travelled further than its name.

## Where the Money Goes

A retail price for agarwood looks arbitrary until the cost structure is laid out.

The first cost is the wood itself, bought by weight at a grade that the buyer must judge. The second is loss: sorting discards pale material, cutting and carving remove more, and a block that looked dense may yield a smaller usable piece. The third is the cost of verification — sampling, documentation, permits, and the occasional rejected lot. The fourth is logistics: packing, freight, insurance, duty, and the financing cost of stock that sits for months. The fifth is the seller's margin, which funds the showroom, the photography, and the returns.

Each layer explains a premium that buyers often read as greed. A piece sold at ten times the price of a rough chip may be ten times further along that chain, or it may simply be overpriced. The way to tell is to ask which of the five layers the seller is claiming to have added: selection, craft, documentation, convenience, or reputation. A seller who can name the layer is usually telling the truth about the price.

## Working It Into a Blend

Agarwood is usually heated alone, but it appears in blends, and knowing how it behaves with other materials prevents wasted wood.

With sandalwood, the pairing is classical: the creamy base fills the room while the agarwood supplies movement and depth. The risk is that a strong sandalwood masks the agarwood's opening, which is the part worth paying for.

With frankincense, the contrast is sharper. The resin gives a bright, citrus-adjacent lift; the wood gives a sweet, heavy middle. A small proportion of agarwood is enough, because the resin carries the top of the blend.

With floral absolutes, agarwood acts as a floor. Rose or osmanthus over an agarwood base reads lush; the same florals over a neutral base read thin. The proportion matters more than the quality of the floral, because a heavy floral load will bury the wood.

With other woods, the blend becomes a question of which wood leads. Cedar and pine are cleaner and cheaper and can support agarwood without competing; a second resinous wood usually competes and muddies the result.

The general rule is that agarwood contributes structure rather than volume. It is a base with movement, and blends that treat it as a top note waste it.

## A Checklist Before You Pay

Eight questions, in order. If three or more cannot be answered, the purchase is a gamble rather than a transaction.

1. What species, or at least what genus?
2. Where was it harvested, and where was it processed?
3. Wild or cultivated?
4. What form and what weight?
5. What grade claim is being made, and on what basis?
6. Is there any documentation, and what does it actually cover?
7. What is the return policy, and for how long?
8. Will the seller send a photograph with a scale before dispatch?

The list is deliberately boring. Boring questions produce checkable answers, and checkable answers are what separate a collector from a gambler.

## What Collectors Argue About

Three disagreements run through every conversation about agarwood, and a buyer who understands them can read a seller's claims more accurately.

**Whether density is a grade or a habit.** The sink test survives because it is objective and cheap, and collectors with a trading background still use it as shorthand. Collectors with a perfumery background argue that buoyancy says nothing about the quality of the aroma, and that a floating chip with a beautiful arc is worth more than a heavy block that smells of nothing. Both are right about different things: density measures conversion, and conversion is only one input.

**Whether cultivated material counts.** Some traditionalists treat plantation wood as a different product, arguing that age and natural injury produce a complexity that induction cannot copy. Others point out that a well-managed plantation with a long grow-out produces material that would pass unnoticed in a blind test, and that the alternative is a market supplied entirely by poaching. The argument is partly empirical and partly moral, and it will not be settled by either side.

**What the apex grade actually is.** Even the word is disputed: its etymology has been traced to Sanskrit candidates, to a temple reading, and to a mistranslation, with no consensus. Whether the category is a distinct material, an extreme degree of the same process, or a trade label applied after the fact is still debated. A buyer should treat confident pronouncements on the subject as a personality trait rather than a credential.

None of these arguments needs to be resolved to buy well. They matter because each one predicts how a seller will describe a piece, and because a seller who acknowledges the disagreement is usually more reliable than one who recites a certainty.

## Antique Pieces and Museum Objects

Old carved agarwood survives in collections as boxes, plaques, rosaries, and inlay, and the museum view of the material is different from the trader's.

Curators care about accession history, joinery, and surface patina, because those establish age and provenance. A piece with a documented collection history is valued as an object before it is valued as a scent, and its aroma may have faded decades ago. Conservators generally avoid heating such pieces: warmth drives off the remaining volatiles and can distort the surface.

For a buyer, the lesson is that the market contains two kinds of value — aromatic and historical — and they do not always coincide. A plain chip can be superb on a plate and worth nothing to a collector of carvings; a carved plaque can be magnificent and nearly scentless. Knowing which value you are paying for prevents a category error.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating 奇楠 as a species.** It is a physical state, and the label is the most counterfeited in the trade.
- **Judging by colour alone.** Uniform darkness is as likely to indicate treatment as richness.
- **Trusting the sink test as proof.** Oil injection defeats it.
- **Burning instead of heating.** A flame wastes the opening notes and produces a crude smell.
- **Reading classical medicine as clinical advice.** It is historical language, not evidence.
- **Buying "wild" without documents.** The legal risk lands on the importer.
- **Comparing prices across grades.** A per-gram figure means nothing without a category.

## The Author's Position

**Position:** The most valuable skill for a new buyer is not grading. It is recognizing a seller who can answer a species question without hesitating.

**Reasoning:** Grading vocabulary can be learned in an afternoon, and it can be recited by anyone. What cannot be faked cheaply is a documented supply chain, a willingness to sell a small sample, and a straight answer about cultivation. Those three things predict the quality of the tenth purchase far better than the description of the first.

**Boundary:** This is a buying heuristic, not a claim that documentation guarantees quality. A well-papered piece can still be disappointing on the heater, because the nose is the final judge and it has to be trained somewhere.

*This is the author's editorial view, based on trade practice rather than a controlled study.*

## FAQ

### Q: What is agarwood incense made of?

Wood from *Aquilaria* trees that has been invaded by resin after an injury, usually warmed rather than ignited. Chips are the raw form. Sticks and cones are manufactured: the wood is milled, mixed with a binder and often other aromatics, formed, and dried. In a stick, the agarwood fraction may be small, so a stick is a delivery system for a blend rather than a sample of one tree.

### Q: Why does agarwood cost so much?

Because the supply is a by-product of damage. Resin accumulates slowly, the affected part of a trunk is small, and wild harvesting is restricted by international agreement. Add the cost of verifying what you are buying and the price reflects scarcity plus the expense of trust. The grade spread is wide, which is the opening that treatment and substitution exploit.

### Q: How can I tell a real piece from a fake?

Better to use several observations than one decisive test. A cut face should show dark veins wandering through pale fibre. Gentle warmth should produce a smell that develops rather than announces itself. The surface should feel dry. The smoke, if any, should be light. Then the decisive question: will the seller put species, origin, form, and weight in writing?

### Q: How does oud relate to agarwood?

The word "oud" covers the wood in Arabic usage and, by extension, the oil distilled from it and the perfumes built on that oil. So the answer depends on which of the three the listing means. Ask whether the item is wood, distillate, or fragrance, and ask the species for the first two.

### Q: What makes the apex grade different?

Structure. Ordinary material is timber with resin channels; the apex grade is resin that has softened the fibre, so it shaves into curls, deforms under pressure, and smells before it is warmed. The distinction is physical, which is why handling tells you something before the nose gets a chance.

### Q: Burn it or heat it?

Heat it. Ignition destroys the light compounds that carry the opening, and the opening is where most of the money goes. Indirect warmth — a coal under ash and a mica plate, or an electric heater — releases the same material in stages across twenty minutes or more.

### Q: Does agarwood have health benefits?

Classical formularies describe it in those terms, and the descriptions are part of its cultural history. They are not clinical findings. No treatment claim is made here, and a listing that markets the wood as medicine is making a regulated claim rather than a fragrance claim.

### Q: How should I keep it?

Dry, dark, and away from other smells. A wooden box or a sealed jar, out of sunlight and away from heat. Keep grades apart, handle with tweezers, never wash it, never freeze it. A piece stored that way will outlast everything it was shipped in.

## Summary

- The material is a wound response, harvested only from trees that survived long enough to produce one.
- Buoyancy tracks conversion, and it is the oldest screen in the trade as well as the easiest to engineer.
- The apex grade is a structure, and the word for it is the most abused in the market.
- Warming releases the scent in phases; igniting it destroys the phase worth paying for.
- All four common fakes add something at the surface, which is why grain beats gloss.
- Paperwork, a cheap sample, and a supplier who answers plainly outperform any bench trick.

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is a scar that learned to smell. Wound an *Aquilaria* tree and it walls the damage off with a dark, aromatic tissue; given years, that tissue soaks into the heartwood around it. The tree itself is ordinary timber. The scar is the product — which is why the material is scarce, graded by weight, and counterfeited so persistently.

**Key facts:** 沉香 translates as "sinking fragrance," a name earned by pieces heavy enough to drop through water. The apex grade, 奇楠 (qínán; kyara in Japanese), is a separate physical condition rather than a rung above the ordinary one. CITES governs the wild trade, and most stock sold today came off planted land. Medical language around the material belongs to the historical record; no clinical claim is made here.

## Key Takeaways

- Agarwood comes from a wound response, and no single species owns it.
- Buoyancy is a crude proxy for converted tissue, and it is simple to engineer.
- 奇楠 names a structure: resin has softened the fiber itself.
- Flame burns off the opening notes that justify the price.
- Every common fake adds material to the outside; none rebuilds the inside.
- A seller able to name species, harvest country, and cultivation method is worth more than a certificate with no issuer.

## A Wound, Not a Species

**Direct answer:** *Aquilaria* belongs to Thymelaeaceae, and sound stock from its trees is pale, light, and almost scentless. What the trade sells is the darkened zone around an old injury.

A healthy stand yields timber, not incense. The aromatic share may be a small part of one trunk, and no one can say how much until the stem is down. That is the first economic fact of the trade: the product is a pathological by-product, unpredictable in place and in quantity.

Three near-neighbors explain most counter confusion. Sandalwood belongs to another genus, smells creamy, and owes nothing to injury — the [sandalwood guide](/blog/sandalwood-incense/) sets out the contrast. Oud is distilled from agarwood, yet it behaves nothing like a commodity, since the still is fed with chips as variable as the ones on sale. And "agarwood" covers no single plant: several *Aquilaria* species, along with the related *Gyrinops*, all ship under it.

That point deserves holding on to. A label can be botanically vague and still honest, but the buyer should know the word names a category rather than a plant.

## The Sequence Behind a Dark Piece

**Direct answer:** A seal of aromatic tissue is what the buyer eventually owns, and the order of events behind it explains the unevenness, the slowness, and the cost.

The chain runs like this:

injury arrives, whether a storm-snapped limb, a beetle gallery, or fungal entry → the host walls the breach off with resinous tissue → that tissue creeps outward into sound fiber → fresh wounds reopen the sequence, season after season → the darkened zone thickens and stiffens → harvest, either by felling or by collecting what died standing → the saw separates loaded wood from white sapwood.

Three consequences follow. The first is unevenness: one face can be black and the other cream, and cutting changes what you own. A seller who photographs the beautiful side is not automatically dishonest, but a photograph is not the object.

The second is time. Accumulation is slow, so planted stock cut after a few years tends to be lighter and less complex than old forest material. Cultivation shortens the clock; it does not abolish it.

The third is that the words wild and cultivated describe a process, not a rank. A well-run plantation with a long grow-out and careful handling can beat badly kept forest stock. What old forest material offers, when it is legal and documented, is age — and age is usually what the nose recognizes.

## What a Sink Test Actually Measures

**Direct answer:** The old water test sorts pieces into three bands, and the bands estimate how much tissue has converted. They are not a verdict on quality.

| Band | What the piece does | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| 沉水 | Settles to the bottom | Most of the fiber has converted; the heaviest load |
| 半沉半浮 | Stays suspended, one part above the surface | A middle load |
| 浮 | Rides on top | A light load; old texts give this as 黄熟香 |

Use the bands as a rough index. A floating fragment can be excellent on a heater, and a sinking block can smell dull. The test is also easy to defeat: oil injection makes pale wood heavy. A sink result is a screening step, and treating it as a certificate is how buyers pay for density that was added rather than grown.

## Weighing Instead of Guessing

**Direct answer:** Buoyancy is a yes-or-no answer to a question that has a number behind it, and the number is easy to get with a scale and a graduated jar.

Weigh the dry piece in grams. Fill a narrow cylinder with water, note the level, submerge the piece with a pin if it insists on floating, and read the rise in millilitres. Millilitres displaced equal cubic centimetres of envelope. Divide mass by volume and you hold a specific gravity figure to compare against water's 1.00.

The arithmetic earns its keep in three situations. It separates two fragments a seller describes with one adjective: a chip at 0.55 and one at 0.95 both get called dense, and only one is close to sinking. It exposes a hollow, since a block with a drilled core weighs little for the liquid it displaces. And it catches inconsistency between faces — measure twice with different faces up and the figure should barely move, while a wide gap says the loading is not uniform, whatever the surface suggests.

What the number cannot tell you is how the density arrived. Oil raises the ratio as effectively as decades of resin, so treat specific gravity as a description of the object rather than a verdict on its history. Read it beside the cut face, not instead of it.

Method note: a wide jar is useless for small chips, since a millilitre of rise vanishes into the meniscus. A narrow cylinder, a steady hand, and the same procedure every time will separate pieces that a sink test lumps together.

## The Apex Grade

**Direct answer:** 奇楠 is a separate condition of the material, not a higher step on the ordinary ladder. In it, resin has occupied the fiber rather than merely lining passages inside it.

Ordinary wood behaves like a hard board with dark threads running through it. Sellers name that 木包油 — oil held inside wood — and a sawn face shows the logic plainly: cream fiber with dark matter between. 奇楠 flips the arrangement. 油包木, wood held inside oil, points to material so completely taken over that the grain turns pliable. It peels into curls, presses into a pellet under the fingertip (削之成卷、捏之成丸), and gives scent with no heat at all.

As a screening rule, the physical difference is useful. Resistance under the thumbnail suggests ordinary stock; a little give plus a cold smell is worth a second look. Neither observation settles the grade, and a vendor who says it does is selling certainty he cannot have.

Color supplies a second set of labels — white, green, purple, yellow, black. Whether the middle names mark distinct grades or merely stages as green material darkens remains argued, which leaves the ranking customary rather than fixed. Weight of evidence should come from the invoice and the permit, not from the adjective.

The name's origin is contested. One camp reads it from Sanskrit *kāla*, "black"; another from *tagara*, a scented plant; a third from liturgical usage. A minority argues *tagara* was a translator's error and that *agaru* — or *aguru* — came first. No derivation has won, and a vendor who presents one as settled is offering assurance in place of evidence.

## Four Labels About Circumstance

**Direct answer:** The classical formation terms describe how a tree met its injury. None is visible in a finished piece, so each is a seller's account rather than an observable property.

| Term | What the label claims about the injury |
|---|---|
| 熟结 | Resin laid down in a host that died and weathered where it stood |
| 生结 | Resin laid down while the host was still growing |
| 虫漏 | Resin laid down around beetle work |
| 脱落 | Resin laid down at an exposed break |

A buyer can ask which term applies. No answer can be confirmed from the fragment in hand, so treat the vocabulary as background for a story, not as a test result.

## How the Smell Unfolds

**Direct answer:** Agarwood rewards change, not loudness, and gentle heat is simply the technique that lets change unfold in order.

Surface warms → the lightest molecules depart first → the face dries → heavier fractions come forward → what remains reads as wood. The old vocabulary for those stages is 头香 (opening), 本香 (body), 尾香 (tail). Set a flame to the same chip and the sequence disappears: everything leaves together, part of it altered by combustion, and the result is a rough copy of an expensive smell.

That is the reasoning behind 隔火焚香, heating at a distance — usually a mica sheet laid over charcoal buried in ash. A modern electric heater performs the same trick with a dial instead of a bed of ash. Either way the material gets to speak in sentences rather than one shout. Burning directly works, but it spends the top notes before anyone has heard them.

There is a diagnostic in this. A fragment that smells identical at one minute and at twenty is either simple stock or mislabeled stock. Good material moves, and the movement is the thing being sold.

## A Note on the Chemistry

**Direct answer:** The scent is not one substance but a mixture, and the mixture explains both the staged release and the way an oil differs from a chip.

The review cited under Sources describes a volatile fraction built mostly from sesquiterpenoids, with chromone derivatives and small aromatic compounds such as benzylacetone contributing the sweeter register. Two practical readings follow. The molecules that carry the opening are the smallest and most mobile, so they are also the first to escape a badly kept piece. Distillation does not merely collect the smell; it selects, concentrating some fractions and leaving others in the spent charge. That is why an oil and a heated chip from one batch read as relatives rather than twins: same family, different cut of the family.

## Species and the Geography Problem

**Direct answer:** Several species yield the material, trade names do not map cleanly onto botany, and regional labels are marketing as often as geography.

*Aquilaria sinensis* (白木香) fed the old Chinese trade and still grows through Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, and Yunnan. *Aquilaria malaccensis* is the historic export grade from Southeast Asia, now much reduced and regulated. *Aquilaria crassna* supplies a large share of planted stock out of Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. *Gyrinops* species from eastern Indonesia, New Guinea, and Sri Lanka lay down comparable material and arrive under the same loose vocabulary.

| Species | Region often cited | Bench note |
|---|---|---|
| *A. malaccensis* | Malaysia, Sumatra, Borneo | Old export wood, much reduced |
| *A. sinensis* | Southern China | Historical domestic source, 白木香 |
| *A. crassna* | Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia | Common in plantation supply |
| *A. beccariana* | Borneo, Sumatra | Regional trade literature |
| *Gyrinops versteegii* | Eastern Indonesia | Plantation interest in recent decades |
| *A. hirta* | Malay Peninsula, Sumatra | Smaller tree, often lighter load |
| *A. khasiana* | Assam, Meghalaya | Northeastern Indian material |
| *A. rostrata* | Peninsular Malaysia | Rarely traded in quantity |
| *Gyrinops walla* | Sri Lanka | Walla patta in local trade |
| *A. filaria* | New Guinea, Maluku | Remote-range species, rarely named at retail |

Genus-level detail can be checked in the [Flora of China account](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=102387). Commercially, the useful fact is that botany and trade names drift apart. A parcel labeled "Cambodian" may be naming a port, a style, or a story.

Region shapes character the way soil shapes wine: broadly, never absolutely. Hainan, Koh Kong, Quang Nam, Terengganu, Kalimantan, Assam, and Palawan all carry reputations, and every one has been attached to stock that never grew there. Ask for the harvest country and the processing country separately. The reply shows whether the seller has thought about the difference.

## Plantation Reality

**Direct answer:** Cultivation turned a scarce material into an affordable one, and in doing so filled the market with stock of wildly uneven quality. The word on the label predicts little.

Wild stock comes from trees hurt by accident and left to accumulate for decades; legal wild material needs harvest documents, and the supply is small. Cultivated stock comes from planted trees wounded on purpose — by boring, cutting, or inoculation — then harvested after a grow-out period.

The plantation sequence deserves attention, because every stage suggests a question for a supplier. Growers plant, then wait until a trunk can survive being hurt. Wounding follows: holes drilled, cuts opened, or a culture introduced to trigger the defense. Around those injuries the aromatic tissue builds over the next several years. At harvest, trunks are sectioned and sorted by eye, by heft, and by nose. Loaded lengths go to incense and carving; cream stock goes to the still, to powder, or to the bottom of the list.

Three questions follow. How long was the grow-out? Which induction method was used? How many grades does the operation sort into? A supplier who answers all three is close to production; one who answers none is a reseller with a story.

A short cycle gives light, deposit-poor stock. A long one costs the grower money and produces something closer to the old material. Neither label guarantees quality: "wild" without paperwork is an adjective, and "plantation" without a grow-out figure is a category.

## Four Ways Wood Gets Faked

**Direct answer:** Price attracts fraud, and the methods repeat often enough to learn by shape.

| Route | Workshop step | What sells the piece | First sign at the bench |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling in oil (煮油) | Cheap timber simmered until it drinks fat | Extra grams, plus a smell that sits on the outside | Tacky surface, fragrance that thins in days, sooty plume |
| Forced impregnation (高压灌油) | Oil driven into pale stock under pressure | A block that goes under in water | Fat beads at a cut edge when warmed, one flat note |
| Dyed and scented soak (泡药水) | Inert stock stained, then perfumed | Dark color and an immediate hit of scent | Color that never varies, chemical edge, tinted rinse water |
| Look-alike stock | Another genus dressed with aroma compounds | Plausible appearance at a bargain number | Grain running the wrong way, no resin threads, smells of scent rather than resin |

The thread running through all four: every one of them works on the outside, and none builds deposit within the fiber. That is why bench observations about structure beat observations about smell — dark lines wandering through cream tissue are difficult to imitate, and a block that is uniformly black with no pale inclusions deserves suspicion.

The [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) collects reference material for these checks, and the [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) explains the grade vocabulary in more depth.

## Five Bench Checks

**Direct answer:** The useful tests are cheap, quick, and cumulative. No single one proves anything.

1. **Read a fresh face.** Natural loading follows the grain, leaving cream fiber in the gaps. A new cut that is equally black edge to edge is a warning, not a bargain.
2. **Heat a sliver.** Gentle warmth should unfold the smell in stages over several minutes. One flat note, a perfumed sharpness, or scorched timber all point the same way.
3. **Watch what leaves the plate.** Old accounts describe pale smoke and a bead of oil sweating from sound stock; black soot argues for added oil or a doctored surface. Treat this as a rule of thumb, never as proof.
4. **Weigh it in the hand.** Impregnated stock feels slick or sticky; aged stock feels dry with a faint waxiness. Weight means something only beside a piece of known size, and a sink result sorts a batch rather than certifying one item.
5. **Ask for the paper trail.** Species, harvest country, and category, written down. Where those are missing, the risk has a home.

## Heat, Not Fire

**Direct answer:** Heating is not burning, and the gap between them explains how one fragment can smell ordinary on one rig and remarkable on another.

The aromatic tissue is a mixture: small mobile molecules that leave at low temperature, heavier sesquiterpenoids that need more energy, and non-volatile matter that stays behind. Bring the heat up slowly and the fractions depart in order of volatility. Drive the temperature and the order collapses.

plate temperature rises → the lightest fraction vaporizes → the nose receives the opening → the surface dries and release slows → heavier compounds dominate → the body forms → deposit near the plate exhausts → the tail thins toward woody residue.

Two rules follow. Let the plate come up to working temperature slowly, because a fast rise overshoots before the nose catches the opening. Let the session run down, because the tail is where a good fragment separates itself from a merely strong one.

| Method | Who holds the heat | Visible smoke | Where it earns its place |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charcoal under ash, mica above | The hand, by feel and habit | Barely any | Long traditional sittings, once the technique is learned |
| Electric heater with a dial | The dial, repeatably | None | Auditioning an unknown piece, side-by-side grading |
| Tealight sub-burner | Roughly, with a gap | A little | First sessions, small chips |
| Coal or ember directly | Nobody | Heavy | Blends sold cheaply, never fine stock |

An electric heater removes the two variables that ruin a beginner's session: heat and ash. It also invites impatience, since the dial makes it easy to raise the temperature when the aroma thins, and that shortens the session while speeding exhaustion. Patience is what the material rewards, which is easy to say and hard to practice.

Whatever the setup, smoke means combustion, and combustion means risk. Work in a ventilated room, never leave live charcoal unattended, keep the plate where a child or a cat cannot reach it, and treat a hot plate as a burn hazard for a good while after the session ends.

## Calibrating the Warmth

**Direct answer:** A dial setting is not a temperature, and treating it as one is the commonest reason two sessions with the same chip produce different verdicts.

Most inexpensive heaters report a number that tracks the element, not the plate. The plate lags, the room steals heat, and a draft moves the reading. A cheap infrared thermometer pointed at the plate surface, taken at the start and again after five minutes, turns the dial into something repeatable. Note the setting where the first note appears and the setting where smoke starts; the working band sits between them.

A session log pays for itself. Record the piece, the date, the plate reading at first aroma, the minutes to the body, and the minutes to the tail. Three or four entries make a personal baseline, and a baseline is what turns "this smells good" into a comparison. Without one, every session starts from zero and every seller's description sounds equally plausible.

## Chips, Blocks, and Portioning

**Direct answer:** A chip has more exposed surface for its mass, so it gives up its scent faster and runs out sooner than the block it came from.

A chip carries more surface per gram, releasing faster and depleting sooner — ideal for tasting, poor for a long sitting. A fragment the size of a rice grain is enough for a first evaluation; one the size of a pea carries a longer session.

Cutting matters. A saw leaves dust, and dust burns rather than warms, putting smoke and a harsh note into the session. A sharp blade that shaves leaves cleaner faces and less waste. The old description of 奇楠 shaving into curls is both a handling property and a diagnostic.

Portioning is the discipline beginners skip. Buy a modest quantity, split it into single-session portions, and keep them apart so a mistake with one does not consume the supply. A small glass vial per portion, labelled with date and the seller's description, is enough. Do not grind agarwood for a warmer: powder belongs in sticks and cones, where a binder carries it, and on a plate it scorches almost at once.

## From Chips to Sticks

**Direct answer:** A stick is manufactured, and the manufacturing steps decide how much agarwood you actually receive. Chips and sticks are different products that happen to share a name.

A producer starts by milling the wood. The powder is sieved, and the mesh matters: a fine flour-like fraction packs tightly and travels through the extruder as a smooth ribbon, while coarse grit leaves voids that burn unevenly and drop ash. The sieved powder is mixed with a binder — plant mucilage from bark, guar, or the Japanese makko powder — plus water, until it holds a shape. Some sticks are pressed around a thin bamboo core; others are extruded coreless. The core adds strength and a little smoke of its own. After forming, sticks dry slowly, because a fast dry sets the outside before the middle has given up its water, and the shrink that follows opens cracks along the length.

The consequence is arithmetic. A stick holds powder, binder, water that has since evaporated, and sometimes a charcoal or char filler. Aromatic stock is one input among several, and a thin resin share can be stretched with scent compounds that never saw a tree. That is why "agarwood stick" on a box says almost nothing about dose, and why a stick can smell strongly while carrying very little of the material this page describes.

| Form | What it contains | Who sets the dose | Where cheating hides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chip | Wood only | You, by the size you break off | Treatment of the chip itself |
| Loose powder | Milled wood, sometimes blended | You, by the pinch | Bulking with pale wood dust |
| Coreless stick | Powder, binder, water | The maker | Perfume compounds, filler char |
| Cored stick | The same, wrapped on bamboo | The maker, plus the core | Thin aromatic layer over a thick core |
| Cone | Coarser powder, more binder | The maker | Binder smell masking weak wood |

Reading a stick claim follows from the table. Ask what fraction is wood, which species, and whether the scent comes from the wood or from an addition. A maker who can answer is describing a recipe; one who cannot is describing a mood.

## Oil, Beads, and the Rest of the Trade

**Direct answer:** A large share of the world's agarwood ends up as oil rather than incense, and that split explains what reaches a chip buyer.

Distillers run hydrodistillation or steam, sometimes after a soak or a ferment. Yield is poor, the run is slow, and the oil is judged by scent rather than by the density scales used for solid stock. Poor yield pushes distillers toward stock that would disappoint on a warmer: bearing deposit, but irregular, small, or ugly.

The market divides along that line. The best solid stock goes to collectors and the warmer. Middle grades go to the still. Lower grades are ground, blended, or treated. A buyer who sees the split reads asking prices more accurately, since a cheap fragment offered at a premium is competing with what a distiller would pay for the same stock.

Oil and warmed wood are related experiences, not identical ones. Distillation concentrates and transforms the volatile share; the solid presents it in sequence, with the woody substrate shaping the tail. Knowing one does not mean knowing the other.

Jewellery sits at another edge of the trade. Beads often use fragments too small or too irregular for display, and carving uses pieces with figure and enough size to hold a form. Skin oil and friction darken a bead over years; that patina belongs to the object rather than counting as damage. Cleaning should be a dry wipe, since water and detergent strip the surface and can lift the deposit.

## Japan's Second Vocabulary

**Direct answer:** Japanese incense culture handed the material a second vocabulary and a different way of using it, and both travel internationally.

In Japan the material is 沈香, jinkō, and the highest class is 伽羅, kyara — cognate with 奇楠. Classical practice warms a small piece on mica over buried charcoal, the method called 隔火焚香, and treats the aroma as an object of attention. The verb is 聞, to listen; the session is 聞香, monkō.

For a Western buyer, two things follow. Japanese grade words travel far and get used loosely, so a "kyara" tag in an English shop may describe a smell rather than a verified class. The Japanese trade also runs on house styles: Kyoto's old incense houses have blended and sold these woods for centuries, and their catalogues remain a practical yardstick for what a well-made stick of a given material should smell like.

The lesson is about form. Small pieces heated gently reward complexity over volume, so a chip that seems underwhelming when burned can be remarkable on a plate. A buyer who only ever burns will misjudge a large part of the market.

## The Gulf Practice

**Direct answer:** The same material appears in Gulf hospitality under another set of words, and that usage explains a further slice of demand.

Bakhoor names fragrant material burned in a mabkhara, the small censer passed among guests so clothing and hair take on the scent. Chips and oil both enter that practice, and the majlis — the sitting room where guests are received — is the setting. The aroma is expected to cling to fabric for hours, which puts a premium on a heavy, persistent tail rather than a delicate opening.

Demand follows that preference. A piece that disappoints on a mica plate can still be prized for bakhoor, since the burning method and the social function differ. Buyers in that market often want a strong, resinous, slightly smoky profile, and the trade supplies it.

For a buyer, the lesson is that quality is not one scale. A piece judged by Japanese listening may be unremarkable in a majlis, and the reverse holds too.

## Routes and Renaming

**Direct answer:** Agarwood moved along the same sea lanes as spices, and that history explains why so many place names attach to the material.

Spice cargo and agarwood rode the same water. For centuries the routes linking the South China Sea with the Mediterranean carried aromatics west, and the ports of the Malay coast, the straits off Sumatra, and the Indian Ocean harbors worked as collection points. There, wood from many forests was pooled, sorted, and given a new name. A parcel reaching Alexandria or Venice had usually lost its origin to a quality label, since a label fetched more than a coordinate.

European traders entered the same network and added vocabulary of their own. Words in older herbals and travel accounts — aloeswood, lign aloes, eaglewood, agalloch — are attempts to fit an unfamiliar material into a familiar frame. None implies a species, and several were applied to more than one wood.

The pattern repeats today. A famous port becomes a style name, the style becomes a price band, and the band attracts stock from everywhere. That history is the best defense against a romantic origin story, because the story has been told for five hundred years and the material has always traveled further than its name.

## What the Formularies Recorded

**Direct answer:** Chinese formularies describe agarwood in a language of movement and warmth, and those phrases belong to the history of materia medica rather than to clinical evidence.

The 本草纲目 carries lines such as 行气止痛，温中降逆，补五脏 — loosely, "move the qi, ease pain, warm the middle, support the organs." They appear here because retail copy still quotes them, not because they carry evidential weight. Read them as a traditional belief system set down by physicians; nothing on this page is a treatment claim. The commercial consequence is narrower: medicine language on a label counts as a regulatory statement in many jurisdictions, and its presence says something about how a seller works.

## Permits and the Legal Supply

**Direct answer:** Forest agarwood has been cut faster than it regrows, and the answer is a permit system that shapes what can legally move.

Several *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* species appear on the CITES appendices. Cross-border trade requires permits from the exporting country and a finding that the trade will not harm wild populations. The current listing sits in the [CITES database](https://checklist.cites.org/#/en/search/output_layout=alphabetical&level_of_listing=0&show_synonyms=1&show_author=1&show_english=1&show_spanish=1&show_french=1&scientific_name=Aquilaria).

Three practical effects follow. A cross-border consignment may need paperwork a casual seller has never handled. Stock described as forest-grown without documents puts the legal exposure on whoever imports it. And planted stock has become the ordinary case because it is easier to document and easier to replace, which is why provenance is a working question rather than an academic one.

A permit is not a vague blessing; it is a form with fields. It names a species, a quantity, and a direction of travel, and it is issued against a finding that the export will not harm wild populations. Those fields are what make it checkable. If the species on the paper is *Aquilaria malaccensis* and the wood in the box is a *Gyrinops* species, the document is real and the shipment is still wrong. If the permit says forty kilograms and the parcel holds four, someone has split a consignment. Ask for the permit number and the named species before paying, and compare both against the invoice; the mismatch is the finding, not the paperwork's existence.

For an individual buyer the question is smaller. A single piece carried in luggage sits in a different category from a commercial import, and the rules that apply depend on the countries at both ends. That is a question for the relevant authority, not for a seller's reassurance, and a supplier who brushes it aside has told you how the rest of the transaction will go.

## What Drives a Quote

**Direct answer:** Agarwood has no published price list, and any page offering one is quoting a single grade or guessing. What can be described is the set of factors that move a number.

**Deposit fraction.** The dominant variable. A fragment that sinks trades in a different market from one that floats.

**Grade category.** 奇楠 sits apart, and within it color and behavior move the figure further.

**Formation and age.** Old 熟结 stock commands more than fresh cultivated stock of similar density, when either can be documented.

**Provenance and legality.** Documented, permit-supported stock costs more because the buyer is paying for the absence of a legal problem.

**Size and shape.** Large intact lengths with good figure are worth more per gram than splinters, since they can be carved, displayed, or cut to order.

**Seller tier.** A specialist with a reputation prices differently from an anonymous marketplace listing. Part of the premium is recourse.

Compare quotes only within a grade and a form. A per-gram figure for chips and one for a sink-grade block are not the same number, and treating them as comparable is how buyers talk themselves into a bargain that is not one.

## Buying in Order

**Direct answer:** Put cheap checks first and expensive commitments last.

1. **Name the use.** Warming, carving, display, and distillation are four separate purchases, and each wants a different grade.
2. **Settle budget and form.** Small chips for a heater, a palm-sized block to handle, something larger if it is to be shown.
3. **Get species, harvest country, and category in writing.** A vague reply is itself an answer.
4. **Ask for both faces, with a scale in frame.** One flattering angle is a portrait, not a description.
5. **Start with a sample.** Warm it, follow it for twenty minutes, and set it against a reference piece if you own one.
6. **Spend more only after the sample earns it.** Trust is built on the second order, not the first.
7. **File the paperwork with the wood.** Species, origin, permits, and the seller's own wording, kept together.

The [natural vs synthetic](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/) page is useful background for the substitution problem, since the same logic applies to fragrance compounds across the trade.

## Storage

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is stable, and handling does most of the damage that time does not.

Keep pieces out of sunlight, away from heat, and away from anything strongly scented. Loaded tissue absorbs odours from its surroundings, and a fragment stored beside a scented candle will carry that smell. A wooden box or a sealed glass jar works; plastic is acceptable only when the piece is fully dry and the bag clean.

Lift with tweezers or clean fingers, and avoid touching the face you intend to warm, since skin oil oxidizes and turns rancid on the surface. Do not wash agarwood: water raises the grain, can mobilize the deposit, and encourages mould in softer tissue. Do not refrigerate or freeze; condensation cycles do more harm than room temperature. If a piece arrives damp, let it dry slowly in shade and moving air before sealing it away.

Time alone does little damage, yet the lightest aromatic share — the one that carries the opening — leaves slowly through any container. Daylight, warmth, and moving air accelerate that loss, and a fragment kept near a scent source trades its own character for a foreign one. Nothing is decaying in the way food decays; the object is shedding the most volatile part of what it was bought for. Dark, dry, sealed, and away from the kitchen is the whole method.

## Reading a Listing

**Direct answer:** A listing is a sales document, and the way it is built shows what the seller actually knows.

Four fields separate a real listing from a mood: name of species, country of harvest, planted or wild, and weight. A page of poetry with no species named is selling atmosphere.

Look at photographs for scale and for both faces. A hand, ruler, or coin gives size; a single face gives the best angle. Warm light flatters color, and heavy contrast hides pale inclusions. A seller who shows the pale side is showing something a faker would hide.

Read the grade claim carefully. "Sinking" describes a test that can be repeated. "Super" and "king" are adjectives. "Wild" names a category that depends on paperwork. "Kyara-grade" is a comparison that means little without a reference in hand.

Paper needs the same skepticism. A CITES permit shows that a consignment was declared and cleared for export; it says nothing about whether the boxes match the invoice, and it grades nothing. A laboratory sheet can name a species from a sample, but the sheet is only as good as three things: whether the sample was cut from the object in front of you, whether the laboratory can be identified, and whether the test covers one piece or a whole consignment. A certificate printed by the seller proves one thing only — that the seller owns a printer. Rank the evidence: legality first, independent species work second, the rest as support.

## Blind Tasting

**Direct answer:** The fastest way to learn agarwood is to remove the labels.

Take three different pieces whose identities you know, code them, and have someone present them in random order. Smell each on one warmer, at one temperature, with a minute between. Write a line per piece before checking the code. Repeat across sessions, then swap one piece out.

The exercise teaches two things reading cannot. Smell memory is weak and needs an anchor, which the written line supplies. Expectation dominates early judgment, which is why blind presentation is the only way to learn what you actually notice.

A pair test is a useful variation: two pieces, one variable. Same region different grade, same grade different region, or one stock at two temperatures. A single variable is easier to detect, and detection builds vocabulary. Keep the notes; after a few months they become a reference no listing can replace.

## What a Laboratory Can and Cannot Tell You

**Direct answer:** Laboratory work settles identity far better than it settles origin, and it says nothing at all about grade.

DNA barcoding can name a species from a small sample, which matters when a listing claims *A. malaccensis* and the box holds something else. Wood anatomy narrows material to a genus and sometimes a group, and it can show whether a piece is a solid block or a glued assembly. GC-MS profiles the volatile fraction, which is useful for spotting added fragrance compounds and for comparing two samples side by side.

Each method has a boundary worth knowing before you pay for it. A species name does not establish a country of harvest, since the same tree grows across borders and logs move. A volatile profile does not date a piece or prove cultivation method. And every result inherits the sample: a shaving taken from the dark face of a block describes that face, not the block. Ask what was sampled, by whom, and whether the report covers one object or a consignment. A laboratory that will not answer those questions is selling a stamp.

## Three Pieces on the Table

**Direct answer:** Rules become usable only when they meet an object, so here are three pieces of the sort that show up in a first purchase, read against the checks above.

| What arrives | What the seller says | What the bench shows | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| A palm block, one face black, the other cream, heavier than it looks | "Sinking grade" | Opens fast, sweet for ten minutes, then thins; the pale side is a third of the block | Likely genuine, likely middle grade, plainly not uniform; the sink test may have been run on the dark end alone |
| A bag of chips, evenly black, faintly shiny, loud through the plastic | "Old stock" | The note lands at once and never moves; gone inside eight minutes; fingers feel slick | Treated. Gloss plus a flat curve plus quick exhaustion is the signature |
| A pale chip, dark threads visible, silent at room temperature, modest price | "Ordinary plantation" | Quiet for two minutes, then resinous and slightly sour, twenty good minutes, woody finish | Honest material, and the most instructive of the three because it behaves the way the wood is supposed to |

## What Goes Wrong in Transit

**Direct answer:** Most damage to a good piece happens between the seller's shelf and yours, and the failures are boring: humidity, heat, paperwork, and repacking.

A parcel that crosses climate zones collects condensation. Wood that has absorbed moisture and then sits in a sealed bag develops a dull surface and, in soft tissue, mould. A desiccant sachet, a breathable inner wrap, and a box that does not press on the piece will prevent most of it. Heat is the second enemy: a container parked in the sun can reach temperatures that drive off the volatile top notes in a week, which is why a summer shipment may arrive smelling flatter than the sample did.

Paperwork fails in predictable ways too. A permit that names one species and an invoice that names another will stop a consignment, and a split shipment that no longer matches the declared weight invites questions no honest buyer wants. Keep the permit, the invoice, and photographs of the sealed parcel together, and photograph the piece as it comes out of the wrap, before you handle it. If the surface looks different from the listing, the images are your only record.

| Stage | Failure | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Packing | Piece rattles against a hard box | Cushion all faces; never rely on a single layer |
| Transit | Condensation after a cold leg | Desiccant plus a breathable inner wrap |
| Storage on arrival | Sealed while still damp | Rest it in shade and moving air first |
| Customs | Species or weight mismatch | Compare permit, invoice, and label before shipping |
| Handling | Skin oil on the working face | Lift by the edges, or with tweezers |

## A Buyer's Heuristic

**Position:** A new buyer's best skill is spotting a seller who answers a species question without hesitation.

Grading vocabulary can be memorized and recited. A documented chain, a willingness to sell a sample, and a straight answer on cultivation cannot be faked cheaply, and they predict the tenth purchase better than the first description. This is a buying heuristic, not a guarantee that paperwork equals quality; a well-papered piece can still disappoint on the plate. That boundary matters, because it keeps the advice from turning into a promise.

*This is the author's editorial view, based on trade practice rather than a controlled study.*

## FAQ

### Q: What is agarwood incense made of?

The starting material is *Aquilaria* heartwood that a dark deposit has invaded after injury, and it is usually warmed rather than set alight. Chips are the raw form. Sticks and cones are built: milled stock, a binder, often other aromatics, formed and dried. In a stick the aromatic share can be tiny, which makes the stick a delivery vehicle for a blend rather than a specimen from one tree.

### Q: How can I tell a real piece from a fake?

Use several observations rather than one decisive test. A cut face should show dark veins wandering through cream tissue. Gentle warmth should produce a smell that develops rather than announces itself. The surface should feel dry. Smoke, if any, should be pale. Then the question that settles more than any bench trick: will the seller put species, harvest country, form, and weight in writing?

### Q: How does oud relate to agarwood?

The word "oud" covers the wood in Arabic usage and, by extension, the oil distilled from it and the perfumes built on that oil. The answer depends on which of the three a listing means. Ask whether the item is wood, distillate, or fragrance, and ask the species for the first two.

### Q: Do I need a special heater, or will a stick burner do?

A stick burner is a convection device: it drags air past a lit ember and never holds a stable, low plate temperature. Agarwood wants the reverse. An adjustable electric warmer, or charcoal under ash with mica, lets you keep a fragment just below the point of smoking, which is where the opening lives. The wrong tool does no damage to the material; it merely guarantees you never hear what it can do.

## Summary

- Agarwood is a wound response in *Aquilaria*, not a species.
- Buoyancy tracks conversion and is easy to engineer; measure volume as well as mass.
- The apex grade is a structure, and its name is the most abused in the market.
- Warming releases scent in phases; flame destroys the phase worth paying for.
- The fakes add surface, so grain beats gloss; paperwork and a sample beat any bench trick.

**Direct answer:** 江南李主帐中香 is a classical 合香 attributed to 李煜 (Li Yu), the last ruler of the Southern Tang — hence "江南李主," "Lord of Jiangnan" — whose most famous version steams agarwood and a trace of sandalwood inside hollowed goose pears so the wood takes on a clean, sweet fruit note.

**Key facts:** The recipe is one of the best-documented in the canon, surviving in several 香乘 versions. Its "assistant" is a fruit, and its "guide" is a process — steaming and aging — rather than an ingredient.

---## The Goose-Pear Version, Step by Step

**Direct answer:** The flagship form reads 沉香末一两，檀香末一钱，鹅梨十枚 — one liǎng agarwood powder, one qián sandalwood powder, and ten goose pears, hollowed, filled, steamed, and aged.

The method runs like this: hollow each pear like a jar, fill it with the two powders, seal it with the pear's own top, steam three times (蒸三溜), strip the peel, grind and blend what remains, then age (久窨) before burning.

| Role | Ingredient | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 君 (chief) | 沉香 agarwood | Supplies the deep, resinous backbone — the formula exists to carry it |
| 臣 (minister) | 檀香 sandalwood | Adds a trace (1/10 by weight) that warms and rounds the agarwood |
| 佐 (assistant) | 鹅梨 goose pear | Lends a clean, sweet top note through its juice |
| 使 (guide) | steaming + aging | The process that fuses the fruit into the wood |

Two mechanism chains explain why the recipe is built this way.

**Chain one — why steam, and why three times.** The agarwood is packed into the pear's hollow and sealed, then heated with steam. The pear's juice is mostly water with dissolved sugars and a faint clean fruit character, and steam drives that liquid into the wood's pores → the sugars and fruit volatiles migrate into the resin-rich agarwood fibers → repeated steaming (three rounds) forces the infusion deeper each cycle → the wood emerges carrying a sweet top note it never had raw. Steaming once would only wet the surface; three passes push the infusion inward.

**Chain two — the trace of sandalwood.** The formula adds only one qián of sandalwood against a full liǎng of agarwood, a 10:1 ratio. That small dose is the mechanism for balance: sandalwood's soft, warm, slightly milky tone rounds the sharper edges of the agarwood without competing with it → the blend reads warm and continuous rather than one-note → the chief stays dominant while the minister just smooths it. A heavier sandalwood hand would bury the agarwood; the trace is what keeps the structure disciplined.

---## The Other Recorded Versions

**Direct answer:** The 香乘 records the same formula idea — agarwood as the body — realized three ways: fruit-infused, juice-steamed, and resin-and-spice.

**Version 2 — pear juice steamed into agarwood (又方一):** 沉香一两 (one liǎng agarwood, cut to wick-sized pieces) and 鹅梨一窗 (one goose pear, minced to juice), steamed in a silver vessel three times until the pear juice is dry.

**Version 3 — the resin-and-spice form (又方二):**

| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| agarwood (沉香) | 四两 (4 liǎng) |
| sandalwood (檀香) | 一两 (1 liǎng) |
| musk (麝香) | 一两 (1 liǎng) |
| borneol (龙脑) | 半两 (0.5 liǎng) |
| 马牙香 (a resin/aromatic) | 一分 (1 fēn) |

Version 3 is cut fine without sieving, bound with refined honey, and burned. A further 香乘 entry soaks agarwood in storax oil (苏合油) for a hundred days and finishes it with rose water (蔷薇水).

The three versions share one idea — agarwood as the body — but change the delivery: fruit infusion in the first, juice steaming in the second, and a resin-and-spice base in the third. The goose-pear version is the most famous because its 10:1:10 ratio (one liǎng agarwood, one qián sandalwood, ten pears) makes the chief's dominance concrete.

The classical units are 两, 钱, and 分, and the honest rule is to read ratios rather than convert to grams — the full unit table lives in the [recipe library](/blog/chinese-incense-recipes/). The famous goose-pear version is a 10:1 agarwood-to-sandalwood ratio, and Version 3 is a 4:1 ratio with musk equal to the sandalwood.

## FAQ

### Q: What kind of incense is the Jiangnan Lord's bedchamber blend?
It is a bedchamber incense tied by tradition to 李煜 (Li Yu), the last ruler of the Southern Tang — "江南李主" means "the Lord of Jiangnan." In its best-known form, agarwood powder and a small amount of sandalwood are packed into hollowed goose pears and steamed, so the wood soaks up a clean, sweet fruit character before it is burned. The 香乘 preserves several variants of the formula, which is why it ranks among the best-documented classical recipes. The Li Yu link is a traditional, widely repeated label rather than a signed document, so the page treats it as a name, not verified authorship.

### Q: Why put incense inside a pear?
Because the pear is the formula's assistant (佐), and it works by infusion rather than by contributing a note of its own. The agarwood powder is stuffed into hollowed pears, the fruit is sealed and steamed three times, and the juice carries a clean, sweet, faintly cool character into the wood. It is an early and unusually clear case of fruit used as an incense ingredient — proof that an assistant can be a fruit, not just another precious wood or resin. The pear also makes the recipe's structure visible: the agarwood chief stays dominant, and the fruit shapes only the top note.

### Q: Are there other versions of this formula?
Yes, the 香乘 gives several. One steams minced goose-pear juice into agarwood wick pieces three times in a silver vessel. Another uses a resin-and-spice base — four liǎng agarwood, one liǎng sandalwood, one liǎng musk, half a liǎng borneol, and a trace of a resin called 马牙香, bound with refined honey. A further entry soaks agarwood in storax oil (苏合油) for a hundred days and finishes it with rose water (蔷薇水). All three realize the same idea — agarwood as the body — through different processes: fruit-infused, juice-steamed, or resin-and-spice.

### Q: Is the Li Yu attribution reliable?
No. It is traditional and widely repeated, but there is no signed, verifiable document proving Li Yu wrote the recipe. The connection is plausible — Li Yu was the last Southern Tang ruler and a famous poet, and "江南李主" clearly points to him — but the formula survives in Ming-era manuals like the 香乘, compiled centuries after his death. The page therefore treats the attribution as a well-established label rather than confirmed authorship, and notes the same caution applies to the pear variety, which is not fixed today.

### Q: Why steam three times instead of once?
Because each steaming pass drives the pear's juice and dissolved sugars deeper into the agarwood's pores, and a single pass only wets the surface. The sugars and fruit volatiles migrate into the resin-rich fibers with each cycle, so three rounds push the infusion inward rather than leaving it as a surface coating. The result is a sweet top note fused through the wood, not just a wet exterior that would burn off first.

## The Steam Infusion, as a Physical Process

**Direct answer:** Steaming the filled pears is not a ritual flourish — it is a heat-driven transfer of juice into wood pores, and the physics of that transfer is why the recipe calls for three passes.

Trace the transfer. Steam carries heat and water vapor into the sealed pear → the pear's juice, mostly water with dissolved sugars and faint fruit volatiles, warms and thins → the warm liquid wets the agarwood powder's exposed pores → the sugars and volatiles diffuse into the resin-rich fibers along a concentration gradient → the wood cools slightly as the steam passes, drawing the liquid deeper by capillary pull. A single pass saturates only the surface; each repeat re-wets and pushes the infusion a little farther in, so three passes are the maker's way of reaching depth without scorching the wood. The peel is stripped afterward because it has given up its juice and would only add spent fruit fiber to the burn.

## Why the Chief Is Dosed at Ten Times the Minister

**Direct answer:** The 10:1 agarwood-to-sandalwood ratio is not a random habit — it is the 合香 principle of dominance made numeric, and it works through a balance that breaks the moment the two woods near equality.

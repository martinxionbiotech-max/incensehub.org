**Direct answer:** 奇楠 (*qínán*) is not its own tree. It is the ceiling of the *Aquilaria* agarwood ladder — wood so completely charged with hardened resin that the fiber softens, bends, and gives off scent while still cold. The same material turns up in Western and Japanese writing as **kyara**, a word that reached English through Japan, not China.

**Key facts:** The entire grade rests on a single inversion of structure. Everyday agarwood is "wood holding oil" (木包油) — a woody skeleton with resin pooled in pockets. 奇楠 is the mirror image, "oil holding wood" (油包木): the resin has so thoroughly flooded the fiber that resin becomes the continuous body and wood the filler. That one flip is what makes the grade scarce, costly, and — the part buyers care about — physically hard to counterfeit.

## A State of Matter, Not a Species

**Direct answer:** 奇楠 belongs on top of the agarwood grade ladder rather than beside it in the botany books — it is *Aquilaria* wood pushed to its most resin-charged extreme, not a parallel plant.

The distinction stops sounding academic the moment a price is attached. Because 奇楠 is a rung on a ladder and not a separate botanical, any object sold under that name is asserting something about *degree* — how completely resin has infiltrated the fiber — rather than about identity. A real 奇楠 sliver and a cheap agarwood chip are the same species; what separates them is concentration, a property you cannot see until you probe it.

That is why the [agarwood entity page](/ingredients/agarwood/) keeps the botanical identity in one place and treats 奇楠 as the ladder's summit. Species, formation, provenance — all of that lives on the entity page. The grade is a judgment draped over it, never a species of its own.

A warning worth flagging early: the color names people drape over 奇楠 — 白 (white), 绿 (green), 紫 (purple), 黄 (yellow), 黑 (black) — are *disputed market vocabulary*, not a botanical sorting system. I flag them as such instead of repeating them as fact, because calling a color a "grade" is the exact move that misleads most buyers. Color describes a look; the grade describes resin saturation; the two are not the same claim.

## Why the Classic Tests Resist Forgery

**Direct answer:** The traditional fingerprint of 奇楠 — it shaves into curls, kneads into a pellet, and smells while unheated — resists fakery because all three behaviors depend on resin lodged *inside* the cell walls, where a surface treatment cannot reach.

This mechanism is the single most useful idea for anyone handed a "奇楠" at a friendly price. Trace the chain end to end:

- Genuine 奇楠 forms as resin saturates the wood over many years → the resin fills cell walls and voids through the whole thickness → the fiber loses its stiff, splintery character → the piece shaves into curls and can be rolled into a pellet (削之成卷、捏之成丸).

A counterfeiter working from ordinary wood can only paint the outside. A boiled or injected piece carries its added weight in a thin outer skin → the interior stays woody and brittle → the piece refuses to curl or knead the way a saturated one does → the old touch test exposes the gap even though the object looks and weighs correct. The test works precisely because it reads the interior, not the surface.

The same reasoning protects the unheated-aroma check:

- Cold fragrance comes from volatile resin compounds distributed through the whole material → those volatiles escape steadily from every part, not just the outer face → the scent holds and stays layered over time.
- A fake loaded with surface oil or synthetic perfume reads strong at first → the added volatiles sit only skin-deep → they dissipate fast or collapse into one flat note → the "aromatic even unheated" claim falls apart the moment you sniff for minutes instead of seconds.

Neither test is unbeatable alone — pressure injection can push oil deeper — but both are hard to fake *cheaply*, and cheap fakery is what the 奇楠 trade runs on. That is the practical reward of understanding the mechanism instead of memorizing a checklist.

*This is editorial analysis; the touch and aroma tests are traditional trade descriptions, not laboratory methods.*

## How the Name Traveled West

**Direct answer:** The English word "kyara" is the Japanese reading (伽羅) of 奇楠, and it entered Western vocabulary first because kōdō writing reached English audiences before the Chinese incense tradition was widely studied.

The route is a compact lesson in how terminology drifts. Chinese 奇楠 → Japanese *kyara* → English "kyara," with Japan's incense-appreciation culture acting as the courier. The result: the "English" name for a Chinese grade actually arrives through a Japanese lens, which is why one material shows up under two names that look unrelated.

Where 奇楠 itself comes from is genuinely unsettled. Sanskrit roots, a possible confusion with *tagara*, and other proposals all have partisans, and the honest position is "disputed." The [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) lays the competing accounts side by side without crowning a winner. I treat the name as a label of convenience, not a settled historical fact.

## Confusion vs: 奇楠 Against Its Nearest Rivals

**Direct answer:** Two mix-ups cost buyers money — 奇楠 versus ordinary agarwood, and 奇楠 versus treated fakes — and they differ in exactly one dimension: where the resin sits.

| Signal | Genuine 奇楠 | Ordinary agarwood | Treated fake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where the resin lives | Throughout the fiber | In pockets and veins | Coated or injected near the surface |
| Feel when cut | Curls and kneads | Splintery, woody | Brittle core under an oily skin |
| Scent while cold | Fragrant, layered, durable | Faint or none until heated | Loud at first, then flat |
| Price logic | Legitimately extreme | Moderate | Suspiciously "reasonable" |
| Relation to agarwood | Its top grade | Its everyday grade | Not authentic |

The table folds down to one axis: **resin depth.** Genuine 奇楠 is resin edge to edge; ordinary agarwood is resin in spots; a fake is resin on the shell. Every practical test is really a probe of that depth. Once you see it that way, the question "which test do I trust" becomes "which test reaches the interior" — and the answer is the ones that cut, knead, or sniff across time.

## When the Name Stops Meaning Anything

**Direct answer:** The word 奇楠 earns its keep only while it points at the saturated grade — the instant it is used to dress up ordinary or treated wood, it quits being a grade and becomes a marketing term.

My boundary is explicit: the grade claim holds only when a seller can say *why* the material qualifies, not merely that it carries the label. "奇楠" means "top resin-saturated grade of agarwood" — it does not mean "any pricey dark wood," and it does not mean "agarwood that smells nice." A seller who cannot name the *Aquilaria* species, the origin, or the grade ladder is selling a word, not a material.

The practical significance for a collector: most of what is marketed as 奇楠 is not. Chasing the name without grasping the grade is exactly the behavior the fakes are engineered to catch. When the price reads "agarwood" but the label reads "奇楠," the label is the liar.

## Reading the Grade Without a Lab

**Direct answer:** For most buyers the honest path is not one magic test but a seller who can be held to account, backed by the physical checks only where the value justifies them.

What your hands can verify — curl, knead, unheated aroma held over time — tells you about resin depth. What your eyes cannot verify is species, resin percentage, or natural-versus-synthetic, and those are the very properties the grade depends on. Photographs, video, even a piece in your hand will not establish *Aquilaria* species or saturation level. Vision only flags gross fakery — a suspiciously uniform dyed color, a compressed texture — but it cannot confirm the grade.

That leaves a structural gap: every visible property can be imitated, while the defining properties sit below the surface. The gap is the argument for documentation — a written species claim, batch-linked test reports, CITES paperwork — and for a seller you can return to. The deep dives live in [qinan authentication](/blog/qinan-buying-authentication/) and the [incense authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

## Common Mistakes

1. **Treating 奇楠 as a different species.** It is the top grade of *Aquilaria* agarwood — the difference is resin concentration, not botany.
2. **Judging by color alone.** The five-color hierarchy is disputed market talk, not a reliable grading system.
3. **Trusting one physical test in isolation.** Curl, knead, and cold aroma each probe resin depth, but a skilled treatment can beat any single one cheaply enough.
4. **Buying "affordable 奇楠."** The price gap is so wide that a bargain under that name is a contradiction in terms.

## FAQ

### Q: What exactly is 奇楠, in plain terms?
Begin with agarwood and push the resin to its limit: the wood fiber becomes so full of hardened resin that it turns soft, flexible, and fragrant without any heat. That saturated condition is 奇楠. The same *Aquilaria* tree yields both ordinary agarwood and this summit grade; what changes is how completely the resin has taken over the fiber. The traditional shorthand opposes the two as "oil holds wood" (油包木) for 奇楠 and "wood holds oil" (木包油) for the everyday grade — in one the resin is the continuous substance, in the other it sits in pockets. Because the saturation runs edge to edge, a genuine piece curls when shaved, rolls into a pellet, and releases scent at room temperature. Those three behaviors — curl, knead, cold aroma — are the practical fingerprint. The [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) carries the full ladder.

### Q: Does 奇楠 come from its own species of tree?
It does not. There is no 奇楠 species; the grade is a material condition of *Aquilaria* agarwood, reached when resin impregnation is complete. The plant stays the same; the degree of saturation is what shifts. This is why the [agarwood entity page](/ingredients/agarwood/) keeps the botanical identity in one place and files 奇楠 as the top rung of that one plant's ladder. Think of it as the difference between a fine cut and the animal it comes from — one creature, many degrees of quality.

### Q: Where did the name "kyara" come from?
By way of Japan. The characters 奇楠 are read in Japanese as 伽羅, *kyara*, and Japanese kōdō (香道) writing reached English readers before the Chinese incense tradition was widely studied. So the familiar Western word for a Chinese grade is a Japanese reading of it. Where 奇楠 itself originates is genuinely unsettled — Sanskrit roots, a possible confusion with *tagara*, and other proposals all circulate — and the honest answer is that no single etymology is established. The [grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) lays the competing accounts side by side without picking a winner.

### Q: Can a photograph confirm 奇楠 for me?
It cannot, and that limitation is worth absorbing before you spend. A picture — or a piece in your hand — will not tell you the *Aquilaria* species, the resin percentage, or whether the aroma is natural, and those three properties are precisely what the grade is. The checks that do mean something are tactile and olfactory: does it curl and knead, and does the cold scent hold up over minutes instead of flattening? Vision only catches gross fakery, like a suspiciously uniform dyed color. For anything costly, pair the physical checks with a seller who names species and origin and offers documentation. See [qinan authentication](/blog/qinan-buying-authentication/).

### Q: Why is the grade so difficult to fake convincingly?
Because the grade is defined by where the resin sits, not by how the piece looks. A forger can coat, dye, or inject the surface of ordinary wood, but the defining behaviors — curling under a blade, kneading into a pellet, holding a layered cold scent — come from resin distributed through the full thickness, which a surface treatment never reaches. A convincing fake would require actually saturating the wood, which is slow and expensive, and at that point the counterfeiter is essentially producing a lower grade of the real thing rather than a cheap copy. The tests work because they probe depth; cheap fakes live only on the surface.

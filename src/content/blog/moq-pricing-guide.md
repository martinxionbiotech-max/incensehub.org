**Direct answer:** Incense at business-to-business scale is priced by quotation, not by a published list, because the unit cost is a function of choices the buyer makes at inquiry time — material grade, run length, customization, packaging, and delivery terms. A number issued without those inputs is not a price; it is a placeholder.

## Key facts

This page explains why the quotation model exists, what a well-built quote contains, how to read one line by line, and how to compare two quotes that were written on different terms. It states no MOQ figures, no unit prices, and no margins. Those values are genuinely settled per inquiry, and any page that prints them is either quoting one fixed configuration or inventing a number.

## Key Takeaways

- Pricing is a function of specification. Change the material, the run length, or the delivery term and the unit cost changes with it.
- Minimum order quantity is the visible edge of a fixed cost block. The block does not disappear at small volume; it concentrates.
- A quote is a document with parts — material, quantity band, tooling, packaging, freight term, validity. Missing parts are where disputes start.
- Two quotes cannot be compared until they are normalized to the same delivery term, the same packaging, and the same validity window.
- "On request" is honest when the inputs really do move the number. It becomes evasion only when the supplier will not name the drivers after a real brief.

## Why the Industry Quotes Per Inquiry

**Direct answer:** In contract manufacturing the object being sold usually comes into existence after the buyer specifies it, so there is no single configuration for a published number to describe.

Retail works the other way around. The object sits on a shelf, already made, already packed, and the buyer takes one. The price is attached to a thing that exists. Incense supply at trade volume inverts that sequence: the inquiry arrives first, the specification is agreed, materials are committed, and only then does a finished batch exist. Cost is incurred in that order and priced in that order.

The chain is short enough to state in one line: brief → specification → material booking → line setup → run length → cost per unit → quote. Every arrow is a place where a decision changes the outcome. A supplier who publishes one number has picked one path through that chain and hidden the rest.

There is a second reason, less flattering to the industry. Botanical materials move. A resin that was available last quarter may be short this quarter, and a price printed six months ago would be a promise the supplier cannot keep. Quotation lets the number track the market instead of drifting away from it.

## What a Quote Should Contain

**Direct answer:** A usable quote reads like a short specification with prices attached, not like a single figure on a line; if a section is absent, that absence is a question to ask before you compare anything.

| Line in the quote | What it must state | What fails when it is absent |
|---|---|---|
| Material and grade | Species or resin name, grade band, natural vs reconstituted vs fragrance-oil blend | Two suppliers quote "sandalwood" and mean different materials |
| Unit price with quantity bands | Price at named quantities, not one price at one volume | You cannot see where the setup cost is recovered |
| Tooling, plate, or mould charges | Whether charged once, per run, or amortized into unit price | A second order arrives with an unexpected repeat charge |
| Sample charge and credit terms | Cost of the sample run, and whether it is credited against bulk | Sample economics get confused with bulk economics |
| Packaging specification | Box type, board, print method, artwork responsibility, carton count | Retail-ready assumptions surface as a change order |
| Delivery term and named place | The Incoterm plus the specific port, airport, or warehouse | Two quotes look comparable and are not |
| Lead time reference point | Whether the clock starts at deposit, at artwork approval, or at PO | Schedule slips are argued rather than planned |
| Validity period | The date the number expires, and what happens to material pricing after it | A quote from last season is treated as current |
| Payment terms | Deposit share, balance trigger, currency, and who bears transfer cost | The effective price differs from the quoted price |
| Inspection and defect allowance | AQL basis or agreed defect rate, and who pays for rework | Quality disputes become price disputes |

Ten lines is not bureaucracy. Each one corresponds to a cost that has to land somewhere, and the only question is whether it lands in the number you were shown or arrives later as an invoice.

## Reading a Quote: Which Variable Dominates

**Direct answer:** Every quote has one dominant driver, and identifying it tells you whether to negotiate on volume, on specification, or on packaging — negotiating the wrong variable wastes the conversation.

| Order profile | Dominant cost driver | Leverage that actually works |
|---|---|---|
| Small run of a standard product | Fixed setup spread thin | Raise quantity, or combine several scents into one run |
| Custom fragrance or custom box | Development and tooling | Simplify the brief, or accept a stock package |
| High-value botanical | Raw material grade | Move grade band, or blend a portion of a lower grade |
| Large plain-bulk order | Volume and freight | Negotiate the delivery term and the sailing schedule |
| Retail-ready packaging | Packaging run size | Standardize cartons across SKUs |

The table is a diagnostic, not a rule. Its value is that it forces a specific answer to "why is this figure what it is," and that answer is worth more than any number you might memorize. *Editorial judgment, not a verified fact.*

## Backing the Setup Cost Out of Two Quotes

**Direct answer:** If a supplier will price the same specification at two different quantities, the pair of numbers reveals how much of the cost is fixed and how much scales — which is the single most useful thing a buyer can learn before negotiating.

The reasoning is arithmetic rather than trade secret. Quote a small run and a large run of the identical specification. The difference in total price between the two is driven mostly by the material and labour that scale with volume. Extend that difference back toward zero units and the remainder is the fixed block: setup, tooling, artwork, and the administrative floor under any order.

What you do with that reading depends on which component dominates. If the fixed block is large relative to the variable part, quantity is your lever — a longer run dilutes the block and the unit price falls. If the variable part dominates, quantity buys you very little and you should be arguing about material grade or packaging instead. Buyers routinely push for volume discounts on orders where volume is not the problem, and the two-quote reading is what stops that.

Two cautions keep this honest. The comparison only holds if both quotes describe the same specification; a "larger quantity" quote that quietly drops a grade is not a data point. And many suppliers price in steps tied to how they buy material — a full roll, a full drum, a full pallet — so the curve is a staircase rather than a smooth line. The staircase itself is information: it tells you which lot sizes the factory actually buys in.

## Three Different Floors Under an Order

**Direct answer:** MOQ, practical minimum, and freight minimum are separate thresholds, and the highest of the three is the real entry point for a buyer.

MOQ is what the supplier states as the smallest order they will accept. Practical minimum is a production fact: the run length below which setting up the line costs more than the order returns, which for custom work is often larger than the stated MOQ. Freight minimum is a logistics fact: below a certain chargeable weight or volume, you pay for space you do not fill, whether by air or by less-than-container load.

Put them in sequence and the consequence is plain: a factory quoting a low MOQ → a consolidator charging a minimum shipment → a buyer ordering at the factory's floor still pays freight as if the shipment were larger → the effective cost per unit at the entry point is far above the quoted unit price. That is why experienced buyers ask about the freight floor before they celebrate a low MOQ.

A related trap sits on the other side. A supplier may hold a low MOQ per scent while requiring a much larger total order across the shipment. Per-SKU minimum and per-order minimum are different commitments, and they are worth separating in writing.

## Comparing Quotes Written on Different Terms

**Direct answer:** An EXW quote is not cheaper than a DDP quote; it is a smaller quote, and the missing legs have to be added before the two can be placed side by side.

Each Incoterm hands a different set of obligations to a different party. Start from the factory gate and walk outward: an ex-works price covers goods at the seller's premises and nothing beyond → a free-on-board price adds export clearance and loading at origin → a cost-and-freight price adds main carriage to the destination port → a delivered-duty-paid price adds import clearance, duty, and inland delivery. Every step you add is a cost the buyer would otherwise have paid separately.

| Term | Included in the quoted number | Still the buyer's cost |
|---|---|---|
| EXW | Goods packed at the factory | Inland haulage, export clearance, main carriage, duty, delivery |
| FOB | Goods loaded on the vessel at origin | Main carriage, insurance, duty, destination delivery |
| CIF | Main carriage and marine insurance to destination port | Duty, port charges, inland delivery, unloading |
| DDP | Everything to the named destination, duty paid | Unloading at destination, and any tax the buyer must self-account |

The practical move is dull and effective: convert every quote to the same term before comparing, using the freight and duty estimates your forwarder gives you. A quote that cannot be converted because the supplier will not name the place is not a quote you can rank.

## Where Quotes Break Down

**Direct answer:** Most pricing disputes are not disagreements about money; they are specifications that were never pinned down, surfacing later as a change order.

The first failure is an ambiguous brief. Ask for "agarwood sticks, medium box" and the supplier will price the cheapest reading of that sentence, because that is the reading that wins the inquiry. The gap appears at the sample stage, and by then the number you were comparing has no meaning.

The second is treating a sample as a miniature bulk order. A sample run carries the full setup across a handful of units, so its per-unit cost is high by construction. Reading that figure as a preview of bulk pricing leads buyers to reject suppliers who are in fact competitive at volume. The sample price measures the setup block, not the production cost.

The third is the late exclusion. Tooling, plate charges, artwork separation, testing, palletization, and destination charges are all real costs that can sit outside the quoted line. A quote that lists them is longer and more trustworthy than one that does not.

The fourth is expiry. A number quoted against last season's material market is a number the supplier may not honour, and renegotiating after the PO is a weaker position than asking for validity up front.

The fifth is currency and payment structure. A price quoted in one currency and settled in another carries a conversion exposure that neither party controls, and a deposit-heavy schedule shifts financing cost onto the buyer. Both change the effective price without changing the quoted one.

## Briefing a Supplier So the Number Is Real

**Direct answer:** Supply five things — product, quantity, packaging, destination, and any hard specification — and the reply becomes a quote rather than an opening bid.

1. **Product** — format, material family, aroma direction, or a reference product the supplier can inspect.
2. **Quantity** — a target figure plus the band you would consider, so price breaks can be offered.
3. **Packaging** — bulk, retail-ready, or gift, with the artwork responsibility stated.
4. **Destination** — country and named place, which fixes freight and duty treatment.
5. **Specification limits** — natural versus fragrance-oil content, burn behaviour expectations, and any certification the market requires.

The [quote request](/request-a-quote/) page carries the full field list. A brief that answers those five questions in three sentences will out-perform a page of prose every time.

## The Author's Position

**Position:** "What is your price?" is the weakest opening in this trade, and a buyer who leads with it advertises that they have not yet decided what they are buying.

**Reasoning:**
- Price is an output. Asked without quantity, material, and destination, it produces a figure with nothing behind it, and the supplier learns only that the buyer is early.
- A buyer who asks "what drives this number?" gets something durable: a map of where the money goes, and therefore where the negotiation can move.

**Boundary:** "On request" does not mean a supplier is hiding something. It means the number genuinely depends on inputs the buyer has not supplied. The distinction matters because the opposite behaviour exists and is easy to miss — a supplier who will not explain *which* variables move the cost even after a complete brief. That refusal is the signal, not the absence of a printed list.

## FAQ

### Q: Why will no incense supplier send me a price list?
Because the unit cost is attached to a configuration, and the configuration is usually built for the order. A list would be accurate for one material grade, one packaging format, one run length, and one delivery term. Send a brief instead of a request for a list, and the reply becomes specific. Suppliers who do send a list are usually quoting a stock product at a single volume, which may not be the product you actually need.

### Q: What MOQ should I expect for incense?
There is no industry-wide figure, and any page that prints one is describing a single product line. What sets the floor is the ratio of setup cost to unit value: a stock stick in a plain carton can be made in short runs because very little has to be prepared, while a custom scent in a printed box needs artwork, a fragrance trial, and a packaging run before the first saleable unit exists. Ask what the minimum is *for the product you described*, and ask separately whether a per-order minimum applies on top.

### Q: How do I compare two quotes that look completely different?
Normalize first: convert both to the same delivery term at the same named place, confirm both describe the same material grade and the same packaging, check that lead times are measured from the same starting event, and confirm both numbers are still valid. Only then compare unit prices. A cheap quote that excludes freight, tooling, or artwork is not a cheap quote; it is a partial one, and the missing parts will be invoiced later.

### Q: Why is my sample so much more expensive per unit than the bulk price?
Because the sample carries the whole setup block across a handful of units. Nothing about the material changed; the denominator did. Read the sample price as a measure of preparation cost, not as a preview of production cost. Ask whether the sample charge is credited against the bulk order, which is a common arrangement and turns the sample into a deposit rather than a cost.

### Q: Is "price on request" a warning sign?
Not by itself. In a trade where material markets move and every order is configured, a supplier cannot honestly name a figure before hearing the brief. The warning sign is different: a supplier who receives a complete brief and still will not explain which variables drive the cost, or who revises the number upward after the sample is approved without being able to say what changed.

### Q: Can I negotiate price breaks at higher quantities?
Ask for the same specification priced at two or three quantities. The shape of the answer tells you whether the fixed block or the variable cost dominates, and therefore whether volume is the right lever at all. Some suppliers price in steps tied to how they purchase material, so a larger order can land on a better step rather than sliding smoothly downward. The price-break ladder is more informative than a single discount request.

## Summary

- Incense is priced per inquiry because cost follows configuration, and configuration is set by the buyer.

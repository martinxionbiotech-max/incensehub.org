**Direct answer:** Buying incense stock in bulk means ordering plant material by weight — heartwood, gum-resin, leaf, peel, seed — instead of by the packet, and holding the supplier to a written brief on every delivery after the first. The stock is the simple part. The difficult part is proving that sack ten still matches sack one.

**Key facts:** What follows concerns the raw stock itself — the layer under finished sticks and cones — rather than the making of the stick. It quotes no prices, minimums, or capacity figures, because those are set per supplier and confirmed on inquiry. Where a number genuinely depends on the material and the recipe — grind, water content — the page says so instead of inventing one.

## Key Takeaways

- The second delivery is the purchase; the first only shows a bench where swatches are cut.
- Form decides what you inherit: order powder and the vendor's mill setting travels with it.
- Grind and water content are numbers to agree, not truths to discover.
- Ask how a lot is checked, what against, and what happens when one fails. Adjectives are not answers.
- A swatch proves a material. A trial run proves a vendor.

## The Second Delivery Is the Purchase

**Direct answer:** A retail buyer judges a finished stick by burning it. A bulk buyer commits weeks before anything burns, so the object of the deal changes: you are no longer buying material, you are buying the supplier's ability to send the same material again.

That shift creates the failure mode that costs the most. Nobody ships a deliberately wrong sack. What happens is quieter: a crop runs short, a second grower is added to fill the order, a mill is re-set, and the powder arrives a shade darker and a fraction coarser. No single delivery looks wrong. The product does.

| | Buying a box | Buying a tonne |
|---|---|---|
| What you are buying | A finished object | A repeatable input |
| When you find out | On receipt | At batch ten |
| Usual failure | One bad item | Slow drift across lots |
| Your remedy afterwards | Return it | Rework, write off, reformulate |
| What protects you | The seller's name | The written brief |

Reputation works as a proxy while the stakes stay small. At volume it turns into a story about the past. What replaces it is a document plus a jar: a written description of what the plant should be, and a sealed portion of the parcel you approved, kept where you can reach it. Everything else — the visit, the photographs, the certificates — is commentary on those two objects.

## Form Comes First, Because Form Is Process

**Direct answer:** Chips, whole pieces, powder, and resin are four materials wearing one name. The first decision is which one your line can consume without adding a step it cannot run.

| Form | What your line does next | What you gain | What you inherit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole pieces | Saw or mill in-house | The grind stays yours | A mill, its labour, its errors |
| Chips | Warm, resell, or grind | Easy handling and dosing | Somebody else's sizing |
| Powder | Blend straight into sticks or cones | No grinding at all | Their screen, their water figure |
| Resin | Melt, blend, or burn direct | Ready to use | Purity, foreign matter |
| Herbs, flowers, seed | Mill or blend into mixes | Whole-plant character | Drying quality, mould risk |

One wood sold as chip and as powder is a single plant cut two ways, not a finer and a coarser class. Powder is not one thing either: a wide cut and a narrow one behave differently inside the same binder, which is why the mill setting belongs on the sheet as a figure rather than on the carton as a word. The processing side is covered in the [how incense powder is made](/blog/how-incense-powder-is-made/) walkthrough.

The rule underneath is about ownership of the awkward step. If the mill is already on your floor, with somebody who knows how to set the gap, buy whole stock and keep the grinding. If it is not, buy meal and accept that the screen now belongs to the other side — including whatever it does on a humid Tuesday.

## Grind and Water: Two Dials, No Universal Setting

**Direct answer:** The two physical variables a maker actually controls are the cut of the powder and the water left in it. Neither has a correct value in the abstract; the correct value is what your recipe and your press can hold.

Walk the grind first. A sieve stack sorts powder into fractions, and the fraction you buy is a range rather than a point. Coarse grains leave gaps and draw air unevenly; fine dust fills the gaps, can choke the burn, and demands more binder to hold together. A recipe tuned on one fraction will not simply tolerate the other, which is why "what mesh?" is the wrong question and "what cut do you hold for this application?" is the right one.

Water works from the other side. Too much and the powder clumps in the fist, smoulders instead of burning cleanly, and feeds mould in a warm room. Too little and it crumbles at the press and burns hot and fast, throwing the aromatic fraction off before the session settles. The usable window is narrow and it belongs to your recipe, your climate, and how long the sack will sit.

Loss on drying is the test that makes the argument concrete. Weigh a sample, dry it to a standard end point, weigh it again. The difference, read against the original weight, is the water the material was carrying. Two sacks that look alike can carry different amounts, which means the same invoice weight delivers different amounts of botanical.

State the target for your line, then require it to be held. That is a conversation about control, and it is the only version worth having.

## The Walk-Through: What to Look At

**Direct answer:** A supplier visit is a set of questions about how incoming plant material is checked, where it waits, and how one parcel is kept like the next. Whatever reaches you carries the whole journey behind it.

| Ask | Why it shows up in your product | Answer that should worry you |
|---|---|---|
| Who confirms identity when a parcel lands, and how? | Species and part decide both aroma and legality | "Our grower is reliable" |
| Where does the stock wait before dispatch? | Plant tissue takes up water and gives off aroma | "In the warehouse" |
| How is one parcel kept like the last? | Cut and water figure are what your press feels | "We always use the same source" |
| What keeps mould, insects, and non-aromatic wood out? | Cheap consignments betray themselves here | "We keep an eye on it" |
| Which sheet exists for this particular parcel? | Traceability is per parcel or it is theatre | "A certificate can be arranged" |

Two probes cut through rehearsed answers. Ask to see the reject log: a facility that has never turned anything away is either lucky or not measuring. Ask for a photograph of the storage room taken on an ordinary day rather than during a visit — sacks standing on a damp floor say more than any certificate.

The [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) supplies the reference side of the identity question. The [storage and longevity guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/) explains why the room matters as much as the plant: water and aroma loss resurface later as a stick that will not stay lit. The four-layer view — identity, capability, checks, paperwork — is in the [factory vetting guide](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/).

## The Swatch and the Trial

**Direct answer:** A swatch tells you what a material is. A trial run tells you whether the supplier can send it again. Confusing the two is how buyers end up committed to a process they have never seen.

| Stage | What you do | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| Brief | Write material, form, trade name, physical target | That you know what you want |
| Terms | Settle what ships, whether it is charged, how it travels | Nothing about quality, plenty about process |
| Cold check | Look, feel, smell in the bag | Identity and obvious defects |
| Heated check | Warm a pinch, smell what comes off | What the burn will release |
| Trial run | Push a production quantity through your own line | Whether any of it repeats |

The heated check is the one buyers skip, and it flips decisions. A powder that smells rich at room temperature can turn thin and sharp once warmed, because the heavy notes that gave it depth never leave at burning heat. The reverse happens too: a flat cold material can open into something resinous and alive. Warm it before you judge it.

What no swatch can show is the shape of the supplier's process. Repetition depends on how they sample, what they measure against, and what they do with a failed lot — none of which fits in a courier envelope. The [samples program](/blog/samples-program/) material draws the line between evidence about a material and evidence about a system.

## Where Fakery Pays

**Direct answer:** Risk is not spread evenly. The material worth the most checking is the one where a near-match is easy to source and the money gap between real and near-real is widest.

| Plant | Risk | Why somebody would bother | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agarwood (*Aquilaria*) | Very high | Wide spread, lookalike heartwoods, resin grading by eye | Highest |
| Sandalwood (*Santalum*) | High | Species mix-ups, tight supply, similar scent | Highest |
| Gum-resins: frankincense, myrrh | Moderate to high | Copal-type substitutes, blends | High |
| Flowers and leaves | Moderate | Lookalike species, stem content, poor drying | Moderate |
| Everyday wood flours | Low | Plentiful, little margin in cheating | Lower |

The table maps incentive, not quality. Agarwood sits on top because a convincing substitute can cost a fraction of a genuine piece and photograph identically. Everyday wood flour sits at the bottom because nobody gains much by adulterating something already cheap. A team applying one light check everywhere over-checks the cheap rows and under-checks the expensive ones.

Grade letters need their own warning. One house's "A" is another house's "B", because the letters are house conventions rather than calibrated classes. Anchor them to a retained swatch and a written defect tolerance, or they mean nothing once two quotations sit side by side. *This ranking is an editorial judgment drawn from trade patterns, not a published standard.*

## Drift, and How to See It

**Direct answer:** Consistency shows up across deliveries, never inside one. The signal is drift: the same name arriving as a slightly different material, cut, or water content.

| Signal | What it looks like | What it usually means |
|---|---|---|
| Colour | A shade darker or lighter | Different part, age, or drying |
| Aroma on heat | Flatter, sharper, more resinous | Different species or origin |
| Feel | Grittier or silkier between fingers | Screen changed, mill re-set |
| Weight | Same volume weighs differently | Water content moved |
| Burn | Slower, faster, more ash | Binder meeting a changed powder |

A sealed portion of the approved lot is what turns those impressions into evidence. Label it with the date and the supplier, keep it closed, and open it beside each new delivery. The comparison catches what memory cannot: a shade, a fraction, a note that used to sit forward and now sits back.

The chain runs one way: a fixed reference → a comparison at each receipt → an early warning → a choice between re-specifying, re-blending, or rejecting. Remove the reference and the same drift is discovered in the finished product, where it costs the most.

## Price Comes Last

**Direct answer:** A price quoted before the brief exists is a number attached to nothing. Species, part, grade, form, water content, and volume all move it, and none of them is fixed until the brief is written.

An unpinned name lets each house quote a different object. One prices a coarse powder from a mixed source, another a fine powder from one origin, and both call it the same thing. The lower number is not a saving; it is a cheaper object wearing the same label. That is the whole of the cheapest-quote fallacy.

The wider picture is bigger than the invoice line. Freight is paid on weight, and part of that weight can be water. Rework appears when a sack will not run. A mouldy delivery becomes a write-off, and the write-off is freight plus material plus the production slot it disrupted. None of it compares across houses until the brief fixes what is shipping.

Brief first, swatch second, price third, terms fourth. Lead with "how much per kilo" and you have told the other side the brief is negotiable.

## The Five-Answer Scorecard

**Direct answer:** Most of what matters can be read from five short answers, written down so the decision can be defended later.

| Question | Strong answer | Weak answer | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|---|
| How is a batch sampled? | Method, positions, size, reduction | "We check it" | Procedure or gesture |
| What is it tested against? | A shared sheet with limits | "Good quality" | A standard outside the sales call |
| What happens on a failure? | Quarantine, rework, rejection | "It never fails" | Whether the system has run |
| Can you produce lot paper? | Species, origin, lot results | A generic certificate | Traceability per lot or per brochure |
| Who else buys this material? | Named buyers in similar work | "Everyone" | Whether your use case is understood |

Five answers tell you more than a factory visit you were not equipped to read. The scorecard does not replace a trial run; it decides who deserves one.

## Resins Between Harvest and Use

**Direct answer:** Gum-resins are the least stable thing on the shelf. Heat softens them, air oxidises them, and every surface they touch holds a smell.

Frankincense and myrrh arrive as tears — irregular lumps, pale to amber, dusty outside. Warm weather softens them enough that a sack left in a loading bay fuses into one mass by evening. Cold makes them brittle enough to shatter into dust during handling. Neither is contamination, but both change how the material doses, and a fused block is far harder to sample evenly than loose tears.

The aromatic side matters more. Resin takes odour from its surroundings, so a warehouse that also holds solvents or paint produces material that smells of the warehouse rather than the tree. Sealed, cool, dark storage is the simple rule; the hard part is checking that it was followed, because a reopened sack has no memory of where it sat.

Sorting has its own conventions. Tears get graded by size, colour, and how much bark is attached, and those grades are visual habits rather than calibrated classes. Two suppliers can call the same lot "first grade" and mean different things. Ask for a photograph of the actual consignment beside a ruler, then compare it with the retained swatch when it lands.

## Reading a Lab Report Without a Chemistry Degree

**Direct answer:** A lab report is a list of measurements with limits attached. The buyer's job is to check that the limits match the ones agreed, not to interpret the chemistry from scratch.

Most reports on botanical material carry a small set of figures. Water content, or loss on drying, says how much of the delivery is water. Total ash says how much mineral residue is left after burning, which rises when soil or sand has not been cleaned out. Acid-insoluble ash narrows that toward silica rather than the plant's own minerals. Volatile oil content says how much aromatic material the sample yields under distillation, and it is the closest thing to a direct measure of fragrance potential.

The trap is reading a figure without its method. Two laboratories can report "volatile oil" with different extraction times and produce numbers that cannot be compared. The same holds for ash. So the question is not "is this number good" but "which method produced it, and is it the method written into the agreement".

A second trap is sampling. A lab can only analyse the portion it received. If that portion came off the top of a sack, the report describes the top of a sack. The sampling plan and the lab request belong together, and a report without a lot number is difficult to attach to anything.

Four sheets usually sit behind those figures: a statement of species and origin, a wild-versus-cultivated declaration where it applies, the physical record for the parcel, and any permit the plant legally needs. The species line should name the plant, the part, and the district in words that can be checked against a botanical reference rather than against the invoice. Permits cover CITES-listed plants such as agarwood, and a permit belongs to a shipment rather than to a brochure. Borders add one more layer, where customs classification and phytosanitary rules decide whether a legal plant arrives legally; the [incense export considerations](/blog/incense-export-considerations/) page covers that boundary.

Read it as three columns: what was measured, what the agreed limit was, what this lot returned. Anything failing the middle column is a conversation. Anything missing from the first column is a gap in the agreement rather than a clean result.

## The Inbound Box

**Direct answer:** Look at the packaging before you look at the material. A torn liner, a swollen bag, or condensation under the wrap describes the journey more honestly than any certificate.

Botanical freight moves through temperature changes, and temperature moves water. A pallet loaded cold and unloaded into a warm warehouse draws condensation onto the inside of the liner. The material nearest that surface takes the water first, which is why the top of a sack can behave differently from the rest of the same lot. The powder is not wrong; it has been rained on in a closed box.

- **Smell the outside of the bag before opening it.** Odour transfer is real, and a sack stored beside solvent carries that smell into the powder.
- **Look for staining, swelling, or a liner that has pulled away.** Those mark where water sat.
- **Feel the top, the middle, and the seam.** Differences describe the journey.
- **Photograph the pallet, label, and lot number before breaking the seal.** A dispute is easier to settle with an image taken on arrival.

Inspect before signing. Once a delivery is accepted, an argument about which stage caused a water problem becomes a negotiation about goodwill rather than a check against a document.

## The Crop Calendar

**Direct answer:** Botanicals are harvested in windows, so availability is a calendar question before it is a commercial one. Anything quoted as "in stock" off-season has usually been sitting somewhere since the last harvest.

A plant can only be collected in its season → material enters the trade in a burst → stocks draw down over the following months → what remains late in the cycle is older, drier, and handled more times than the fresh material was. None of that makes it unusable. It makes it a different input from the one you sampled in season.

The questions change with the calendar. In season, ask about freshness and drying. Out of season, ask about storage history: where it sat, how many times it changed hands, and whether a retained sample from the harvest still exists to compare against. A supplier who can answer the second set is describing a warehouse rather than a crop.

## Borneol, Camphor, and the Cooling Line

**Direct answer:** Two crystalline materials sit at the cool end of the Chinese palette, and both are bought in small quantities where purity matters more than volume.

Borneol — 龙脑 (lóngnǎo), also called 冰片 (bīngpiàn) — is a crystalline alcohol with a sharp, camphoraceous, slightly sweet smell. It occurs in several plants and is also produced synthetically, and the trade distinguishes natural from synthetic material because the two behave differently in a blend. Camphor comes mainly from *Cinnamomum camphora* and is more aggressive: a little lifts a blend, a little too much dominates everything around it.

Both are volatile solids, which changes how they are stored and handled. They sublime slowly at room temperature, so a container left open loses weight and strength; sealed glass or a tight double bag is the usual answer. Both also carry a strong smell that migrates, and a sack of powder stored beside them will pick it up.

The buying question is purity rather than grade. Ask what the material is, whether it is natural or synthetic where that distinction is sold, and how it was packed. A small consignment of the wrong crystal can ruin a large batch of something else, which is why these belong in sealed, labelled containers away from the main stock.

## Herbal Lines: Mugwort and Its Neighbours

**Direct answer:** Herbal ingredients bring the least predictable part of a botanical order, because leaf, stem, and flower dry at different rates and carry different amounts of dust.

Mugwort — 艾草 (àicǎo) — is the familiar example: the leaf is the useful part, the stem is woody filler, and the ratio between them changes the smell and the burn. Material harvested late carries more stem and more seed head; material dried too fast goes brittle and dusty; material dried too slowly can mould in the middle of a bundle before anyone notices.

What to check on arrival is mostly visual and tactile. Leaf should look whole rather than powdered unless powder was ordered. Colour should be even, without dark patches that suggest damp. The smell should be green and herbal rather than musty, and a handful squeezed should feel dry rather than cool and clammy.

Herbal lines also carry the highest risk of confusion between species, because many leaves look alike once dried. Ask for the botanical name, keep a labelled reference portion, and treat a change in supplier as a change in material until proven otherwise.

## Name the Part, Not Just the Plant

**Direct answer:** Most of the difference between a good parcel and a disappointing one is not the species but the part, and the part is the thing a quotation most often leaves out.

Heartwood, sapwood, bark, leaf, stem, root, flower, seed — each carries a different load of oil, a different drying behaviour, and a different price. Sandalwood is the clearest case: the pale outer wood holds little of the scent that makes the material worth buying, and a parcel that mixes heart and sap reads weaker than its grade suggests. Agarwood has the same logic in reverse, where the value sits in the resinous portion and the pale wood is closer to packaging than product.

Part confusion is easy to miss because the trade name stays the same. "Sandalwood chips" can mean heartwood chips, sapwood chips, or a blend, and no photograph taken from arm's length will show you which. Ask for the part in words, then confirm it against the physical evidence: colour, density, and the smell released when a corner is warmed.

The specification line that prevents most of this is short: plant, part, district, form, cut, water. Six fields, and the last four are the ones buyers skip.

## A Short Glossary for Chinese Supply

**Direct answer:** Chinese suppliers quote in trade names that do not map cleanly onto English ones, and a buyer who knows 沉香 from 檀香 is harder to mislead.

沉香 (chénxiāng) is agarwood, the resinous heartwood of *Aquilaria* species. 檀香 (tánxiāng) is sandalwood. 乳香 (rǔxiāng) is frankincense resin. 陈皮 (chénpí) is aged mandarin peel. Form words matter as much as material words: 块 (kuài) means pieces, 片 (piàn) means slices or chips, 粉 (fěn) means powder. A quotation that says only "sandalwood" leaves species, part, and form open, and the Chinese trade name usually carries more of that than the English translation does.

| Trade name | Botanical name | Part bought |
|---|---|---|
| Agarwood, 沉香 | *Aquilaria malaccensis*, *A. sinensis* | Resinous heartwood |
| Sandalwood, 檀香 | *Santalum album*, *S. spicatum* | Heartwood |
| Frankincense, 乳香 | *Boswellia sacra*, *B. carteri* | Gum-resin tear |
| Myrrh, 没药 | *Commiphora myrrha* | Gum-resin tear |
| Camphor, 樟脑 | *Cinnamomum camphora* | Wood, crystallised |
| Borneol, 冰片 | *Blumea balsamifera*, *Dryobalanops aromatica* | Crystal |
| Mugwort, 艾草 | *Artemisia argyi* | Leaf |
| Mandarin peel, 陈皮 | *Citrus reticulata* | Dried rind |
| Copal | *Bursera* species | Gum-resin |
| Guggul | *Commiphora wightii* | Gum-resin |
| Spikenard | *Nardostachys jatamansi* | Root and rhizome |
| Patchouli | *Pogostemon cablin* | Leaf |
| Vetiver | *Chrysopogon zizanioides* | Root |
| Clove | *Syzygium aromaticum* | Flower bud |

The practical use is narrow and real: keep the Chinese term beside the English one when a brief is translated, so a later dispute points at the same word on both sheets.

## Agarwood: What Grading Actually Measures

**Direct answer:** Agarwood grades are usually a shorthand for resin, and resin is what makes the wood aromatic. The trade vocabulary around it is old and inconsistent, so the numbers and letters need translating before they mean anything.

The material is the heartwood of *Aquilaria* trees, formed when the tree responds to injury by producing a dark, resinous deposit in the wood. Wood with more of that deposit is denser, darker, and heavier, and it smells stronger when warmed. The traditional "sinking" description refers to pieces dense enough to sink in water, which is a rough proxy for resin load rather than a laboratory measurement. Treat it as traditional belief about quality, not as a calibrated class.

What a buyer can check: the colour of the resin lines against the pale wood, the weight of a piece in the hand, the smell when a corner is warmed, and whether the piece is heartwood or a slice that includes pale sapwood. Photographs help only when they include a scale and a plain background. A vendor who cannot say which species, which region, and whether the wood is wild or plantation is describing an idea rather than a lot.

Species naming matters especially here because the genus spans several traded plants — *Aquilaria malaccensis*, *A. sinensis*, *A. crassna*, *A. subintegra* — and protection status differs between them. Wild collection is restricted under CITES for the genus, which is why plantation material has become common in trade and why a permit question is a sourcing question rather than a formality. A vendor who cannot say whether the wood is plantation or wild, and which species it is, cannot say whether the parcel can legally move at all.

## Sandalwood: Species Before Grade

**Direct answer:** Sandalwood's scent differs by species, and the species name matters more than the grade letter printed beside it.

*Santalum album* is the species most associated with the creamy, sweet heartwood scent that defines the material in perfumery. Other *Santalum* species carry their own profiles — some drier, some greener, some closer to cedar — and they are sold under the same English word. Heartwood versus sapwood is the second split: the pale outer wood carries little oil, and a sack that mixes the two will smell weaker than its grade suggests.

For a buyer, the practical sequence is species, then part, then grade. Ask for the botanical name, ask whether the material is heartwood, and only then discuss the letter. A retained sample warmed on a plate will tell you more about the oil content than any description, because the difference between a rich heartwood and a pale mix is obvious the moment heat reaches it.

## Keeping Water Out of the Warehouse

**Direct answer:** Most moisture problems are solved before they start, by keeping material off the floor, out of the sun, and inside a sealed liner that is opened as little as possible.

A working method is unglamorous. Pallets keep sacks off concrete, which wicks water upward. A liner keeps the material from breathing the room's air. A hygrometer on the wall turns "it feels damp in here" into a reading somebody can act on. Rotation matters as much as any of it: the sack at the back of the stack is the one that sits through a humid season before anyone opens it.

The rule of first-in, first-out is easy to write and hard to follow when a new delivery arrives and the old one is still sealed. Label arrival dates on the outside of every sack, not just on the paperwork, and the rotation stops depending on memory.

## Packing for the Journey

**Direct answer:** Packaging is the part of the specification the material travels inside, and a weak pack can undo a good lot before it reaches your door.

Three layers do the work: an inner liner that blocks water vapour, a outer sack or carton that resists tearing and compression, and a pallet configuration that keeps weight off the bottom layer. A liner that is sealed but pierced is worse than one that is open, because the damage is invisible until the material is opened. Compression matters for fragile material such as dried flowers, which crumble into dust under a stacked pallet.

Ask how the material will be packed before the order ships, and specify the liner. It is a cheap line in the agreement and an expensive omission in practice.

## Two Quotations, Side by Side

**Direct answer:** Two replies that both say "sandalwood powder" can describe different products, and the only way to see that is to lay the answers beside the questions you asked.

Picture the first reply. It gives a price per kilo, a photograph of pale dust, and a delivery window. Nothing about species, nothing about which part of the tree, nothing about the screen or the water. It is not a dishonest reply. It is an incomplete one, and it leaves every variable that decides how the powder behaves open to whoever loads the sack.

Now the second. It names *Santalum album*, states heartwood rather than a mix, gives a screen range and a water figure, says how the material is packed, and offers a retained portion of the lot it will ship. The price is higher. The product is also defined, which means it can be compared with anything else that has been defined the same way.

The first quote is cheaper in the only sense a spreadsheet understands. It is not cheaper in the sense that matters, because the buyer has no way to know what will arrive, and no grounds to complain when it does. A quote without a brief is a bid to supply something, not a bid to supply your something.

The practical habit: never compare two numbers until the surrounding answers match. Where they differ, the difference is the product.

## When to Walk Away

**Direct answer:** Most bad suppliers announce themselves early, in the small evasions rather than the large claims.

A short list of signals worth acting on:

- **No lot number on the paperwork.** Traceability that stops at the invoice is not traceability.
- **Refusal to keep a retained portion.** A vendor confident in their process has no reason to object.
- **A species name that changes between messages.** The material is not settled if the name is not.
- **"Certificate available" with no issuer.** A document nobody can trace is decoration.
- **Pressure to commit before a trial.** Urgency is a sales technique, not a supply argument.
- **Prices that move without a spec change.** Either the material moved or the quote was never firm.

None of these proves bad faith. Each one removes a way for you to check, and a vendor who removes your checks is asking to be trusted instead of verified. Trust is what verification produces over time; it is not what replaces it.

## Traditional Language Is Not a Specification

**Direct answer:** Many plants carry traditional materia-medica descriptions — warming, calming, moving qi — and those record traditional belief rather than modern evidence. Sourcing rests on identity and sameness, never on the vocabulary.

The words are old and precise inside their own frame: agarwood, sandalwood, and various herbs carry grading terms older than modern commerce, and that history explains why a plant is prized. It names no species, no part, no water figure, no cut. Accept "warming" in place of those and the order rests on a story. No health claim appears on this page, and none belongs in a supplier's literature.

## The Author's Take

**Position (editorial):** The most expensive mistake in bulk botanicals is buying a name, and the cheapest insurance is a written brief plus a jar of the approved material kept on a shelf.

**Reasoning:** A plant is not a chemical reagent. The same trade name covers different species, different parts of the plant, different grades, and different amounts of water, and none of that is a fault — it is what a grown material is. Write down the form, the cut, the water band, and what "the same" is supposed to mean, and a vague enquiry turns into a procurement. Write nothing, and the other side fills the gaps, usually toward whatever is cheapest to load that week.

**Boundary:** A brief is not a promise. It is a claim somebody can test, which is the more useful thing to own. A vendor can tick every line and still send a parcel you dislike, because aroma never fits cleanly into figures. That is why the jar sits next to the sheet rather than under it: one holds what can be written, the other holds what cannot.

## FAQ

### Q: Where do I start when sourcing ingredients in volume?
On paper, before any call. Put down four things: the plant and the part, the form your line can process, the physical target for a powder, and the quantity one run consumes. That sheet is the brief, and it is the only thing a supplier cannot argue with later. Minimums and prices are confirmed per inquiry and belong at the end of the sheet.

### Q: How fine should incense powder be ground?
Fine enough for the recipe in front of you, which is a different answer in every workshop. The screen opening has to suit the binder, the way the mix wets out, and how the stick or cone is formed. Ask what opening the supplier runs for the use you described, then hold the next parcel against that answer. If the milling happens on your own floor, write the setting on the wall and leave it there.

### Q: How do I stop a supplier's second delivery from drifting?
Keep a jar on the shelf. Seal a portion of the parcel you approved, write the date on the lid, and leave it where your hand will find it. When the next delivery lands, open both and look at them together: shade, grit, smell at rest, smell warmed. A change too small to notice alone becomes obvious beside a fixed point.

### Q: Chips or powder?
Whichever leaves the grinding where you want the responsibility. A workshop with a mill and someone who can set it should buy chips or whole pieces. A workshop without one should buy powder, then treat the supplier's screen and water figure as contract lines rather than incidental details.

### Q: Is a certificate of analysis the same as a specification?
No. One is a rule, the other is a measurement. You write the rule — what the material must be, which tests matter, where the limits sit. A laboratory reports what a particular lot measured. Read the two together, match the lot number on the report to the one on the delivery, and check who signed it. If the issuing laboratory cannot be identified, the document is decoration.

## Summary

One question, asked over and over: can this vendor send the same material again? Form fixes what you inherit. Three answers decide whether a process exists behind the price. Grind and water are numbers written on a sheet, not universal truths. A swatch settles a question about a material; a trial run settles one about a business. Write the brief first and leave price until last — not from caution, but because that is the only order in which the number means anything.

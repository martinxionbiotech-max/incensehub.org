**Direct answer:** A stick and a candle scent a room through opposite fuels and opposite fires — the stick burns plant material down to an ember, the candle burns wax under an open flame. The honest difference is ember versus flame, and material smoke versus wax soot.

**Key facts:** Both release airborne particles into the room, so neither qualifies as "clean air." The real question is the experience: incense gives a material, often ritual scent; a candle gives ambient light plus scent.

---

## What Actually Burns in Each

**Direct answer:** The fuel is the first fork in the road — plant matter on one side, wax on the other — and the fuel dictates everything downstream, from the byproduct to the fire risk.

An incense stick is ground wood, resin, and botanicals held together with a binder. When the ember reaches the paste, the plant material chars → volatile aromatics lift off as smoke → fine particles and ash are left behind. A candle is wax (a fuel) carrying fragrance oil; the flame melts a small pool of wax → the liquid wax wicks upward and vaporizes → the fragrance rides out with the heat, and the wax leaves soot rather than plant smoke. Different fuels, different fires, different residue.

| | Incense stick | Candle |
|---|---|---|
| What burns | Plant material + binder | Wax + fragrance |
| Combustion | Ember at the tip | Open flame at the wick |
| Airborne output | Smoke + fine particles | Soot + particles |
| Duration | ~30–60 min (varies) | Hours |
| Extra | Ash to catch | Melted wax, flame to watch |

---

## Two Combustion Loops, Two Clocks

**Direct answer:** A stick burns itself out on a fixed clock because its fuel is a thin, fixed length; a candle sustains itself on a loop that keeps feeding wax to the flame, which is why one ends in an hour and the other can run all evening.

The mechanism chains run in opposite directions. On a stick, the ember marches down a fixed rod → each millimeter of paste is consumed exactly once → the burn ends when the rod is spent, with no way to refuel. On a candle, the flame melts a pool → the wick draws the liquid up by capillary action → the vapor feeds the flame → the flame keeps melting more wax → the loop repeats until the wax is gone. The stick is a one-way trip; the candle is a self-sustaining cycle. That difference — fixed path versus recirculating loop — is why "how long does it last" means a range for one and a reservoir for the other, and it is the physical reason the two products cannot be compared minute-for-minute as if they were the same thing.

## What Each Leaves Behind

**Direct answer:** The residue is the tell — a stick leaves ash, a candle leaves soot and an empty jar — and reading the residue is the fastest way to see which combustion you actually ran.

The two fires do not even burn the same way at the molecular level. A stick's plant material undergoes incomplete combustion in the open air → the carbon that does not fully burn stays behind as ash, and the half-burned volatiles escape as visible smoke. A candle's wax is a clean-burning hydrocarbon → most of it converts to carbon dioxide and water vapor → the small fraction that burns imperfectly condenses as soot, which is why candle soot is usually faint and sometimes invisible. This is not a "cleaner" claim — both still release fine particles — but it explains why the stick leaves a visible gray mound while the candle leaves a smudge on the glass. For a buyer, the residue is a clue to the fuel, not a verdict on safety.

## Fire Risk, Compared Honestly

**Direct answer:** A candle's open flame is the more obvious fire risk; a stick's ember is the subtler one — and the setup that contains each differs even though the underlying rules match.

An open flame can ignite anything it touches if the candle tips or sits near curtains or paper, so a candle needs a stable holder that contains the flame and catches dripping wax. An ember is not a flame, but it is still hot enough to scorch or ignite if a stick falls into flammable material, so a stick needs a holder that keeps the ember above a heatproof surface and catches the ash. Neither is categorically safer — both demand the same two habits: a heatproof, stable surface clear of flammables, and no unattended burning. The full precautions are in the [safety guide](/blog/incense-safety-guide/).

## A Decision Table: What Each Is Actually For

**Direct answer:** The two are not rival answers to the same want — they solve different jobs, and the table below is the fastest way to see which job is yours.

| What you want | Which fits | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A short, deliberate material moment | Incense stick | Fixed ~30–60 min, ends on its own |
| Light plus scent for an evening | Candle | The flame is half the point |
| A ritual with a beginning and an end | Incense stick | The burn is the clock |
| A background glow that keeps going | Candle | Hours from one reservoir |
| Minimal visible smoke | Candle (faint soot) | Soot is usually less visible than incense smoke |
| A strong, immediate scent throw | Incense stick | Smoke carries aroma fast |

The point of the table is to expose the category error: "incense vs candle" is not "which is better," it is "do you want a session or an evening, an ember or a flame." Once you answer that, the product picks itself.

## When Each Makes Sense

**Direct answer:** Pick the stick when you want a material, ritual scent and are fine with ash; pick the candle when you want light and scent together, or a slow, decorative evening.

They are different products for different moments, not two ways of doing the same thing. A stick is a single timed session — light it, and in roughly thirty to sixty minutes it is gone. A candle is a reservoir that runs for hours across many uses, and it adds a living flame that a stick never offers. If you want one object to carry a whole evening with light, choose the candle; if you want a short, deliberate, material moment, choose the stick.

## A Misconception: "Candles Are Smoke-Free"

**Direct answer:** A candle is not smoke-free — combustion always puts particles into the air, even when the flame looks clean and the output is nearly invisible.

This is the error that makes candle-versus-stick look lopsided. Incense smoke is visible, so it gets blamed; candle soot is usually faint, so it gets a pass. But a flickering, smoking wick can produce more soot than a well-made stick in the same span, and both are combustion sources that should be burned in a ventilated room. The judgment boundary is this: "less visible" does not mean "absent." The detail is in [incense ventilation & indoor air](/blog/incense-ventilation-indoor-air/).

## A Second Misconception: "The Ember Is Safer Than the Flame"

**Direct answer:** The ember feels safer because it is not a flame, but a glowing tip dropped onto paper or fabric ignites just as surely — the difference is not danger level, it is the kind of setup that fails.

People rank the ember below the flame on instinct, because an ember does not look like fire. That instinct is misleading. A candle fails when it tips or a draft reaches the flame; a stick fails when the holder is too small, the ash tray is missing, or the burning end falls against something. Both failures end in fire. The honest framing is not "which is safer" but "each needs its own containment" — a tip-proof candle holder on one side, a heatproof ash-catcher with clearance on the other. Treating the ember as automatically safe is the mistake that lets a stick get propped on a bookshelf. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## FAQ

### Q: What exactly is burning inside each one?
Two different fuels. A stick burns plant material — wood powder, resins, and botanicals bound with a binder — and the ember releases that material's aroma as smoke. A candle burns wax as a fuel with fragrance oil mixed in, and the flame melts and vaporizes the wax so the scent rides out with the heat. The core material difference is plant matter versus wax, and the practical difference is an ember at the tip versus an open flame at the wick — that fork drives soot, smoke, and fire risk alike.

### Q: Which of the two is safer to burn?
Neither, in a simple sense — each carries a different risk. A candle has an open flame that can ignite nearby material if knocked over or set too close to curtains or paper. A stick has an ember rather than a flame, but that ember is hot enough to scorch or ignite if the stick tips into flammable material. Neither is categorically safer; both need a stable holder on a heatproof surface, clearance from anything flammable, and no unattended burning. The [safety guide](/blog/incense-safety-guide/) covers both.

### Q: Does a candle actually give off smoke?
Yes. A burning candle produces soot and fine particles even when the flame looks clean and the output is barely visible. Candle smoke is usually less visible than incense smoke, which is why candles are often wrongly assumed to be smoke-free, but combustion always puts particles into the air — a flickering, smoking wick produces more soot. A candle is not a "clean air" option; both a candle and a stick are combustion sources to use in a ventilated room.

### Q: Which one keeps going longer?
A candle, generally by a wide margin. A candle is a reservoir of wax that burns slowly, so a single jar or pillar runs for hours — often tens of hours across many uses. A stick is one session: it burns about thirty to sixty minutes depending on thickness and then is gone. So a candle suits a long, ambient evening or repeated use, while a stick is a timed, one-sitting experience. Treat them as different durations, not two versions of the same product.

### Q: Which one puts more particles into the room?
Both are combustion sources and both put airborne particles into the room — incense smoke and candle soot — so neither is particle-free and neither is "clean air." Incense tends to produce more visible smoke, and some studies of indoor burning show incense releasing substantial fine particulate matter; candles produce soot too, though often less visibly. The exact amount depends on the material, the burn, and the room, and ventilation is the practical remedy. The detail is in [incense ventilation & indoor air](/blog/incense-ventilation-indoor-air/).

### Q: Can I burn a candle and a stick together?
You can, but they compound what each already needs. Both add particles to the room at once, so ventilation matters more, and you are now managing an open flame and an ember in the same space rather than one fire to watch. If you want both the glow and the material scent, place them on separate heatproof surfaces with clearance between them, and do not leave either unattended. Most people get a cleaner result by picking one for the moment — the candle for the evening, the stick for the ritual.

## The Wick's Hidden Pump

**Direct answer:** A candle wick is not a passive string — it is a pump that lifts molten wax upward against gravity, and the whole burn depends on that lift staying steady.

The chain runs on capillary action: the flame melts a shallow pool at the top → the hollow fibers of the wick draw the liquid wax upward the way a paper towel pulls water → the heat vaporizes the wax at the tip → the vapor feeds the flame → the flame keeps the pool molten. If any link breaks, the candle misbehaves. A wick trimmed too short starves the pump and the flame drowns in its own pool (tunneling); a wick left too long feeds too much fuel and the tip "mushrooms" with unburned carbon, which is the source of soot. None of this exists on a stick, where the ember walks down a solid rod with no pump and no pool — which is the deeper reason the two burn so differently.

## Wax Isn't One Thing: A Fuel Matrix

**Direct answer:** "Candle" hides a spread of wax fuels, and each one changes how much soot and scent the burn produces — so comparing a candle to incense without naming the wax is comparing two different candles.

| Wax base | Source | Burn traits | Soot tendency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paraffin | Petroleum byproduct | Hot, strong scent throw | Higher (long wick = more) |
| Soy | Soybean oil | Cooler, slower, softer scent | Lower |
| Beeswax | Honeycomb | Warm, faint honey note, long burn | Low |
| Coconut | Coconut oil | Slow, even, creamy | Low |

The table matters to the comparison because a "clean" candle and a "sooty" candle are often the same product category with different fuel, while an incense stick is always the same plant-paste fuel. The practical rule: if soot is your concern, the wax and the wick length matter more than the word "candle" on the label.

## What the Flame Actually Eats

**Direct answer:** A candle flame is a small chemical engine that runs on vapor, not on solid wax — and recognizing that vapor is the real fuel explains every difference in how the two burn.

The chain: the flame heats the wax surface → wax melts → the melt evaporates into a thin vapor layer around the wick → the vapor, not the liquid or the solid, is what actually combusts → the heat of that combustion melts more wax in turn. The flame never touches the solid wax directly. An incense ember, by contrast, burns the solid paste in place, charring the surface as it advances, so its fuel is the solid itself rather than an evaporated phase. That single contrast — vapor-fed flame versus solid-fed ember — is why a candle needs a stable melt pool and a wick to keep it supplied, while a stick needs neither and simply walks down its own length.

## The "Which Is Better" Trap

**Direct answer:** Asking whether a stick or a candle is better is a category error — they are not two grades of one product, they are two different tools, and the honest answer is a boundary, not a verdict.

The misconception to correct is that a comparison page must end in a winner. It must not. A candle is better when you want a reservoir of hours and a flame; a stick is better when you want a short, self-terminating session with a material scent and no wax. The boundary that actually helps a buyer: choose by what you want the object to do, not by a quality score — a "better" candle does not replace a stick any more than a "better" lamp replaces a flashlight. Once you name the job, the product picks itself, and the whole versus question dissolves.

## Reading Both Before You Buy

**Direct answer:** Two quick checks — the wick state on a candle and the core and ash on a stick — predict most of the burn quality before anything is lit.

| Check | Candle | Stick |
|---|---|---|
| Look at the wick | Trimmed, centered, no mushroom | — |
| Look at the core | — | Bamboo core or coreless, evenly coated |
| Cold sniff | Faint, natural | Botanical, not perfume-loud |
| Surface | Smooth, no cracks | Straight, unbroken paste |

The shared principle: both formats telegraph their quality in the parts you can see before lighting. A candle with a leaning or over-long wick will soot and tunnel; a stick that smells like a perfume bottle and shows a thick bamboo core will smoke more than a thin coreless one. Ten seconds of looking saves the hour of regret.

## More FAQ

### Q: Why does my candle smell stronger when it is lit than when it is unlit?
Because a candle stores its fragrance in solid wax, and heat is what frees it. Unlit, the surface of the wax releases only a faint "cold throw." Once the flame melts a pool, the warm liquid evaporates fragrance far faster, which is the "hot throw" that fills a room. An incense stick works differently: its fragrance is already in the dry paste and leaves as the ember consumes it, so there is no cold-versus-hot throw gap of the same kind. This is also why a candle's scent fades between burns and a stick's is gone once the rod is spent — the candle keeps its payload in the reservoir, the stick spends it all in one pass.

## Scent Throw, Explained in One Chain

**Direct answer:** "Scent throw" — how far a fragrance carries — is not a single property but a chain of three links, and a stick and a candle break different links to fill a room.

The chain: a heat source vaporizes fragrance → the vapor mixes into the surrounding air → the air moves and carries the scent outward. A stick dominates the first link (its ember vaporizes aroma fast and copiously) but relies on air movement for the last; a candle dominates the third link (its flame creates a rising draft that pulls room air toward it and pushes the scent outward in a steady loop) while releasing fragrance more slowly. That is why a strong candle can scent a whole room with a modest fragrance load — its flame is a built-in fan — while a stick's throw depends more on where the air is already moving. The practical difference: a candle scents a room by circulating it, a stick scents it by emitting into whatever draft exists.

## A Substitute Table: When Neither Is the Right Tool

**Direct answer:** Sometimes the honest answer to "stick or candle" is neither — a third format does the job better — and the table maps the cases where each format misfires.

| You want | Stick's problem | Candle's problem | Better tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| No flame, no ember, no soot | Has an ember | Has an open flame | Electric diffuser |
| A timer you can leave running | Short, fixed burn | Needs supervision (flame) | Reed diffuser |
| No smoke at all | Produces smoke | Produces soot | Reed or electric diffuser |
| A scent you can pause and restart | Burn is one-shot | Relights, but pools unevenly | Wax melts with a warmer |

The table's lesson is that stick and candle sit on the same side of a bigger divide: both are combustion. Once you need the benefits of *no combustion*, both lose to the flameless formats, and the honest recommendation is to step off the comparison entirely. Choosing between two burners only makes sense while you actually want something burned.

## More FAQ

### Q: Why does my stick's scent fade faster than my candle's?
Because the two release fragrance on different clocks and store it in different places. A stick's fragrance is all in the dry paste, and the ember consumes that paste in a single pass — when the rod is spent, the scent source is gone, and what remains in the room is only what already escaped. A candle keeps most of its fragrance locked in the wax reservoir and releases it gradually across the whole burn, so the source lasts for hours. Add to that the candle's flame-driven draft, which keeps circulating the scent, and the room stays scented long after a stick would have finished. It is not that one is stronger — it is that one spends its fragrance in one sitting and the other meters it out across an evening.

## Wick Trimming and the Soot Loop

**Direct answer:** The single most effective candle habit — trimming the wick — works because a long wick feeds too much fuel at once, and the excess becomes soot; the fix is a cleaner burn, not a cleaner candle.

The chain: a wick left long draws more molten wax than the flame can burn → the unburned fuel leaves the flame as carbon → that carbon condenses as the dark smoke and black smudge on the jar → a trimmed wick lowers the fuel flow to what the flame can fully combust → the burn runs hotter and cleaner. The same logic has no incense equivalent, because a stick's ember self-limits — it can only consume the paste directly in front of it, with no reservoir to overfeed. That is the physical reason candles need wick care and sticks need none, and why a neglected candle smokes more than a well-made stick despite looking "cleaner."

## More FAQ

### Q: Why does my candle smoke when I blow it out, but not while it burns?
The smoke you see at the moment of blowing out is vapor, not soot. While the flame burns, the wick's heat vaporizes wax into gas that the flame consumes; the instant you extinguish it, the wick stays hot enough to keep vaporizing wax for a second or two, but there is no flame left to burn that vapor — so it escapes as a visible white plume that quickly condenses. It is the same wax vapor the flame was eating a moment earlier. The plume is brief and mostly harmless, but it is a reminder of the vapor-fed mechanism: the candle runs on gas, and that gas is still rising right after the flame dies.

## The Flash Point and Why Wax Never Quite Burns

**Direct answer:** A candle is a subtle paradox — the solid wax is not what burns, and the "flame" is actually a zone of vapor igniting just above the pool, which is why the wax level drops but the solid itself never catches fire.

The chain: the flame's heat radiates down → the top of the pool vaporizes at the wax's flash point → the vapor rises and mixes with air → the mixture ignites in a thin shell above the wick → the burning shell feeds heat back down to vaporize more wax → the cycle holds the flame aloft while the solid wax below stays cool enough not to ignite. That last point is the safety-relevant one: a candle rarely spreads fire from the wax itself; it spreads fire when the flame or the hot jar touches something flammable nearby. The wax is the reservoir, the vapor is the fuel, and the flame is a standing wave of combustion parked just above the pool.

## More FAQ

### Q: Why doesn't the wax itself catch on fire?
Because solid wax has to vaporize before it can burn, and the flame is already consuming that vapor the instant it forms. The flame heats only the top surface of the pool to its flash point, turning a thin layer into gas; the gas ignites above the wick, and that combustion keeps the pool just hot enough to release more gas — but not hot enough to set the deeper solid wax alight. The flame stays parked in the vapor zone, not in the wax. That is why a candle dies when the wax is gone rather than bursting into flame: there is simply nothing left to vaporize.

## The Ember Front: How a Stick Advances

**Direct answer:** A stick's ember is not a point that travels — it is a moving front, a thin burning zone that advances through the paste, and the front's speed is what the burn time actually measures.

The chain: the lit tip chars the paste directly ahead of it → the charred material glows and feeds the next millimeter → the front advances at a steady rate set by the paste's density and the air supply → the spent paste behind the front collapses into ash → the stick burns from one end because the front has only one direction to go. A candle's flame, by contrast, stays put above the pool while the *fuel* moves up to it — the candle's reaction zone is stationary and the wax travels; the stick's reaction zone travels and the fuel stays still. That inversion is the cleanest way to see why the two burn so differently, and why a stick's duration is a distance-over-speed problem rather than a reservoir problem.

## More FAQ

### Q: Why does a stick burn faster in a draft?
Because the ember's speed is set partly by how fast oxygen reaches the burning front, and moving air delivers oxygen faster. A draft fans the ember, much as blowing on a campfire makes it surge, so the front advances more quickly through the paste and the stick finishes sooner. The same stick in still air smolders slower. This is why a stick burned near an open window or a fan runs short — the draft is quietly shortening the session, not just carrying the smoke away. If you want a longer burn, shield the stick from the strongest airflow.

## The Fragrance Load: How Much Scent Each Actually Carries

**Direct answer:** A stick and a candle carry their fragrance in different concentrations and different states, which is why a stick can smell stronger with less total material — the scent is more concentrated in the paste than in the wax.

The chain: a stick's fragrance is blended into the paste at a relatively high load because the paste is mostly aromatics and binder → each millimeter of paste delivers a dense burst as it burns → the scent throw is strong but short-lived. A candle's fragrance oil is dissolved into a large mass of wax → the oil is a small fraction of the total → the flame releases it slowly as the wax vaporizes → the throw is steady but more dilute. The difference is concentration: the stick spends its scent fast and hot, the candle meters it out. That is why a small stick can fill a room faster than a large candle, and why the candle keeps going long after the stick's burst has faded.

## More FAQ

### Q: Which is better for a strong scent, a stick or a candle?
For immediate strength, a stick usually wins — its paste carries a concentrated fragrance load that the ember releases in a fast, dense burst, so the scent throw hits quickly. A candle releases its fragrance more slowly from a large wax reservoir, so its throw is steadier but less concentrated at any one moment. The trade-off is duration: the stick's strong burst ends in an hour, while the candle's gentler release runs for hours. So "stronger" depends on the time scale — the stick is stronger now, the candle is stronger over an evening.

## The Wick Size Question: Why Candles Tunnel

**Direct answer:** A candle that burns a narrow hole down its center — "tunneling" — is a wick-and-wax mismatch, where the flame melts a pool too small to reach the walls, and the wax is wasted around the edges.

The chain: a wick that is too thin for the candle's diameter melts only a small pool → the pool never reaches the jar's walls → the flame keeps consuming the center and sinking → the unmelted wax hardens as a ring around the edge → the candle burns down a narrow shaft and dies early with wax left behind. The fix is a wick sized to the wax column, or burning long enough on the first light that the pool reaches the edge before the wax sets. A stick has no equivalent failure, because it has no reservoir to tunnel — it consumes the whole rod uniformly. Tunneling is a candle-specific geometry failure, not a wax-quality one.

## More FAQ

### Q: Why does my candle leave wax stuck around the sides?
That is tunneling, and it happens when the flame's melt pool never reaches the jar walls, so the outer wax never melts and sets back as a ring. The usual causes are a wick too small for the candle's diameter, or burning it for only short periods so the pool never spreads before it re-hardens. On the first burn, let the candle stay lit long enough for the melted pool to reach the edge — usually an hour or more for a wide jar — and that establishes the full pool the later burns will follow. A stick does not do this because it has no wax reservoir to strand.

## The Relight Question: Why a Candle Restarts, a Stick Cannot

**Direct answer:** A candle is the one format you can pause and resume, because its fuel reservoir survives the flame — while a stick, once lit, is a one-way trip that cannot be undone.

The chain: a candle's wax pool remains after the flame is blown out → relighting the wick restarts the melt-and-vapor cycle from where it stopped → the same reservoir keeps serving across many sessions → a stick's paste is consumed in a single advancing front → once a segment is ash, it cannot burn again → relighting a half-burned stick skips the spent portion and only finishes the rest. The practical difference is that a candle meters out its life across evenings, while a stick spends its whole life in one sitting. If you want a scent you can stop and restart, the candle is the answer; if you want a fixed, un-repeatable ritual, the stick is the point.

## More FAQ

### Q: Can I relight a stick that went out halfway?
You can, but only the unburned remainder — the ash and the spent paste behind the ember cannot burn again, so relighting only finishes the remaining length. The result is a shorter session, and the aroma profile may read slightly differently because the top notes already flashed off during the first burn. It is safe enough to relight, but the format is not designed for it: a stick is a one-way burn, unlike a candle that keeps its reservoir. If you frequently want partial sessions, a candle or a shorter stick is the better fit than relighting.

## The Ash-Drop Chain: Why a Stick Needs Something Under It

**Direct answer:** A burning stick drops ash continuously, and that ash is still hot as it falls — so the surface beneath the burn is part of the safety setup, not an afterthought.

The chain: the ember advances and the spent paste collapses → the ash falls away from the rod → each falling fleck carries residual heat for a moment → if it lands on paper, fabric, or a finished table, that heat can mark or ignite → a heatproof tray beneath the burn catches the ash and absorbs the heat → the hazard is neutralized at the point of contact. That is why a holder without a tray is only half a setup, and why a stick propped on a bare shelf is the classic avoidable accident. The ash is the spent fuel, but it is not cold the instant it drops.

## More FAQ

### Q: Do I really need a tray under my stick, or is a holder enough?
You need both a holder and a heatproof catch surface, because they do different jobs. The holder keeps the stick upright; the tray catches the falling ash, which is still warm enough to mark a surface for a moment after it drops. A holder with no tray lets the ash fall onto whatever is below — a table, a shelf, a book — which is how marks and fires start. The complete setup is a steady holder over a heatproof, non-flammable tray, with clearance around the burn. The tray is not decoration; it is the second half of the safety.

## The Flame-Height Chain: Why a Candle's Height Is a Thermostat

**Direct answer:** A candle flame is not a fixed object — its height tracks the fuel flow, and a tall, dancing flame is the candle running rich, which is exactly when the soot appears.

The chain: the wick draws fuel at a rate set by its length and the pool's heat → more fuel vapor rises into the flame → the flame burns taller → a taller flame has more fuel than oxygen can fully consume at its edges → the excess carbon leaves as soot → a steady, modest flame means the fuel and air are balanced, a tall, flickering flame means they are not. The flame's height is the visible readout of the fuel-air balance, which is why a smoking, flickering candle is usually a wick-too-long or draft problem rather than a bad wax. Reading the flame is reading the burn's health.

## More FAQ

### Q: Why does my candle flame flicker and smoke sometimes?
Because the fuel-air balance is being disturbed, usually by a draft or a too-long wick. A draft pushes the flame around and cools one side, so the combustion runs uneven and a wisp of unburned carbon escapes as smoke; a long wick overfeeds the flame, making it tall and sooty. Trim the wick and move the candle out of the draft, and the flame settles into a steady, low-soot burn. The flicker and smoke are not the wax misbehaving — they are the flame telling you its fuel or air supply is out of balance.

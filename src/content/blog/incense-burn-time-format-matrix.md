**Direct answer:** Pick a format by answering two questions: how many minutes the session needs, and how much smoke the room can absorb. Sticks usually give 20–60 minutes, cones 15–30, coils 1.5–4 hours; resin and powder follow whatever heat source you use — an electric warmer produces almost no smoke, charcoal much more.

## Key facts

Treat these figures as planning windows. Diameter, paste pressure, binder load, storage moisture and the air in the room all shift the result, so a label describes an average batch in an average room rather than the piece in your hand.

## A Planning Table, Format by Format

**Direct answer:** The table below sets out seven common formats with their working windows, smoke output, the setting each suits and the stand it needs.

| Format | Working burn | Smoke | Where it fits | Resting on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thin coreless stick | about 20–40 min | Light to moderate | Brief sittings, compact rooms | Stick holder or tray |
| Standard stick | about 30–60 min | Moderate | Daily use | Holder with an ash catcher |
| Cone | about 15–30 min | Moderate | Short bursts, backflow displays | A flat heatproof dish |
| Coil (盘香) | about 1.5–4 hours | Moderate | Long, uninterrupted scent | Coil stand or plate |
| Resin over electric heat | As long as the heater runs | Very low | Low-smoke, controlled release | Heater plus a small dish |
| Resin over charcoal | about 15–30 min per disc | Higher | Traditional, forceful release | Disc bedded in sand or ash |
| Powder (香粉) | Set by the method | Varies | Seal patterns (篆香), bespoke blends | Charcoal, or pressed into shape |

The rows are not a leaderboard. Each one trades something away: the coil buys hours with a long commitment, the cone buys intensity with a short window, and the electric heater buys clean air with a device on the table. A useful comparison reads the trade in each row rather than hunting for a "best" entry.

## Two Variables Carry the Whole Table

**Direct answer:** Session length and smoke tolerance decide the format; holder, cleanup and ritual follow from those two choices rather than leading them.

Ask how long the session actually runs, then ask how much smoke the room can take. A forty-minute meditation and a ten-minute pause are different problems even if the same person lights both. A small bedroom and a ventilated studio are different problems even if the same stick is burning.

The two variables do not move together. A coil is long and moderate-smoke. A charcoal resin is short and high-smoke. An electric heater is long and near-zero-smoke. That independence is why "which incense is best" has no single answer, and why the same shelf can hold three formats that are each correct for a different evening.

## What Actually Moves the Clock

**Direct answer:** Five variables set a burn time, and the one a ruler measures — length — is the weakest of them.

Start with the cross-section. More paste per millimetre of travel → the ember front has more fuel to consume at each step → the front advances more slowly → the stick lasts longer. A fatter stick therefore tends to outlast a slimmer one cut to the same length.

The binder is the second knob, and it works in the opposite direction. A paste with a higher binder share → ignites more readily and smoulders hotter → the front chews through the aromatic material faster → the stick finishes sooner while throwing a stronger scent. A lean blend can do the reverse and gutter, because there is too little combustible structure to carry the front.

Packing density is the third. A tightly packed paste holds less air between its grains → the ember smoulders cooler and creeps → the same volume takes longer to spend. A loose, airy paste lets the front race. Two sticks can share a length and a diameter and still behave like different products because one was pressed harder.

Moisture is the fourth. A stick stored in a damp cupboard spends its first minutes driving water off → the front stalls and cools → the burn runs uneven, then speeds up once the moisture is gone. Dry storage is not a fussy habit; it is part of the burn time.

Airflow is the fifth. A glowing ember needs oxygen, and moving air keeps replacing the spent layer around it → the ember burns hotter and advances faster → a stick near an open window finishes before the same stick in still air. The maker's test room rarely has your ceiling fan.

| Knob | Direction | Pull on the clock | How you notice it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cross-section | Thicker burns longer | Strong | Two sticks, same length, different diameter |
| Binder ratio | More binder burns faster and hotter | Strong | A stick that flares, throws hard, and ends early |
| Packing density | Tighter packing burns slower | Moderate | A dense stick outlasts a loose one of equal size |
| Storage humidity | Damp burns cooler and unevenly | Moderate | Sputtering at the tip, then a late rush |
| Room airflow | A draft shortens the burn | Moderate | The same box behaves differently by a window |
| Length | More distance, same speed | Weak | A long thin stick losing to a short fat one |

The ranking matters for buyers. Two of the strong knobs are invisible in a product photo, and one of them — binder ratio — is rarely printed on a label. That is the honest reason a burn time is sold as a range.

Length, the visible number, says how much material sits on the stick, not how long the stick will take to spend.

## Why "Approximate" Is the Honest Word

**Direct answer:** A range such as 20–60 minutes is not vagueness; it is the most accurate statement a manufacturer can offer about a system with five independent variables and a room the maker does not control.

A single printed minute would imply control over the buyer's humidity, airflow and storage. No manufacturer has that control. The range is a confession of the real structure: the same recipe behaves differently across seasons, rooms and batches.

This is the misconception worth correcting. Buyers often read a range as a sign of a sloppy maker and a precise number as a sign of a rigorous one. A precise number can be equally average — it simply hides its assumptions. What distinguishes a careful maker is not a tight number but a stated test condition: still air, moderate humidity, a dry stick, lit once at the tip.

## Reading the Clock as a Diagnostic

**Direct answer:** A burn that misses its label is information about the batch, not just an inconvenience, and the kind of miss points to a different cause.

| What you observe | What it suggests | How to respond |
|---|---|---|
| Finishes 20–40% early | Loose packing or a thin pour | Plan a shorter session; treat the label as optimistic |
| Same box burns unevenly | Moisture or a dense spot in the paste | Store sealed and dry; try another stick before judging the batch |
| Ember keeps dying | Too little binder to carry the front | A fragile blend; relight or choose a different product |
| Scent fades before the ember | Binder outlasting the fragrance load | Judge by the nose, not the remaining burn |
| Flares, then races | High binder share or a very dry stick | Expect a strong opening and a short life |
| Burns long but faint | Dense packing with a light aromatic load | A slow, quiet product rather than a defective one |

The table turns a missed time into a readable symptom. "This cone is short" is a description of the format; "this cone is loosely packed" is a description of the batch, and only the second one helps a buyer decide whether to return to the same maker.

## Format Notes: Where Each Row Earns Its Place

**Direct answer:** Each format solves a different kind of evening, and the notes below say which one, at what cost.

### Thin and standard sticks

A stick is the easiest thing to own: one rod, one small stand, no special fuel. The slim coreless type gives twenty to forty minutes and a light plume, which suits a desk or a bedroom where a heavy cloud would be unwelcome. A standard rod runs thirty to sixty and holds a steadier presence. Both like an upright holder with something underneath to catch ash, and both reward still air.

What a stick cannot do is stop halfway. Once the tip catches, the ember intends to walk the whole rod unless you intervene. That makes it excellent for a fixed sit and awkward for a session that might end at any moment.

### Cones

Buy a cone when the session is short and you want the room to notice. Its broad face consumes quickly, so the fragrance lands as a concentrated opening rather than a long murmur. Fifteen to thirty minutes covers most of the burn. Backflow versions route the smoke downward for a visual effect; the window stays short, and the dish still has to take heat.

### Coils

The spiral is the marathon runner of the shelf. An hour and a half to four hours is normal, and larger coils stretch further. Its geometry also gives you portion control — snap off what you need and keep the rest. While it burns, the spent ring lays down ash beside the live edge; that ash insulates the ember, keeping the advance slow and even instead of flaring. Plan to stay within sight of it.

### Resin on an electric heater

Here the material never catches fire. A heater holds the resin or wood below its combustion point, so the aromatic compounds leave as vapour and the plume is close to nothing. You get a long, controlled session and a cleaner room, at the cost of a device, a socket and a gentler throw. Small flats, rented rooms and households that dislike smoke are where this row earns its keep.

### Resin on charcoal

A glowing disc is the old way, and it is not subtle. Heat hits the resin immediately, the volatiles leave in a rush, and the session is short and loud. Expect roughly fifteen to thirty minutes per disc and more smoke than any other row. Bed the disc in ash about a finger deep: enough to protect the vessel, not so much that the disc drowns as it sinks.

### Powder and seal incense

Powder refuses to have one burn time. Press it into a seal groove (篆香) and it walks a measured path; scatter it over charcoal and it flashes; fold it into a stick and it borrows the stick's clock. Ask what the powder is for before asking how long it lasts. The method is the answer.

## Same Material, Different Clocks

**Direct answer:** Hold the aromatic material constant and change only the shape, and the session changes character — proof that format, not scent, sets the pace.

| Shape, same material | What sets the pace | What the session feels like |
|---|---|---|
| Stick | A narrow face advancing down a rod | Even, moderate, easy to time |
| Cone | A wide face consuming itself fast | A dense opening and a quick finish |
| Coil | A spiral plus the ash bed it leaves behind | Slow, continuous, and in need of watching |

A sandalwood lover who wants a longer session does not need a different sandalwood. The lever is the shape and the heat source. That is the practical significance of the whole matrix: it separates the material decision from the format decision, so a buyer can solve them one at a time.

Duration and intensity are separate knobs. A cone spends its material across a wide face in a short window → the room receives a dense burst. A coil spreads a similar load across hours → the scent stays present but softer at any given moment. The long format is the steadier one, the short format the louder one; buyers who assume longer means stronger have the trade backwards.

## Choosing in Five Steps

**Direct answer:** The decision takes five steps, and doing them in order prevents the usual mismatch between a format and the evening it was bought for.

1. **Name the session length.** Ten minutes, forty minutes, three hours. Be concrete.
2. **Name the smoke ceiling.** A large room with open windows, a small bedroom, or a space with a sensitive occupant.
3. **Pick the format that matches both.** Use the matrix, not the shelf order.
4. **Burn one piece as a calibration.** The first burn measures your room, not the maker's.
5. **Adjust placement before adjusting the product.** Drafts, distance and holder angle change the burn more than a different brand usually will.

The steps are deliberately boring. Most format mistakes come from starting at step three — choosing a product first and inventing a use for it afterwards.

## The Safety Boundary: A Long Burn Still Needs a Person

**Direct answer:** No incense should burn unattended, and a coil's multi-hour window is a supervision plan rather than permission to leave the room.

Every row in the matrix involves heat, and most involve open combustion. Keep the burner on a heatproof surface, clear of curtains, paper and other flammables, and out of reach of children and pets. Do not place it where a draft can move ash or tip a holder. Ventilate the room, and do not treat a low-smoke format as a no-risk format — an electric heater still gets hot.

The coil deserves its own warning because its length invites misuse. A four-hour coil in an empty flat is not a long ritual; it is an unattended fire. If the session cannot be watched, choose a shorter format or skip the burn. The safety page has the fuller checklist: [incense safety](/safety/). Airflow and ventilation are covered in [incense, ventilation and indoor air](/blog/incense-ventilation-indoor-air/).

## Measuring Your Own Burn Time

**Direct answer:** A home burn test takes one holder, one timer and three sticks from the same batch, and it produces a number that applies to your room rather than the maker's.

Run the test in the space where the incense will actually burn. Set the holder on a stable, heatproof surface away from a window, a fan and an air-conditioning vent. Light the tip, let the flame settle into a steady ember, and start the timer at that moment rather than at the match. Stop it when the ember reaches the holder. Write down the date, the room and whether the air felt dry or damp.

Repeat with two more sticks from the same bundle. The three readings tell you two different things. A tight cluster — say, all three within a few minutes of one another — suggests a consistent batch and a stable room. A wide spread suggests that moisture, packing or airflow is varying between burns, and the average is less trustworthy than the range.

One rule matters: do not time the flame. A stick can hold a visible flame for several seconds while the ember is still forming, and that time is not part of the burn. Start the clock when the flame has gone and the tip is glowing on its own.

What you do with the result is a planning decision. If your room shortens a nominally forty-minute stick to thirty, plan thirty-minute sessions around that stick or move the holder out of the draft. The label remains the maker's average; your three readings are the local figure. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## What the Table Leaves Out

**Direct answer:** A burn-time chart describes a piece of incense, not a room, and three omissions are worth naming before anyone treats the numbers as a complete buying guide.

Room volume and air exchange matter more than most buyers expect. A high-ceilinged open-plan kitchen and a small study with the door shut will read the same plume differently, and mechanical extraction can clear a space while a closed bedroom holds it. The chart cannot know either.

Olfactory fatigue is the second omission. A scent that seems to vanish after twenty minutes may still be present; the nose has simply stopped reporting it. This is why a stick can feel weak halfway through and then smell strong again after a break outdoors. Judging output by one continuous sit therefore overstates the fade.

The third omission is legal and practical. Some leases forbid open flame, some buildings have sensitive smoke detectors, and shared walls carry odour. None of that changes a burn time, yet all of it changes which row of the table a reader can actually use.

Read the matrix as a shortlist; the room, the nose and the rules decide the rest.

## Storage Changes the Clock

**Direct answer:** How the incense was kept before you bought it can move the burn further than the difference between two brands.

A sealed tin or a lidded jar slows the exchange of moisture with the room. A cardboard box left on a windowsill does the opposite: it breathes, takes in damp air and lets sunlight warm the contents. The stick that spent a summer in that box may be brittle at the tip and stubborn in the middle, and no label accounts for the summer.

Buy in quantities you will burn within a season, keep the bundle sealed, and store it away from radiators and direct sun. If a batch performs poorly, try a stick from the centre of the pack before blaming the recipe — the outer pieces take the room's weather first.

## FAQ

### Q: How many minutes does one stick give?

A mainstream stick most often lands in the half-hour-to-hour band; a slim coreless type runs shorter, nearer twenty to forty minutes. Diameter, how hard the paste was pressed, the binder load and the cupboard it lived in all shift the result. Identical-looking sticks can part company by ten minutes or more. If the session is timed, measure one stick in your own room first.

### Q: Which format burns the longest?

A coil (盘香) holds the record among self-contained pieces: an hour and a half to four hours, and the big spirals go beyond that. Heated resin can run longer still, yet the mechanism is different — the device keeps supplying warmth, so there is no fixed piece to finish. Between a coil, a stick and a cone, the spiral is the only one built for an uninterrupted stretch.

### Q: What is the cleanest-burning option?

Warmed resin on an electric device. No flame touches the material, the temperature stays under the combustion point, and the aroma arrives as vapour with far less particulate. Charcoal sits at the opposite extreme, and any lit stick, cone or coil makes visible smoke by definition. A slim coreless stick is the middle path for someone who wants a flame without a heavy plume.

### Q: Why does burn time drift inside one box?

No two pieces in a bundle are quite the same object. The paste is pressed with slight variation along each rod, dampness migrates through the pack while it sits, and the piece nearest a cold wall may hold more water than the one at the front. Add the room: a holder beside a fan or an open sash behaves differently from one in still air. Tiny gaps in packing and moisture show up plainly on a clock.

### Q: Can I leave a coil burning overnight?

No. The very trait that makes a coil useful — hours of slow smouldering — is what makes it unsafe without a person nearby. Stand it on a heatproof base, on a surface that cannot tip, well clear of cloth, paper and draughts, and put it out before you sleep or leave. For overnight scent, a heater on a timer is the more sensible route; it still belongs on a heatproof surface.

**Direct answer:** Two arts, one word. 香道 names a field in Chinese and a bounded discipline in Japanese, and the difference is not decoration: one side composes materials into a formula, the other warms a single graded wood and follows it. The method travelled east; the codification, the lineages, and the scoring stayed Japanese.

**Key facts:** The covered-coal arrangement 隔火焚香 is documented in Song China and is generally understood to have crossed with Buddhist and tea traffic, taking formal shape in the Muromachi period (c. 1336–1573). One column of the comparison runs warm and layered; the other runs spare and close to smokeless.

## Key Takeaways

- The Chinese side is an art of assembly. 合香 gives each material a rank drawn from herbal practice: one leads, one supports, one softens, one lifts.
- The Japanese side is an art of attention. A graded piece of wood is warmed on a plate and followed in sequence.
- The two share a name and a technique, not a purpose. 香道 names a wide field in one language and a formal discipline in the other.
- Influence ran eastward. Say it as the balance of sources supporting a direction, not as a proven chain of custody.
- Formats multiplied in China and narrowed in Japan, until the everyday forms converged and stopped signalling anything.
- The verb gap is the sharpest marker: one tradition listens, the other evaluates.
- Both grading vocabularies are inherited frameworks. Neither is a laboratory result or a map reference.
- Smoke is smoke. Nothing here is a health claim, and refined methods reduce exposure without removing it.

## One Name, Two Practices

**Direct answer:** The same two characters name a wide field in one language and a bounded discipline in the other. That gap in scope is where the comparison starts.

Chinese usage treats 香道 as a domain: everything from burning a stick in a study to composing a seasonal formula to grading a piece of wood belongs inside it. There is no single syllabus, no examinations, no line of transmission that everyone recognises. The field is held together by texts, trade, and habit rather than by an institution.

Japanese usage treats it as a post: a specific practice, entered through a teacher, with a fixed sequence, a small set of approved materials, and an etiquette that governs how you sit and how you handle a plate. Two schools dominate its modern form, and the grammar of a session is stable enough to be taught, assessed, and inherited.

What follows is a difference in what the practitioner is *for*. In the Chinese field the practitioner is a maker and a judge: someone who can build a formula and say whether it works. In the Japanese post the practitioner is a listener and a witness: someone who can sit with one piece of wood and report what it did, accurately, in company.

Both roles are demanding, and neither is a lesser version of the other. The confusion arises only when the shared characters are read as a shared job description.

## How the Influence Actually Ran

**Direct answer:** The paper trail leans in a single direction. Chinese texts describe the refined method long before Japan gives that method a name, and it seems to have moved along the routes that carried monastic traffic and tea.

Three fixed points, then one inference.

The fixed points are textual. The character 香 is cut into Shang oracle bones, which puts aromatic burning in the Chinese record long before any Japanese codification, and by the Han period the ritual use is unambiguous. The plate-over-coal arrangement — 隔火焚香, the setup that keeps flame away from the material — is set out in Song material and preserved in Ming compilations, among them 周嘉胄's 香乘.

The inference is the crossing. No surviving document is a travel diary: nothing says a named monk carried this method to a named temple in a named year. What survives is a pattern — the same liturgical traffic, the same tea culture, the same exchange among literate elites — and a Japanese practice that adopts the arrangement and then, in the Muromachi period, gives it schools, procedure, and a name. Assembled from several secondary accounts, that reading is stronger than any single line of evidence, and it remains a reading rather than an entry in a ledger.

One more Chinese frame is worth carrying across. 吴自牧's 梦粱录 sets incense beside tea, flowers, and painting among the cultivated pleasures of Song Hangzhou — four arts, none of them useful in the ordinary sense. The instinct that aroma belongs with the arts and not with the apothecary is the premise Japan later pushed to its end, by taking one of those arts and making a discipline of it.

**Evidence status:** the crossing is interpretation, not primary documentation. The Chinese texts are primary for the technique itself. Neither side contributes a therapeutic claim to this page.

Dynasty-level detail sits in the [history of Chinese incense](/blog/history-of-chinese-incense/), which follows the thread in both directions.

## Why Burying the Charcoal Changes the Art

**Direct answer:** Covering a live coal with ash is a temperature instrument, not a safety measure. Everything else about the aesthetic — the low smoke, the staged release, the long quiet session — descends from that one arrangement.

Run the chain. A coal is lit until it glows, then buried in sifted ash → the ash limits the oxygen reaching its surface → it burns cooler and more slowly than it would in open air → the heat arriving at the plate settles below the point where wood ignites → the resin leaves as vapour instead of burning → the aroma arrives in stages rather than as a single scorched event.

The depth of that burial is the dial. Press the coal closer to the surface and the release is brighter and faster; set it deeper and the session becomes slower and more layered. An experienced hand adjusts depth to the material in front of them, which is why the same chip can behave differently on two trays.

The hardware follows from the physics. Sift the ash so the bed is even, press it so the surface holds, place and cover the coal, lay a thin plate on top, and rest the material on the plate. A Chinese setup does the same work with less ceremony: buried charcoal, a mica or thin silver disc, material above. A Japanese setup turns the same steps into a fixed sequence with named tools. The engineering is identical; the ritual is not.

There is one beginner's error worth naming. A bare coal with material dropped on it is not a shortcut to this practice — it is a different event. The wood chars, the lightest compounds die first, and what reaches the nose is scorch. The ash bed is not decoration around the technique. It is the technique.

## The Heating Window and Its Two Edges

**Direct answer:** Aromatic wood gives its best between two failures. Too little heat and nothing arrives; too much and the wood is ruined before it can speak.

The lower edge is easy to misread. Warm a chip gently and you may conclude the material is dull, when the truth is that the resin never volatilised. Beginners abandon good wood this way, blaming the chip for a timid flame or a shallow ash bed.

The middle is where the sequence lives. The opening lifts first and light, the body thickens, and the tail turns quiet and dry. A chip can run a long time here because consumption is slow; nothing is being destroyed to produce the effect.

The upper edge is a cliff rather than a slope. Past a certain point the wood chars, compounds break down instead of evaporating, and the nose reads sharp, tarry, and short. That transition is the one thing every practitioner learns on their own equipment, because it depends on the burner, the plate, and the draft in the room rather than on the tradition.

No universal temperature belongs in this paragraph. Published figures are device-specific, and quoting one as if it were a standard would mislead anyone using different hardware. What transfers is the procedure: start low, climb in small steps, and stop before the sharp turn.

**A disclosure that belongs in the body, not a footnote:** reduced smoke is not absent smoke. Covered heating lowers particulate output relative to a burning stick, and it does not eliminate it. Ventilation, session length, and personal sensitivity all matter, and none of that is a reason to treat incense as a treatment for anything.

## The Form Repertoire: Many Vessels, One Object

**Direct answer:** Chinese makers kept inventing containers for aromatic material; Japanese practice removed almost everything between the sitter and one piece of wood.

A useful way to read the Chinese range is by what each form does to time. Loose material gives no fixed duration — you control it by how much you put on the heat and how close it sits. A pressed seal burns along a groove at a predictable pace, which is why it doubles as a clock and as something to watch. A stick runs for a set stretch and then stops. A coil stretches that stretch across hours, which is what temple and shop use wants. A cone compresses it into a short, dense burst.

The Japanese range answers a different question. The warmed chip gives no fixed duration either, but for the opposite reason: the practitioner is not trying to finish, only to follow. Around that chip sits a fixed apparatus — bed, press, chopsticks, plate, burner. The everyday form, the thin stick without a bamboo spine, exists because a core adds a woody note and a visible seam of ash; removing it gives a cleaner burn and a more even one. Nerikō, the kneaded pellet of powder and binder, goes the other way: it concentrates a blended formula into a single dense object that releases slowly over warmth.

That pellet is the revealing case. It is composition in Japanese dress — several materials worked into one mass — but the destination is a single austere object rather than a repertoire of shapes. The Chinese instinct multiplied formats; the Japanese instinct reduced them until only the quietest remained. How old the pellet is, precisely, is not settled in the sources consulted here. ## The Verb: Listen, Not Smell

**Direct answer:** Kōdō's working verb is 聞 — to hear, to listen — rather than 嗅, to smell. The choice is not poetic decoration. It changes what counts as a mistake.

Listen to a piece of music and you can be wrong about it: you can miss the entry of a theme, confuse two movements, lose the thread. Smell a thing and there is nothing to be wrong about — the sensation is simply present. Once fragrance is treated as something heard, it becomes something that can be followed, remembered, compared, and scored, and a whole apparatus of training follows from that single verb.

The practitioner's report reflects this. You do not say "it is sweet and woody"; you say what arrived first, what came forward later, how long the tail held, whether the second chip matched the first. That is sequence language, not label language. It is why a session can be discussed afterwards, and why a teacher can correct a student's account of a chip rather than only their taste.

Chinese practice has no single equivalent verb, and the omission is not a deficiency. Its vocabulary is about construction and assessment: proportions, processing, density, grade. The practitioner's report is a judgement of the object — how the chief material carried, whether the guide note lifted too fast, whether the blend held together at the end. One tradition reports an event; the other reports a verdict.

The practical difference shows up in what beginners are told to do. A Chinese beginner is handed a stick and asked what they notice about the blend. A kōdō beginner is handed a chip and asked to describe what it did, in order, without adjectives of liking. Both are training attention. They are training it toward different objects.

## Two Grammars for One Wood

**Direct answer:** Both traditions grade agarwood, and their vocabularies meet at the top grade and diverge almost everywhere else. A rough crosswalk helps; the gaps in it are the useful part.

| Label you will meet | Nearest idea in the other tradition | What the label actually encodes |
|---|---|---|
| kyara (伽羅) | 奇楠 | Top grade — sensory in Japan, density-linked in China |
| rikkoku gomi (六国五味) | No counterpart | Six inherited "countries" and five taste words; not provenance data |
| 沉水 | No counterpart | A sink test, using density as a proxy for resin |
| monkō (聞香) | No single verb | Reception treated as a discipline rather than an errand |
| kumikō (組香) | No counterpart | Scored discrimination games, answer key included |
| jinkō (沈香) | 沉香 (chénxiāng) | Agarwood itself, written in two languages with the same characters |

On the Chinese side the test is physical and can be repeated by anyone with a glass of water. A piece that goes down carries more resin than one that floats, and the trade has built its grades on that simple observation. 奇楠 sits above the ordinary ladder: soft enough to mark with a fingernail, fragrant without heat, described in the trade as oil wrapping wood.

On the Japanese side the categories are sensory and literary. Rikkoku gomi sorts material into six inherited "countries" and five taste words, with kyara at the summit. Those country names read like places and are not reliable addresses; treating them as provenance is the most common mistake in English writing on the subject. They are received categories, and they carry connoisseurial information rather than assay data.

The gaps in the table are the argument. A density test has no counterpart in a tasting vocabulary, and a verb of reception has no counterpart in a vocabulary of proportions. Force one system to answer the other's question and both come out wrong. For the material itself, two pages go further: [agarwood grading](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/) and the pairing of [qínán with kyara](/blog/qinan-kyara/).

## What the Comparing Games Prove

**Direct answer:** A scored smelling game is a philosophical statement. It says the nose can be trained, that its performance can be measured, and that a wrong answer is possible. Chinese practice never needed to make that claim.

The mechanics are plain. Several chips are prepared, some of them duplicates. They are warmed one after another. Each sitter records which burn corresponds to which earlier burn, and a key settles the answers. In the best-known variant, genjikō, the pattern of matches is written as a chapter reference from *The Tale of Genji*, so a correct sheet is also a small literary joke. Scoring schemes differ between schools; the tables are not reproduced here. [need-source]

Strip away the literary layer and what remains is an examination. The point is not to enjoy the scent but to be right about it, and being right requires a vocabulary stable enough to compare across an hour. That stability is the actual product of the game. A practitioner who can score a sheet can also describe a chip to someone who was not in the room.

Compare the Chinese testing tradition. It tested material: sink or float, resinous or dry, soft or hard, top grade or ordinary. The tester was an instrument measuring an object. In the Japanese game the object is fixed and the tester is measured. Two different directions of scrutiny, and the difference explains why kōdō developed pedagogy around perception while Chinese practice developed manuals around processing.

## The Tool Set as a Syllabus

**Direct answer:** Kōdō ships its method as objects. Press, chopsticks, plate, burner — each one removes a decision from the sitter's hands and fixes it in metal, bamboo, or ceramic.

Look at what the objects decide for you. The press flattens the bed to a repeatable height, so the coal sits at a depth the previous session also used. The chopsticks let you place and cover the coal without breaking the surface you just pressed. The plate holds the material above the heat rather than in it. The burner's proportions set the distance from nose to chip, which sets how concentrated the experience is. None of these are conveniences. They are decisions made in advance so that the sitter's attention is free for the wood.

Chinese practice put its method in text. A manual states proportions, describes processing, warns about substitutions, and leaves the equipment to whatever the household had. That is why the recipes travelled: paper crosses borders easily, and a written proportion can be followed anywhere. The Japanese kit did the opposite — it kept the method local by embedding it in hardware that has to be handled, in a room, with someone who already knows.

The result is two different kinds of entry barrier. A reader can begin Chinese practice alone with a book and a stick. A kōdō beginner needs a teacher or at least a kit and a demonstration, because a kit without a demonstration is just a box of tools. Neither barrier is a virtue in itself. They simply follow from where each tradition decided to store its knowledge.

## The Two Practices Side by Side

**Direct answer:** Read down the columns and the traditions stop looking like rivals; read across the rows and each choice looks like a consequence of the one above it.

| Question | Assembled practice (合香) | Receptive practice (kōdō) |
|---|---|---|
| When is the work done? | Before the session, at the bench | During the session, at the burner |
| What is being adjusted? | Proportions and processing | Distance and heat |
| What is the unit of skill? | A formula that behaves as intended | A chip read accurately |
| How is teaching stored? | In printed recipes | In tools and in a teacher's hands |
| What goes wrong first? | Notes that never fuse | Heat that runs past the window |
| What carries the cost? | Ingredients and judgement | Grade and scarcity |
| Where does the tradition break down? | When breadth becomes undisciplined | When reverence outruns the material |

The rows are ordered by dependency, which is the point of the table. A practice that finishes its work at the bench will store its knowledge in recipes and price itself on judgement. A practice that finishes its work at the burner will store its knowledge in tools and price itself on material. Each of the lower rows follows from the first one.

## Reading a Box by the Question It Asks

**Direct answer:** Labels lie less than people think, provided you read them for the question they answer rather than the impression they create.

| What the packaging offers | Which side it belongs to | What to check before buying |
|---|---|---|
| A named formula with several ingredients | Chinese | Whether proportions or processing are described anywhere |
| One wood with a grade and an origin story | Japanese | Whether the grade term is a descriptor or a claim about place |
| Sticks, coils, cones, and powder in one line | Chinese | Whether the breadth is a range or the same paste in four shapes |
| Ash, press, plate, and burner sold together | Japanese | Whether instructions or a demonstration come with the kit |
| A blended pellet meant for warming | Japanese (nerikō) | Whether the binder and its proportion are disclosed |
| A single-wood product from a Chinese studio | Either | Which question the seller answers first — material or method |

The last row is the one that catches experienced buyers. A Chinese studio selling single-wood chips is doing something the tradition permits and the market rewards, so the product cannot be placed by its format. Place it by the sentence the seller reaches for: an answer that begins with grade and heat points to a receptive practice; one that begins with a batch and its processing points to an assembled one.

## Where the Money Goes: Composition Against Rarity

**Direct answer:** The two arts are not sold on the same scale. One charges for what was assembled; the other charges for what survived in a single piece of wood.

A blended product carries the cost of its ingredients plus the cost of judgement — the proportions, the processing, the decision about which material leads. Those costs are diffuse. No single component explains the price, and two blends using similar materials can sit far apart because one is better balanced. The buyer is paying for a decision made by someone else, and the only way to audit it is to burn the thing.

A chip carries cost in a narrower place. The price tracks the piece: species, resin load, density, aroma, and above all scarcity at the top of the range. Skill does not enter the price. The same chip costs the same whether a master or a beginner warms it, and that is a strange thing to notice about an art built on skill.

Follow the consequence. Because value concentrates in the material, kōdō is expensive to practise and cheap to fake: a poor chip cannot be rescued by technique, and a good chip needs almost none. Because value concentrates in judgement, Chinese practice is cheap to enter and hard to fake at the top: anyone can buy powder and a press, and the difference between competent and excellent is invisible until the stick is lit.

The boundary worth stating: **"more refined" is not a price ranking, and "more expensive" is not a quality ranking.** They measure different scarcities. A collector who understands that stops asking which tradition is worth more and starts asking which scarcity they are willing to fund.

## Why Neither Tradition Won

**Direct answer:** They were never in the same contest. One optimised for reach, the other for depth, and each optimisation produced the market it now has.

Start from the material and the rest follows. Fine agarwood is limited, kyara more so, and a practice built on warming single pieces of it cannot scale. Cost per session stays high, teaching stays personal, and the art stays small — a few schools, a few houses, a clientele that treats the expense as part of the discipline. That is not decline. It is what the material permits.

Chinese practice faced a different constraint. Its materials span the cheap and the precious, so a maker can build a product for a temple, a household, a gift box, or a collector's cabinet without changing the underlying craft. Breadth follows, and with breadth comes the ordinary commercial noise that any broad market generates.

The two now overlap on the shelf. A Chinese studio may sell a single-wood chip and a warming set; a Japanese house may sell a box of everyday sticks at a price that undercuts the local competition. Format stopped being a reliable label years ago, and the old shortcut — thin stick means Japan — now misleads more often than it helps.

What survives the overlap is the question each tradition asks. Ask what a blend is made of and how it was processed, and you are reading the Chinese side. Ask which wood, which grade, and how it is heated, and you are reading the Japanese side. Products have converged; the questions have not.

## Where the Comparison Usually Goes Wrong

**Direct answer:** Most errors in this comparison come from agreeing too quickly — either collapsing the two traditions into one, or ranking them on a single ladder that neither uses.

Five habits worth breaking.

**Treating the refinement as Japanese by origin.** The covered-coal method is Chinese in the record. Japan's achievement is systematization, which is a different and considerable thing; the confusion usually comes from the fact that Japan preserved what China kept modifying.

**Turning a strong reading into a certainty.** "The technique travelled east" is well supported by converging accounts. "We know exactly who carried it and when" is not. The first claim is defensible in a sentence; the second invites the correction that undermines the first.

**Expecting visible smoke.** Refined practice is nearly flameless, and a visitor who came for atmosphere may leave disappointed. The disappointment is a category error, not a defect in the art.

**Reading grade names as addresses.** Rikkoku gomi's six "countries" and the colour terms around 奇楠 are inherited descriptors. They organise connoisseurship; they do not certify where a piece was dug up.

**Assuming shared vocabulary means shared practice.** 香道 is the same two characters on both sides. What sits under them is a different training, a different economy, and a different idea of what a good session is.

## Choosing a Starting Point

**Direct answer:** Pick the question you want to spend a year on, not the tradition that sounds more serious. Everything else — budget, kit, reading — follows from that choice.

| If this is what you want | Begin here | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| To understand how materials interact | A small set of Chinese sticks and a few loose materials | Fast progress in vocabulary, slow progress in judgement |
| To learn to follow one aroma closely | One decent chip and a gentle heat source | Slow start, then a sudden shift in what you can hear |
| To spend little while learning the most | Chinese everyday sticks first, one chip later | The cheapest route into both questions |
| To practise attention as a discipline | A kōdō procedure, even borrowed informally | The sequence teaches before the nose does |
| To judge quality in a shop | The Chinese density frame first | A physical test beats a poetic vocabulary |

The order is deliberately asymmetric. Starting on the assembled side is cheaper and gives faster feedback, which is why most beginners do it; starting on the receptive side teaches patience faster, because there is nothing to hide behind. Neither order is wrong. What is wrong is assuming that one year of sticks teaches you what a chip feels like, or that one year of chips teaches you how a formula is built.

## A First Session, Either Way

**Direct answer:** One evening with a stick and one with a chip will teach the difference better than another thousand words about it. The trick is to write down what happened before you decide what you think.

For the stick: light it, let the flame settle, and give it a minute before you start paying attention. Then note three moments — the opening, the middle, and the tail. Repeat with a second stick from the same box on another evening. If the three moments land in similar places both times, the formula is consistent; if they wander, the batch is uneven or your room is interfering.

For the chip: start cooler than you think you should, and raise the heat in small steps. Note the temperature setting, or the depth of ash, at which aroma first appears, and the point at which it turns sharp. That second point is your working ceiling on that device, and it stays useful for years. Everything below it is the art; everything above it is a bonfire.

Then put the two pages side by side. The stick gave you a structure someone else designed; the chip gave you a sequence you had to find. That contrast — designed versus discovered — is the comparison in miniature, and it is more convincing than any table in this article.

## Reading the Evidence, Not the Marketing

**Direct answer:** Most claims attached to incense are inherited rather than tested, and the useful skill is telling the two apart without becoming a bore about it.

Three labels cover almost everything on a product page. A **traditional belief** is something the craft has held for a long time and cannot verify: that a particular wood suits a particular season, that a grade name marks a region. A **documented practice** is something the manuals describe and makers still do, such as ageing a blend before use. A **measured claim** would require test data, and for aroma and burn behaviour that data is rarely published.

The last category is where marketing does its work. Words like "pure", "wild", "aged", and "single-origin" carry commercial weight and no fixed definition, so a buyer cannot check them against a standard that does not exist. That is not a reason to distrust every seller. It is a reason to ask for the thing behind the word: which species, which grade frame, what processing, how long rested.

The same discipline applies to history. Dates, dynasties, and lineages are the easy part to state and the easiest part to overstate; the sentence "the technique travelled east" and the sentence "the technique was carried east in 538 by a specific embassy" look adjacent on the page and are nowhere near each other in evidential weight.

A practical test for any write-up, including this one: count how many sentences could survive being read aloud to a maker without embarrassment. A sentence that says a wood is scarce is checkable in principle. A sentence that says an aroma opens the heart is not, and it belongs to somebody's poetry rather than to a product description.

There is one claim this page refuses outright. Nothing here suggests that burning or heating aromatic material improves health. Smoke is an exposure, refined methods lower it without removing it, and a reader with respiratory sensitivity should treat every session as something to ventilate rather than something to benefit from.

## What to Do With the Comparison Afterwards

**Direct answer:** Keep the comparison as a diagnostic, not as an allegiance. It is most useful when a product or a session confuses you and you need a question to ask next.

Three situations repay it. A stick smells flat and you cannot tell whether the formula is weak or the batch was rushed: the assembled side tells you to look at processing and balance. A chip turns harsh before it opens: the receptive side tells you the heat ran past the window. A shop cannot explain what it sells: the two-column table tells you which question it failed to answer.

Allegiance is the least useful thing to take away. Readers who decide they are "a kōdō person" or "a Chinese incense person" after an afternoon of reading have usually chosen a team rather than a practice. The traditions are old enough to survive without supporters, and the reader is the one who loses by narrowing too early.

## FAQ

### Q: How do Chinese incense and Japanese kōdō differ?

One is a kitchen, the other a listening room. The Chinese side takes many materials and builds a formula in which each has a role, then delivers it as a stick, a coil, a cone, a pressed seal, or loose powder. The Japanese side takes one graded piece of wood, warms it over a covered coal, and asks the sitter to follow what happens. China's art is judged by the composition; Japan's by the attention. If you want the two halves in more depth, start with [Chinese incense explained](/blog/what-is-chinese-incense/), then [Japanese incense and kōdō](/blog/japanese-incense-kodo/).

### Q: Did one tradition turn into the other?

No single tradition became the other, and the honest phrasing matters. The record supports a direction of travel: a Chinese heating method went east with monastic and mercantile traffic, and Japanese practitioners turned it into a formal art. Calling that "Japan copied China" overstates it; calling the two unrelated understates it. What happened is closer to a technique finding a second home and being rebuilt there around different priorities.

### Q: Why "listening" rather than "smelling"?

The verb in use is 聞, which covers hearing and listening, and the choice is deliberate. A listener follows a sequence and can be wrong about it; a smeller registers a fact. Once the practice is framed that way, comparison games and scoring become possible, because there is something to get right. Chinese practice never needed that framing. Its tests were about material — how dense, how resinous, how well composed.

### Q: A thin stick without a bamboo core must be Japanese, surely?

That rule has expired. Japanese makers popularized the coreless stick because it burns without a woody spine, and Chinese studios adopted the format because buyers like it. Both now sell it, at very different quality levels. Judge the product by what the seller can say about it: a formula with proportions and processing behind it, or a wood with a grade and a heater to match.

### Q: Is any of this good for my health?

Nothing here is offered as therapy, and the traditions themselves are aesthetic and ritual practices rather than treatments. Heating aromatic material produces smoke and fine particles; indirect methods produce less, not none. Ventilate the room, keep sessions moderate, and treat respiratory sensitivity as a reason to limit exposure rather than a reason to expect benefit.

## Summary

**Direct answer:** Two traditions, one material, opposite instincts. Chinese practice assembles; Japanese practice attends.

The mechanics carry the aesthetics. Bury charcoal and the burn cools; put a plate above it and the wood stops short of ignition; work that window and resin leaves in stages rather than in a flare. Around that physics China built recipes, formats, and a density-based grading culture. Japan built procedure, tools, lineages, and games with answer keys.

The direction of influence runs from Song China to Muromachi Japan, and the honest way to say so is that the evidence supports it rather than proves it. Neither tradition is a better version of the other, and neither is owed the last word. A reader who wants to build should go and build; a reader who wants to listen should go and listen. The comparison earns its keep by showing that one material can support two entirely different disciplines without either one being diminished.

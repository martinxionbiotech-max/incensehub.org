**Direct answer:** A classic incense scent traces back to one plant part — the heartwood of sandalwood, an exudate from frankincense, a jasmine petal, citrus peel, a clove bud, cassia bark. Once you can name the part, you know the material class, and the class predicts both the smell and the behaviour over heat.

## Key facts

Nothing a buyer can read on a label predicts the burn better than the plant part. Wood and resin are dense and slow, so they hold a blend down. Petals are fragile and mostly reappear as accords, because the raw material scorches. Peel, bud and bark flash bright and leave early. One material in the trade is scentless by design — makko, the binder that gives a stick its shape.

## Mapping scent to plant part

**Direct answer:** Fourteen scents cover most of the classical trade, and each one maps to a species, a part and a family. The table is the page compressed — read the part column first, then the family.

| Scent | Plant | Part | Family |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood | *Santalum album* | Heartwood | Santalaceae |
| Agarwood | *Aquilaria* spp. | Resin-impregnated wood | Thymelaeaceae |
| Frankincense | *Boswellia* spp. | Gum-resin | Burseraceae |
| Myrrh | *Commiphora* spp. | Gum-resin | Burseraceae |
| Benzoin | *Styrax* spp. | Resin | Styracaceae |
| Jasmine | *Jasminum* spp. | Flower | Oleaceae |
| Rose | *Rosa* spp. | Flower | Rosaceae |
| Osmanthus | *Osmanthus fragrans* | Flower | Oleaceae |
| Lotus | *Nelumbo nucifera* | Flower | Nelumbonaceae |
| Lavender | *Lavandula angustifolia* | Flowering top | Lamiaceae |
| Citrus / 陈皮 | *Citrus reticulata* | Peel | Rutaceae |
| Clove | *Syzygium aromaticum* | Bud | Myrtaceae |
| Cassia | *Cinnamomum cassia* | Bark | Lauraceae |
| Makko (binder) | *Machilus thunbergii* | Bark | Lauraceae |

Two rows are deliberately odd. Makko is a bark that smells of almost nothing and exists to make the stick burn; it belongs here because a buyer who does not recognise it will read "binder" as an adulterant. Agarwood is a wood only in the sense that the wood is the carrier — the aromatic fraction is resin produced after injury, which is why grading follows darkness and density.

## Beyond the fourteen: roots, leaves and cousins

**Direct answer:** The classical fourteen are the trade's backbone, not its whole inventory. Roots, rhizomes and leaves follow the same part logic with a different burn, and several *Burseraceae* cousins sit close enough to the resins above to be confused with them.

| Material | Plant | Part | Family | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copal | *Bursera* spp. | Resin | Burseraceae | Pale, citrus-pine, softens at low heat |
| Elemi | *Canarium luzonicum* | Resin | Burseraceae | Peppery-lemon, softer than frankincense |
| Labdanum | *Cistus ladanifer* | Leaf resin | Cistaceae | Sticky, ambered, leathery |
| Opopanax | *Commiphora* spp. | Resin | Burseraceae | Sweet-balsamic, sold as "sweet myrrh" |
| Guggul | *Commiphora wightii* | Resin | Burseraceae | Dense balsamic with a pharmacy edge |
| Spikenard | *Nardostachys jatamansi* | Rhizome | Caprifoliaceae | Rooty, heavy, valerian-adjacent |
| Vetiver | *Chrysopogon zizanioides* | Root | Poaceae | Dry earth, smoke-adjacent |
| Calamus | *Acorus calamus* | Rhizome | Acoraceae | Sweet-woody, spicy, faintly camphor |
| Patchouli | *Pogostemon cablin* | Leaf | Lamiaceae | Damp earth, camphor, sweet |
| Camphor | *Cinnamomum camphora* | Wood | Lauraceae | Sharp, cooling, crystalline |

The additions extend the logic rather than break it. A rhizome is dense and moist, so it releases slowly, like a wood with less finesse; a root behaves the same way with more earth in the profile; a leaf is thin and quick, close to a peel and shorter in persistence. The four *Burseraceae* cousins matter because they are the resins most often met under a neighbouring name.

The character column grades behaviour, not quality: adding a material to the map says nothing about how it was grown or sorted, only which part you are dealing with and how it will behave when warmed.

## The part is a burn instruction

**Direct answer:** Density and moisture decide how a material behaves over heat, and the part supplies both. That is why the part predicts the burn more reliably than the species does.

Follow the chain for a wood. Heartwood is dense and low in moisture → heat penetrates slowly → volatiles leave over a long window → the aroma arrives late and stays → the material anchors a blend.

Now the same chain for a petal. A petal is thin, wet and delicate → direct heat flash-vaporizes its surface oils → the light compounds scorch before they can travel → what reaches the nose is char and a ghost of the flower.

Resin sits between the two. It is solid but not fibrous → it melts rather than smoulders → the melt releases volatiles in a steady stream → the release is bright at first and heavy at the end.

Peel, bud and bark take a fourth path. They are dry and thin-walled → they give up their oils fast → the note arrives immediately and fades quickly → they lift a composition at the top and rarely carry it.

A material's job follows from its structure, and its structure follows from the part.

## Why floral notes are rebuilt

**Direct answer:** A flower's scent is a thin oil in fragile surface cells, and heat destroys it before it can be enjoyed. The trade either captures the oil first or rebuilds the smell from other materials.

Capturing the oil is the costly route. Rose and jasmine can be extracted into absolutes, and serious work does use them — a large mass of petals yields a small mass of extract, and the harvest window is days, not months.

Rebuilding is the norm. An accord assembles a flower's impression out of other aromatic materials, and the result stays consistent and costs far less. That is not a defect; it is the standard method, and a good accord can smell closer to a living flower than a mediocre absolute does.

The distinction that matters is method, not authenticity. "Is this real lotus?" is the wrong question. "Is this an extract or an accord?" is the right one — a maker who uses an absolute generally says so, because the cost is a selling point.

## Four bands, one label

**Direct answer:** The part sorts the market into four bands — material, accord, component, structure — and each band says what kind of thing the label is selling.

**Material.** Wood and resin names point at plant substance — sandalwood, agarwood, frankincense, benzoin — so the question is species and grade.

**Accord.** Flower names point at a reconstruction — jasmine, rose, lotus, osmanthus — so the question is extract or accord.

**Component.** Peel, bud and bark names point at a top note. A stick described only as "citrus" is a lift with little behind it.

**Structure.** Makko and similar binders are neither scent nor filler; they are why a stick holds shape.

"Sandalwood" claims material; "lotus" claims an impression. Neither word is dishonest; treating them as the same kind of statement is where buyers lose the thread.

## The binder that carries no scent

**Direct answer:** Makko is powdered *Machilus thunbergii* bark, and its job is combustion, not fragrance. It binds the aromatic powders, keeps the stick straight and burns cleanly enough to disappear.

A stick is a composite. Fragrant powders — woods, resins, spices — do not hold together alone. The binder supplies adhesion while wet and a steady ember when lit.

That is why "contains natural binder" is information rather than a confession. A stick without one is pressed under great pressure, dipped in a combustible carrier, or not a stick at all.

One boundary worth keeping: the binder is not where quality is decided. Quality lives in the fragrant fraction — which species, what grade, how fresh.

## Common names drift; binomials do not

**Direct answer:** The same English word can point at plants in different families, so a common name carries no family, no part and no chemistry. The binomial carries all three.

Three examples cover most of the confusion. "Cedar" can mean a true *Cedrus* or the unrelated juniper sold as cedarwood in aromatic trade. "Cassia" can mean *Cinnamomum cassia* bark or drift toward other cinnamon species depending on the market. "Lotus" wanders between the aquatic *Nelumbo* and several unrelated plants depending on the tradition doing the naming.

The pattern is old and not going away, because common names travel with trade and translate badly. A buyer's defence is simple: find the binomial. *Santalum album* is unambiguous in a way that "sandalwood" is not.

There is a second trap inside the first. A label naming the genus but not the species — " *Boswellia* resin" — is honest and still incomplete, because the species decides the aroma.

## The harvest calendar explains the price

**Direct answer:** Each part becomes fragrant material on its own schedule — decades for heartwood, a season for resin, days for flowers — and that schedule quietly explains the price tags.

| Part | Example | When it is taken | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heartwood | Sandalwood, agarwood | After decades of growth, or after wounding | Slow to form, deep and persistent, expensive |
| Gum-resin | Frankincense, myrrh | On a seasonal tapping cycle | Renewable annually, moderate cost |
| Flower | Jasmine, rose | At bloom, often picked by hand | Narrow window, low yield, costly extract |
| Peel, bud, bark | Citrus peel, clove, cassia | At maturity, often dried | Cheap, stable, bright |

Read the calendar as a cost model. Heartwood is a decades-long investment in a slow-growing tree, so it is dear before anyone touches it. Resin renews annually from wild or semi-managed stands, which keeps it affordable. Flowers are a days-long harvest with a poor mass ratio — exactly why the market prefers accords.

## Heartwood and the decades-long wait

**Direct answer:** Sandalwood's aroma lives in the dense centre of the trunk and takes decades to develop, which is why the genuine material is costly and why so much "sandalwood" is something else.

*Santalum album* is hemiparasitic: it draws water and nutrients from neighbouring roots while also photosynthesising, which makes it slow and fussy to establish. Oil builds in the heartwood as the tree ages, so a young trunk carries little worth and an old one carries a great deal.

For a buyer the shape is familiar: expensive, finite, and easy to fake with pale wood or an accord. The part is heartwood; the open question is whether this piece matches the name.

Agarwood reaches a similar place by another route. The wood alone is unremarkable; the value sits in resin produced after wounding, which is why the trade grades by darkness and density rather than species.

## Resin: the seasonal case

**Direct answer:** Frankincense and myrrh are tapped on a seasonal rhythm, and the timing of the cuts shapes yield and grade.

The mechanism is a wound response. A cut severs resin ducts → the tree pushes a milky exudate to seal it → air takes the water and the lightest volatiles → the remainder hardens into a tear that is picked off by hand.

Season matters because sap movement follows the growing cycle. Cuts made while the tree is active yield more; a first pass over a fresh wound runs thin and often darker; later passes give the thicker, cleaner tears that grade higher. Over-cut a stand and the trees weaken, which surfaces as lower yield in following years rather than in today's price.

For a buyer, the result is simple: resin is the affordable classical material, and its quality tracks weather and harvest discipline more than any manufacturing step.

## Peel, bud, bark: fast top notes

**Direct answer:** Citrus peel, clove buds and cassia bark are dry, thin-walled and loaded with volatile oil near the surface, so they release quickly and fade quickly.

陈皮 — aged tangerine peel — is the classical Chinese citrus note, and it rounds off as the oil oxidizes. Clove buds carry eugenol, a heavy phenolic note that reads warm and dental and swamps anything beside it in quantity. Cassia bark brings cinnamon's heat with less floral softness than true cinnamon.

None of these anchors a composition. Their role is the first minute, a bright opening before the heavier materials arrive; used as a base they collapse under the weight.

## Matching the part to the job

**Direct answer:** Choose the part by the job — anchor, body, lift or structure — and the material follows. Choosing by scent name first is how blends end up unbalanced.

| Job in the composition | Part to reach for | Why it works | Failure if overused |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchor the base | Heartwood | Dense, slow, persistent | Muddy, heavy, flat |
| Build the body | Resin | Melts steadily, fills the middle | Cloying, sooty |
| Lift the opening | Peel, bud, bark | Fast volatile release | Sharp, thin, one-note |
| Give a floral signature | Flower accord or absolute | Impression without scorching | Perfumed, synthetic edge |
| Hold it all together | Makko or another binder | Combustion and adhesion | Brittle sticks, uneven burn |

The table is the argument in one page. A composition needs one anchoring material, something in the middle, and a way to open. A stick built entirely from top notes smells loud for ninety seconds and then smells of nothing.

## Where the map stops

**Direct answer:** The part predicts the type of material and its behaviour. It does not grade quality, certify origin or prove the material is present at all. *Editorial boundary, not a measured result.*

The misconception to correct is that a botanical map doubles as an authenticity test. "Sandalwood" predicts heartwood, a slow burn and a creamy-woody profile; it says nothing about whether the powder is *Santalum album*, a cheaper *Santalum* species, or pale wood hiding under an accord.

What the map gives is a cheap first filter. A product claiming wood character at peel prices deserves a question, and a "lotus" stick priced like a real absolute invites the mirror question. The map narrows what to ask; the asking remains.

## A three-step label check

**Direct answer:** Name the part, infer the band, then confirm the method. Three checks turn a label from a list of pleasant words into a prediction.

**Step one — name the part.** Wood, resin, flower, peel, bud, bark or binder. A name that implies no part is marketing, not information.

**Step two — place the band.** Wood and resin mean material, flower means impression, peel and spice mean component, binder means structure.

**Step three — confirm the method.** A flower invites the extract-or-accord question; a wood or resin invites species and origin; a blend invites the question of what carries its base.

## FAQ

### Q: Which part of sandalwood carries the smell?

The heartwood of *Santalum album* and a few relatives — not leaves, flowers or outer bark. Oil accumulates in the trunk's dense centre over decades, and that slow build explains the price.

### Q: Is a floral note in incense ever the real flower?

Rarely as petal material. Direct heat scorches petals, so most floral sticks rely on an accord built from other aromatics. Rose and jasmine absolutes do appear in better work, yet the everyday floral note is a rebuild — a method, not a fraud.

### Q: What exactly is frankincense?

A gum-resin that seeps from *Boswellia* bark once the tree is cut. A secretion rather than a structural part, it is filed under resins. It sets into tears sorted by size, colour and cleanliness.

### Q: Why is makko listed as a plant source if it has no scent?

Because it is the one plant material whose job is structure. Powdered *Machilus thunbergii* bark binds the powders and burns steadily, and it is near-odourless in the finished stick. Listing it signals a transparent formula, not a warning. See [makko and natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

### Q: Can one plant yield several incense materials?

Yes. A citrus tree supplies peel for the bright note and flowers for neroli-type material in perfumery; a *Cinnamomum* supplies bark and a leaf oil with a different profile. The part is the variable — which is why a label naming only the plant is still incomplete.

### Q: Does the part say anything about quality?

About behaviour, not grade. Whether the material was grown well, harvested cleanly and labelled honestly is a separate question — species, origin, sorting, supplier.

## Summary

The map is a four-column shortcut: scent, plant, part, family. Read the part and you can predict density, burn speed and the rough shape of the aroma before anything is lit; read the family and you can place the material among its relatives.

What it cannot do is grade the material or certify the species. "Sandalwood" should be heartwood and should behave like one; which *Santalum* sits in the box is another question. Keeping those jobs apart is the difference between using the map and trusting it too far.

The rest is method. Woods and resins anchor, flowers are rebuilt, peel and spice lift, binders hold. A composition that respects those roles burns as designed; one that ignores them smells of its loudest ingredient and nothing else.

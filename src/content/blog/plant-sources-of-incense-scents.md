**Direct answer:** Every classic incense scent comes from one part of a plant — heartwood in sandalwood, an exudate in frankincense, a petal in jasmine, a peel in citrus, a bud in clove, bark in cassia. Name the part and you have named the material class, which predicts both how it smells and how it behaves over heat.

**Key facts:** Plant part is the most reliable single predictor a buyer has. Woods and resins are dense and slow, and they anchor a blend. Flowers are delicate and are almost always rebuilt as fragrance accords because raw petals scorch. Peels, buds and barks give a fast, bright lift. One plant material in the trade carries no scent at all — makko, the binder that holds a stick together.

## Key takeaways

- Scent names are shortcuts to a **plant part**, and the part is what predicts the burn.
- **Heartwood** means decades of growth and a slow, deep release: sandalwood, agarwood.
- **Resin** means a wound secretion, tapped seasonally: frankincense, myrrh, benzoin.
- **Flowers** rarely burn as themselves. The note you smell is usually an accord, occasionally a costly absolute.
- **Peel, bud and bark** are top-note materials — bright, quick, seldom alone.
- **Makko** is structure, not fragrance. Its presence on a label is normal.
- The common name tells you the *impression*; the binomial tells you the plant. Only one of them survives translation.

## The botanical map

**Direct answer:** Fourteen scents cover most of the classical trade, and each one maps to a species, a part and a family. The table is the page compressed — read the part column first, then the family.

| Scent | Plant | Part | Family |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood | *Santalum album* | Heartwood | Santalaceae |
| Agarwood | *Aquilaria* spp. | Resin-impregnated wood | Thymelaeaceae |
| Frankincense | *Boswellia* spp. | Gum-resin | Burseraceae |
| Myrrh | *Commiphora* spp. | Gum-resin | Burseraceae |
| Benzoin | *Styrax* spp. | Resin | Styracaceae |
| Jasmine | *Jasminum* spp. | Flower | Oleaceae |
| Rose | *Rosa* spp. | Flower | Rosaceae |
| Osmanthus | *Osmanthus fragrans* | Flower | Oleaceae |
| Lotus | *Nelumbo nucifera* | Flower | Nelumbonaceae |
| Lavender | *Lavandula angustifolia* | Flowering top | Lamiaceae |
| Citrus / 陈皮 | *Citrus reticulata* | Peel | Rutaceae |
| Clove | *Syzygium aromaticum* | Bud | Myrtaceae |
| Cassia | *Cinnamomum cassia* | Bark | Lauraceae |
| Makko (binder) | *Machilus thunbergii* | Bark | Lauraceae |

Two rows are deliberately odd. Makko is a bark that smells of almost nothing and exists to make the stick burn; it belongs in the table because a buyer who does not recognise it will read "binder" as an adulterant. Agarwood is a wood only in the sense that the wood is the carrier — the aromatic fraction is a resin produced in response to injury, which is why the material is graded by how dark and dense the infection ran.

## Beyond the fourteen: roots, leaves and cousins

**Direct answer:** The classical fourteen cover the trade's backbone, not its whole inventory. Roots, rhizomes and leaves follow the same part logic with a different burn, and several *Burseraceae* cousins sit close enough to the resins above to be confused with them.

| Material | Plant | Part | Family | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copal | *Bursera* spp. | Resin | Burseraceae | Pale, citrus-pine, softens at low heat |
| Elemi | *Canarium luzonicum* | Resin | Burseraceae | Peppery-lemon, softer than frankincense |
| Labdanum | *Cistus ladanifer* | Leaf resin | Cistaceae | Sticky, ambered, leathery |
| Opopanax | *Commiphora* spp. | Resin | Burseraceae | Sweet-balsamic, sold as "sweet myrrh" |
| Guggul | *Commiphora wightii* | Resin | Burseraceae | Dense balsamic with a pharmacy edge |
| Spikenard | *Nardostachys jatamansi* | Rhizome | Caprifoliaceae | Rooty, heavy, valerian-adjacent |
| Vetiver | *Chrysopogon zizanioides* | Root | Poaceae | Dry earth, smoke-adjacent |
| Calamus | *Acorus calamus* | Rhizome | Acoraceae | Sweet-woody, spicy, faintly camphor |
| Patchouli | *Pogostemon cablin* | Leaf | Lamiaceae | Damp earth, camphor, sweet |
| Camphor | *Cinnamomum camphora* | Wood | Lauraceae | Sharp, cooling, crystalline |

The additions extend the logic rather than break it. A rhizome is dense and moist, so it releases slowly, like a wood with less finesse; a root behaves the same way with more earth in the profile; a leaf is thin and quick, close to a peel and shorter in persistence. The four *Burseraceae* cousins matter because they are the resins most often met under a neighbouring name.

Notice what the table does not contain: no scent column for quality, only character. Adding a material to the map says nothing about whether it was grown well or sorted honestly. It only says which plant part you are dealing with and how it will behave when warmed.

## The part is a burn instruction

**Direct answer:** Density and moisture decide how a material behaves over heat, and the part supplies both. That is why the part predicts the burn more reliably than the species does.

Follow the chain for a wood. Heartwood is dense and low in moisture → heat penetrates slowly → volatiles leave over a long window → the aroma arrives late and stays → the material anchors a blend.

Now the same chain for a petal. A petal is thin, wet and delicate → direct heat flash-vaporizes its surface oils → the light compounds scorch before they can travel → what reaches the nose is char and a ghost of the flower.

Resin sits between the two. It is solid but not fibrous → it melts rather than smoulders → the melt releases volatiles in a steady stream → the release is bright at first and heavy at the end.

Peel, bud and bark take a fourth path. They are dry and thin-walled → they give up their oils fast → the note arrives immediately and fades quickly → they lift a composition at the top and rarely carry it.

A material's job follows from its structure, and its structure follows from the part.

## Why flowers are mostly accords

**Direct answer:** A flower's scent is a thin oil held in fragile surface cells, and heat destroys it before it can be enjoyed. So the trade does one of two things: captures the oil first, or rebuilds the smell from other materials.

The capture route is expensive. Rose and jasmine can be extracted into absolutes, and those do appear in serious work — a large mass of petals yields a small mass of extract, and the harvest window is days, not months.

The rebuild route is the norm. An accord assembles the impression of a flower from other aromatic materials, and the result is stable, consistent and far cheaper. That is not a defect; it is the standard method, and a good accord can smell closer to a living flower than a mediocre absolute does.

The distinction that matters is method, not authenticity. "Is this real lotus?" is the wrong question. "Is this an extract or an accord?" is the right one, and it is answerable — a maker who uses an absolute generally says so, because the cost is a selling point.

## Four bands, one label

**Direct answer:** The part sorts the market into four bands — material, accord, component and structure — and each band tells you what kind of thing the label is selling.

**Material band.** Wood and resin names point at plant substance: sandalwood, agarwood, frankincense, benzoin. The question is species and grade.

**Accord band.** Flower names point at a reconstruction: jasmine, rose, lotus, osmanthus. The question is extract or accord.

**Component band.** Peel, bud and bark names point at a top note: citrus, clove, cassia. A stick described only as "citrus" is a lift with little else behind it.

**Structure band.** Makko and similar binders are neither scent nor filler — they are why a stick holds shape.

"Sandalwood" is a claim about material; "lotus" is a claim about impression. Neither is dishonest; treating the two as one kind of statement is where buyers get lost.

## The binder that carries no scent

**Direct answer:** Makko is powdered *Machilus thunbergii* bark, and its job is combustion, not fragrance. It binds the aromatic powders, keeps the stick straight and burns cleanly enough to disappear.

A stick is a composite. Fragrant powders — woods, resins, spices — do not hold together alone, and many burn badly unsupported. The binder supplies adhesion while wet and a steady ember when lit.

That is why "contains natural binder" is information rather than a confession. A stick without one is pressed under great pressure, dipped in a combustible carrier, or not a stick at all.

A useful judgment boundary: the binder is not where quality is decided. Quality lives in the fragrant fraction — which species, what grade, how fresh. A maker who lists the binder and the species is being transparent about both the structure and the substance.

## Common names drift; binomials do not

**Direct answer:** The same English word can point at plants in different families, so a common name carries no family, no part and no chemistry. The binomial carries all three.

Three examples cover most of the confusion. "Cedar" can mean a true *Cedrus* or the unrelated juniper sold as cedarwood in aromatic trade. "Cassia" can mean *Cinnamomum cassia* bark or drift toward other cinnamon species depending on the market. "Lotus" wanders between the aquatic *Nelumbo* and several unrelated plants depending on the tradition doing the naming.

The pattern is old and it is not going away, because common names travel with trade and translate badly. A buyer's defence is simple: find the binomial. *Santalum album* is unambiguous in a way that "sandalwood" is not, and *Aquilaria sinensis* is a specific tree in a way that "agarwood" is not.

There is a second trap inside the first. A label that names the genus but not the species — " *Boswellia* resin" — is honest and still incomplete, because the species decides the aroma. Genus is the first fork; species is the answer.

## The harvest calendar explains the price

**Direct answer:** Each part becomes fragrant material on its own schedule — decades for heartwood, a season for resin, days for flowers — and that schedule is the quiet reason the price tags differ so much.

| Part | Example | When it is taken | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heartwood | Sandalwood, agarwood | After decades of growth, or after wounding | Slow to form, deep and persistent, expensive |
| Gum-resin | Frankincense, myrrh | On a seasonal tapping cycle | Renewable annually, moderate cost |
| Flower | Jasmine, rose | At bloom, often picked by hand | Narrow window, low yield, costly extract |
| Peel, bud, bark | Citrus peel, clove, cassia | At maturity, often dried | Cheap, stable, bright |

Read the calendar as a cost model. Heartwood is a decades-long investment in a slow-growing tree, so the material is dear before anyone touches it. Resin is an annual crop from a wild or semi-managed stand, so it can be affordable. Flowers are a days-long harvest with a poor mass ratio, which is exactly why the market prefers accords. Peels and spices are agricultural by-products of a food crop, which is why they are cheap and why they smell familiar.

## Heartwood: the slow case

**Direct answer:** Sandalwood's aroma lives in the dense centre of the trunk and takes decades to develop, which is why the genuine material is costly and why so much "sandalwood" is something else.

*Santalum album* is hemiparasitic: it draws water and nutrients from neighbouring roots while also photosynthesising, which makes it slow and fussy to establish. The oil builds in the heartwood as the tree ages, so a young trunk carries little of value and an old one carries a great deal.

For a buyer the shape is familiar: expensive, finite, and easy to fake with pale woods or accords. The part is heartwood; the question is whether the part matches the name.

Agarwood arrives at a similar place by another route. The wood itself is unremarkable; the value is a resin produced in response to wounding, which is why the trade grades by darkness and density rather than by species alone.

## Resin: the seasonal case

**Direct answer:** Frankincense and myrrh are tapped from bark on a seasonal rhythm, and the timing of the cuts shapes both yield and grade.

The mechanism is a wound response. A cut severs resin ducts → the tree pushes a milky exudate to seal it → air takes the water and the lightest volatiles → the remainder hardens into a tear that is picked off by hand.

Season matters because sap movement follows the growing cycle. Cuts made when the tree is active yield more; a first pass over a fresh wound is thin and often darker; later passes yield the thicker, cleaner tears that grade higher. Over-cut a stand and the trees weaken, which shows up as lower yield in following years rather than in the current price.

The practical result for a buyer: resin is the affordable classical material, and its quality varies with weather and harvest discipline more than with any manufacturing step.

## Peel, bud, bark: fast top notes

**Direct answer:** Citrus peel, clove buds and cassia bark are dry, thin-walled and loaded with volatile oil near the surface, so they release quickly and fade quickly.

陈皮 — aged tangerine peel — is the classical Chinese version of the citrus note, and its character rounds off with age as the oil oxidizes. Clove buds carry eugenol, a heavy phenolic note that reads warm and dental and swamps anything beside it in quantity. Cassia bark brings cinnamon's heat with less of the floral softness that true cinnamon carries.

None of these anchors a composition. Their role is the first minute — a bright opening before the heavier materials arrive. Used as a base they collapse under the weight.

## Matching the part to the job

**Direct answer:** Choose the part by the job you want done — anchor, body, lift or structure — and the material follows. Choosing by scent name first is how blends end up unbalanced.

| Job in the composition | Part to reach for | Why it works | Failure if overused |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchor the base | Heartwood | Dense, slow, persistent | Muddy, heavy, flat |
| Build the body | Resin | Melts steadily, fills the middle | Cloying, sooty |
| Lift the opening | Peel, bud, bark | Fast volatile release | Sharp, thin, one-note |
| Give a floral signature | Flower accord or absolute | Impression without scorching | Perfumed, synthetic edge |
| Hold it all together | Makko or another binder | Combustion and adhesion | Brittle sticks, uneven burn |

The table is the argument in one page. A composition needs at least one anchoring material, something in the middle, and a way to open. A stick built entirely from top-note materials smells loud for ninety seconds and then smells of nothing.

## Where the map stops

**Direct answer:** The part predicts the type of material and its behaviour. It does not grade quality, certify origin or guarantee that the material is present at all. *This is the author's editorial boundary, not a measured result.*

The misconception to correct is that a botanical map doubles as an authenticity test. "Sandalwood" predicts heartwood, slow burn and a creamy-woody profile; it does not tell you whether the powder is *Santalum album*, a cheaper *Santalum* species, or a pale wood under an accord.

What the map gives is a cheap first filter. A product claiming wood character at peel prices is worth a question, and a "lotus" stick priced like a real absolute invites the mirror question. For a buyer that is the honest use: the map narrows what to ask, and the asking remains.

## A three-step label check

**Direct answer:** Name the part, infer the band, then confirm the method. Three checks turn a label from a list of pleasant words into a prediction.

**Step one — name the part.** Wood, resin, flower, peel, bud, bark or binder. A name that implies no part is marketing, not information.

**Step two — infer the band.** Wood and resin point at material; flower at impression; peel, bud and bark at a component; makko at structure.

**Step three — confirm the method.** For a flower, ask extract or accord; for a wood or resin, ask species and origin; for a blend, ask what carries the base.

Run it on "sandalwood and lotus" and the label resolves into a heartwood material plus a floral impression, probably an accord, species unknown. More useful than the adjectives.

## FAQ

### Q: Where does the sandalwood scent actually come from?

The heartwood of *Santalum album* and a few relatives — not the leaves, flowers or outer bark. The oil builds in the dense centre of the trunk over decades, and that slow accumulation, plus the tree's hemiparasitic habit, is why genuine material costs what it does. Only the heartwood, milled or distilled, carries the note.

### Q: Are flower scents in incense real?

Usually not as flower material. Raw petals scorch under direct heat, so most floral sticks use an accord — a reconstruction built from other aromatics. Rose and jasmine can be captured as absolutes, and those appear in better work, but the everyday floral note is a rebuild. That is a method, not a fraud.

### Q: What part of the plant is frankincense?

A gum-resin exuded from *Boswellia* bark after cutting. It is a secretion, not a structural part, which is why the trade classes it with resins rather than woods. It hardens into tears sorted by size, color and cleanliness.

### Q: Why is makko listed as a plant source if it has no scent?

Because it is the one plant material whose job is structure. Powdered *Machilus thunbergii* bark binds the fragrant powders and burns steadily, and it is close to odourless in the finished stick. Listing it is a sign of a transparent formula, not a warning. See [makko and natural binders](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

### Q: Can one plant give more than one incense material?

Yes. A citrus tree gives peel for the bright note and flowers for neroli-type material in perfumery; a *Cinnamomum* gives bark and also leaf oil with a different profile. The part is the variable, not only the species — which is why a label naming just the plant is still incomplete.

### Q: Does a plant part tell me anything about quality?

Only about behaviour, not about grade. The part predicts density, burn speed and the general shape of the aroma. Whether the material is well grown, cleanly harvested and honestly labelled is a separate set of questions — species, origin, sorting and supplier.

## Summary

The map is a four-column shortcut: scent, plant, part, family. Read the part and you can predict density, burn speed and the rough shape of the aroma before anything is lit; read the family and you can place the material among its relatives.

What it cannot do is grade the material or certify the species. "Sandalwood" should be heartwood and should behave like one — which *Santalum* is in the box is another question. Keeping those jobs separate is the difference between using the map and trusting it too far.

The rest is method. Woods and resins anchor, flowers are rebuilt, peels and spices lift, binders hold. A composition that respects those roles burns as designed; one that ignores them smells of its loudest ingredient and nothing else.

**Direct answer:** Agarwood and frankincense are both premium aromatic materials, but they are different substances from different plants. Agarwood (沉香) is the resin-impregnated *heartwood* of *Aquilaria* trees — wood that turns fragrant only after injury — while frankincense (乳香) is a tapped *gum-resin* that *Boswellia* trees exude from scored bark. Agarwood is deep, sweet, and woody; frankincense is fresh, citrus, and resinous. In price and status they sit far apart: agarwood is the most prized and most heavily faked of all incense materials, while frankincense is comparatively abundant and rarely substituted.

**Key facts:** Agarwood is a condition of *Aquilaria* wood (Thymelaeaceae); frankincense is a *Boswellia* resin (Burseraceae). **Direct answer:** Both materials are loosely called "resins" in casual writing, but the word means different things in each case — agarwood's resin is trapped inside wood, while frankincense *is* the resin.

- **Agarwood (沉香)** is *wood*. Healthy *Aquilaria* wood is pale and nearly scentless; only after the tree is wounded — by insects, weather, or deliberate inoculation — does it saturate the damaged heartwood with resin over years or decades, darkening, densifying, and scenting the wood. "Agarwood" names a **condition**, not a species. See the [agarwood entity page](/ingredients/agarwood/).
- **Frankincense (乳香)** is *resin*. *Boswellia* trees are scored, and the tree exudes a milky resin that hardens into droplets ("tears") collected by hand. It is a gum-resin, not wood, graded by tear size, color, and clarity. See the [frankincense entity page](/ingredients/frankincense/).

The shared word is a false-friend. Agarwood's resin is inseparable from the wood that holds it; frankincense's resin is the material itself.

---## How Each One Forms: Two Different Mechanisms

**Direct answer:** The two materials form by opposite mechanisms — agarwood is the tree's internal wound response, frankincense is the tree's external exudate — which explains almost every difference in how they are processed and graded.

Agarwood's formation is a defense story:

tree is wounded or infected → the tree deposits resin into its own heartwood to seal the damage → resin accumulates over years → the wood darkens, densifies, and becomes fragrant → the resin-bearing heartwood is harvested and graded.

Frankincense's formation is a tapping story:

harvester scores the bark → the tree bleeds a milky resin to close the wound → the exudate hardens into pale tears at the cut → tears are collected by hand → graded by size, color, and clarity.

These two chains explain the pricing asymmetry that runs through the rest of the comparison: one material requires a slow internal transformation inside specific trees, the other is a renewable surface exudate. That asymmetry — not marketing — is why agarwood sits at the top of the incense market.

---## Botanical Source and Naming

**Direct answer:** Agarwood comes from *Aquilaria* species (Thymelaeaceae), chiefly *A. sinensis* in southern China and *A. malaccensis* in Southeast Asia. Frankincense comes from *Boswellia* species (Burseraceae), chiefly *B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, and *B. serrata*. Different families, different genera, different plant parts.

- **Agarwood** — genus *Aquilaria*, family Thymelaeaceae; roughly 15–20 tree species across South and Southeast Asia. The two names most often encountered are *Aquilaria sinensis* (白木香, southern China) and *Aquilaria malaccensis* (the main Southeast Asian commercial species, CITES Appendix II).
- **Frankincense** — genus *Boswellia*, family Burseraceae; small trees and shrubs of northeastern Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and India. The main commercial species are *B. sacra* (Arabia), *B. carterii* (the Horn of Africa), and *B. serrata* (India).

In Chinese, the names record their nature: 沉香 (chénxiāng) means "sinking fragrance" (the density test), and 乳香 (rǔxiāng) means "milk fragrance" (the milky resin).

---## Aroma: Deep Wood vs Bright Resin

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is woody, resinous, sweet, and complex, developing in stages as it warms — top note, body, dry-down. Frankincense is fresh, resinous, citrus over a warm, creamy, earthy base. Agarwood is deep and evolving; frankincense is bright and clean.

| | Agarwood (沉香) | Frankincense (乳香) |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Deep, layered, evolving | Fresh, bright, clean |
| Notes | Woody, resinous, sweet, complex | Citrus, resinous, earthy, creamy |
| Behavior on heat | Develops in stages (头香 / 本香 / 尾香) | Releases cleanly, melts and volatilizes |
| Role in a blend | Base note — anchors and deepens | Top note — lifts heavier woods and resins |

Because agarwood is a base and frankincense a top note, the two are **complements in the same blend, not competitors**: a small amount of frankincense can lift a heavy agarwood-centered formula, which is part of why both appear in classical compound incense (合香).

---## Heating Method: Why Neither Is Usually Burned

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is warmed over indirect heat (隔火焚香) so its layered aroma unfolds without a charred note; frankincense is heated on a charcoal disc or electric heater because resin melts and releases scent below open-flame combustion. Both are "heated, not burned," but for different reasons.

- **Agarwood** — the refined method rests chips above charcoal buried in ash, so the material releases scent without combusting. This preserves the top-note → body → dry-down progression that is the whole point of the material.
- **Frankincense** — tears are placed on a lit charcoal disc or warmed on an electric heater; the resin melts and volatilizes. An electric heater gives a cleaner, lower-smoke result.

The distinction matters in practice: anyone who burns agarwood on open flame loses most of its value, while frankincense needs enough heat to melt the tear but not so much that it chars.

---## Grades, Quality, and Adulteration

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is graded primarily by resin density — the sinking test (沉水 → 半沉半浮 → floating) — with 奇楠 (qínán) as the structurally distinct top grade, and it is among the most heavily adulterated aromatics. Frankincense is graded by tear size, color, and clarity, plus species and origin, and is comparatively rarely faked.

- **Agarwood quality** — resin content drives density, aroma intensity, and longevity. Origin, age, formation type, and aroma character all matter. The top grade, 奇楠, is structurally different ("oil wraps wood"). See the [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/).
- **Agarwood adulteration** — dyed white wood, foreign woods dressed up as agarwood, and oil-injected low-grade material are all common. Screening methods are in the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).
- **Frankincense quality** — larger, paler, cleaner tears rank higher; dust and mixed fragments are lower grade. Species sets the aroma profile (*B. sacra* classic, *B. carterii* clean and citrus, *B. serrata* earthier).
- **Frankincense substitution** — low. It is usually the real resin; the practical questions are species, grade, and form.

The shared lesson is the one the [comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) makes: the column that matters most is **substitution risk**, and here the two materials sit at opposite ends.

---## Traditional Use and Context

Frankincense is a classic ritual and fumigation resin, habitually paired with myrrh. Both appear in materia medica, but that language is traditional, not clinical.

- **Agarwood** — the premium anchor in 合香 and the material of refined appreciation; burned in ritual and meditation; recorded in classical materia medica for "moving qi" (traditional language, not clinical evidence).
- **Frankincense** — a classic heated resin in temple and church, ritual and fumigation; in Chinese materia medica it is classically paired with myrrh (没药).

The frankincense–myrrh pairing is the counterpart to agarwood's pairing with sandalwood — two different reference pairs in two different traditions.

## FAQ

### Q: Is agarwood the same as frankincense?
No. Agarwood (沉香) is resin-impregnated heartwood from *Aquilaria* trees (Thymelaeaceae); frankincense (乳香) is tapped gum-resin from *Boswellia* trees (Burseraceae). They are different substances from different plant families. The confusion comes from both being loosely called "resins" — but agarwood's resin is trapped inside wood, while frankincense *is* resin hardened into tears.

### Q: Which is more expensive, agarwood or frankincense?
Agarwood is far more expensive, and its top grade 奇楠 (qínán) sits at the top of the incense market. Frankincense is comparatively abundant and low-to-mid priced. The gap follows scarcity and substitution risk: agarwood forms slowly inside only some *Aquilaria* trees and is heavily faked, while frankincense is tapped more readily and rarely substituted.

### Q: Do agarwood and frankincense smell the same?
No. Agarwood is deep, woody, resinous, and sweet, developing in stages as it warms. Frankincense is fresh, citrus, resinous, and earthy, with a bright, clean character. Agarwood is a base note that anchors a blend; frankincense is a top note that lifts it.

### Q: How do you use agarwood vs frankincense in incense?
Both are heated rather than burned. Agarwood is warmed over indirect heat (隔火焚香) — chips above charcoal buried in ash — so its layered aroma unfolds without a charred note. Frankincense tears are placed on a lit charcoal disc or warmed on an electric heater, where the resin melts and releases its scent. An electric heater gives the cleanest, lowest-smoke result for frankincense.

### Q: Is frankincense or agarwood more likely to be faked?
Agarwood, by a wide margin. It is one of the most heavily adulterated aromatics — dyed white wood, foreign woods, and oil-injected low-grade material are all common. Frankincense is comparatively low-risk: it is usually the real resin, and the practical questions are species, grade, and form. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

### Q: What is the difference between frankincense and myrrh, and where does agarwood fit?
Frankincense and myrrh are both tapped gum-resins from the same family (Burseraceae) but different genera — *Boswellia* (fresh, citrus, creamy) vs *Commiphora* (bitter, balsamic, earthy). Agarwood is a different thing entirely: resin-saturated *Aquilaria* wood. Agarwood pairs with sandalwood in Chinese incense, while frankincense pairs with myrrh in both Chinese and Western tradition. See [myrrh](/ingredients/myrrh/) and the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).

## The Conservation Angle Most Comparisons Skip

**Direct answer:** A comparison that ends at aroma and price misses the conservation axis, where the two materials diverge sharply — agarwood is CITES-listed with wild populations depleted, while frankincense faces over-tapping pressure in parts of its range.

Agarwood sits in CITES Appendix II, which tracks and restricts trade in species threatened by it → wild *Aquilaria* trees have been heavily harvested for their resin → much of the modern supply is plantation-grown and inoculated → the legal and conservation status becomes part of what a buyer is really paying for and should verify. Frankincense is not CITES-listed, but the trees are wild or semi-managed → heavy tapping in parts of the Horn of Africa and Arabia has raised concerns about tree health and long-term yield → the sustainability question is about harvest pressure rather than legal restriction. The two materials occupy different conservation realities, and a conscientious buyer checks a different thing in each case: legality for agarwood, harvest pressure for frankincense.

## A Heating-Temperature Ladder for Each Material

**Direct answer:** Both materials want gentle heat, but at different temperatures and for different reasons — agarwood needs low, indirect warmth to stage its aroma, while frankincense needs enough heat to melt the tear without charring it.

| Heat level | Agarwood behavior | Frankincense behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Very low (barely warm) | Opens the top note slowly | Tear softens but barely releases |
| Low to medium | Unfolds top → body → dry-down | Melts and volatilizes cleanly |
| Medium high | Body note fully expressed | Releases fast; risk of scorching |
| Open flame | Scorches and ruins the staged aroma | Chars the resin into acrid smoke |

The ladder shows the asymmetry in the "heated, not burned" advice. Agarwood's value lives in a staged progression that only gentle, indirect heat can reveal → too much heat collapses the stages into one charred note. Frankincense is simpler → it needs enough heat to melt the resin and carry the volatiles, but not so much that the tear burns. Two materials, two temperature ceilings, one shared rule: heat them, do not burn them.

## The Sink Test vs the Tear Grade: Two Different Ladders

**Direct answer:** Both materials are graded, but by unrelated ladders — agarwood by density (sinking), frankincense by tear appearance — and confusing the two ladders is a common buyer's error.

Agarwood's ladder runs on density: the resin-dense pieces sink (沉水), lighter pieces half-float, and low-resin wood floats → the sink test reads resin content, which tracks aroma intensity and value. Frankincense's ladder runs on appearance: larger, paler, cleaner tears rank above dust and mixed fragments → the grade reads purity and species character, not density. A buyer who applies the sink test to frankincense, or the tear grade to agarwood, is grading the wrong material by the wrong ruler. The ladder that fits the material is the one that catches its particular fraud — density fraud for agarwood, grade-mixing for frankincense.

## What "Sinking" and "Milk" Names Actually Record

**Direct answer:** The Chinese names of the two materials encode their physical natures — 沉香 records the density test, 乳香 records the milky resin — and reading the names recovers a layer of meaning the English loanwords drop.

## A Chain for Each Material's Scent Release

**Direct answer:** The two materials release their scent through different physical chains, and those chains are why one is a staged base note and the other a clean top note.

Agarwood, warmed gently: heat reaches the resin trapped in the wood → the resin's lighter volatiles release first → the heavier fractions follow as the temperature climbs → the scent unfolds in stages (top, body, dry-down) → the full character appears only across time. Frankincense, warmed: heat melts the solid tear → the melted resin volatilizes in a burst → the citrus and balsamic notes release cleanly and quickly → the scent arrives all at once rather than in stages. One is a slow unfolding, the other a clean arrival, and the chain is what separates a base note from a top note.

## A Grading Snapshot: What Each Ladder Rewards

**Direct answer:** The two grading systems reward opposite qualities — agarwood rewards density and depth, frankincense rewards clarity and cleanliness — which is why a high-grade agarwood and a high-grade frankincense look nothing alike.

A high-grade agarwood is dense, dark, and resin-heavy → it sinks and smells layered and deep. A high-grade frankincense is large, pale, and clean → it reads bright and citrus with no dust or mixed fragments. The ladders reward what each material is: agarwood is judged by how much resin the wood holds, frankincense by how pure the tears are. A buyer who expects a high grade to look the same way in both materials is applying one ladder to two different things.

## The Bottom Line, in One Sentence Each

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is wood made fragrant by injury — deep, scarce, and heavily faked; frankincense is resin tapped from a living tree — bright, abundant, and rarely substituted.

If you remember only two sentences from this comparison, keep those. They compress the origin, the aroma role, the scarcity, and the fraud risk into the two facts that matter. Everything else on this page — the heating methods, the grading ladders, the substitution risks — is an elaboration of those two sentences, and returning to them is the fastest way to keep the comparison straight.

## Where the Two Meet in One Room

**Direct answer:** In practice the two materials often share the same space and the same session — a heavy agarwood base with a frankincense lift — and that coexistence is the best final proof that the comparison is about complement, not competition.

Light a blend built on agarwood with a trace of frankincense and you experience the comparison directly: the frankincense opens the session with its clean citrus → the agarwood then rises through the body and lingers through the dry-down → the two occupy different moments of the same burn → the session reads as one unfolding scent rather than two competing ones. This is the practical version of everything the page argues: agarwood and frankincense are not rivals in a category but two voices in one register, and a buyer who understands the comparison can hear both.

## Why Agarwood Stages and Frankincense Arrives

**Direct answer:** The single deepest difference in how the two materials behave is a matter of phase — agarwood releases its scent from a solid matrix in stages, frankincense releases from a melted liquid in a single clean arrival — and that difference is why one is a base and the other a top.

Run the two mechanisms side by side. Agarwood on gentle heat: the flame must first travel into a dense, resin-soaked solid → the lightest volatiles leave first → the heavier fractions follow as the wood warms through → the scent unrolls in stages — top, body, dry-down. Frankincense on gentle heat: the solid tear melts first → the molten resin presents a liquid surface → the volatiles evaporate in one concentrated burst → the citrus-and-balsamic note arrives at once. One is a slow unfolding, the other a fast arrival, and everything else on this page — the heating methods, the blending roles, the grading — follows from that single physical contrast.

## A Matrix of the Forms and Their Jobs

**Direct answer:** The two materials reach the buyer in overlapping forms, but each form has a distinct job, and mismatching form to job is the most common beginner's slip on the way to the burner.

| Form | Agarwood | Frankincense | What the form is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chips / whole pieces | Indirect heating (隔火焚香) | — | Staging the full aroma |
| Tears | — | Charcoal disc or electric heater | Melting and volatilizing cleanly |
| Powder | Blending into sticks and pills | Blending into sticks and pills | Building a compound incense |
| Oil | Oud oil for fragrance | Essential oil for fragrance | A portable, concentrated scent |

The matrix shows the two materials as mirror images: agarwood's signature form is the chip, frankincense's is the tear, and the powder and oil forms are shared but serve blending and fragrance rather than heating. Read the form and you know how the seller expects the material to be used; buy the wrong form and you have a fine material for a job you never meant to do.

## A Judgment Boundary: Rarity Is Not a Virtue

**Direct answer:** A common error is to read agarwood's scarcity and price as proof that it is "better" — the honest boundary is that rarity is a fact about supply, not a verdict about scent, and the two must be kept apart.

Agarwood is rarer and dearer because it forms slowly inside only some trees and is heavily faked → scarcity and fraud, not aroma, set the price. Frankincense is abundant and cheap because it is tapped on a renewable cycle → abundance, not inferiority, sets its price. Neither fact tells you which scent you will prefer, and a buyer who lets the price tag make that decision has let the market substitute for the nose. My position: choose by role and aroma, and treat the price gap as a supply story you understand, not a ranking you obey. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## More Questions Answered

### Q: If I can only afford one, which should I start with?
That depends on the job, not the budget. If you want a deep, anchoring base that rewards slow, attentive heating, agarwood — but buy a small, verified amount from a seller who names the species, because it is the most-faked material in the category. If you want a bright, forgiving top note that is easy to heat and rarely faked, frankincense is the safer first purchase and the better learning tool. Most people who start with a cheap, unverified agarwood end up studying fakes; most people who start with frankincense end up studying the material itself.

## The Two Grading Ladders, Side by Side

**Direct answer:** Agarwood and frankincense are both graded, but by ladders that reward opposite things — agarwood rewards density and depth, frankincense rewards clarity and cleanliness — and a buyer who runs one ladder on the other material grades the wrong thing.

Agarwood's ladder is a density scale → the resin-rich pieces sink (沉水), lighter ones half-float, low-resin wood floats → the sink test reads resin content, which tracks aroma intensity and value. Frankincense's ladder is an appearance scale → larger, paler, cleaner tears rank above dust and mixed fragments → the grade reads purity and species character. The two ladders could not be more different: one measures how heavy the wood is, the other how clean the tears are. A buyer who drops frankincense in water, or squints at agarwood for "tear size," is applying a foreign ruler and will read nonsense. Match the ladder to the material and the grade snaps into focus.

## The Collector's Two Opposite Instincts

**Direct answer:** A collector approaches the two materials with opposite first instincts — agarwood is bought to be verified, frankincense is bought to be graded — and that single difference is the practical heart of the comparison.

With agarwood the first instinct is skepticism → the material is the most-faked in the category → the reflex is to verify species, origin, density, and warmed aroma before any price is discussed → trust is earned, never assumed. With frankincense the first instinct is sorting → the material is usually genuine → the reflex is to grade tears by size, color, clarity, and species → the question is which grade, not which truth. Opposite instincts, one shared spine: know the material's particular risk and aim your attention at it. For agarwood the risk is fraud, for frankincense the risk is grade variance, and a collector who inverts the two instincts pays for the mistake.

## Why One Material Rewards Patience and the Other Rewards Clarity

**Direct answer:** The two materials demand opposite virtues from their user — agarwood rewards patience, because its value only appears across time, while frankincense rewards a clean setup, because its value appears the moment the resin melts.

Run the two experiences side by side. Agarwood on indirect heat → the top note opens first, the body follows, the dry-down arrives last → the full character needs minutes to unfold → a user who rushes, who cranks the heat, collapses the whole progression into one charred note. Frankincense on a heater → the tear melts and releases in one clean, bright burst → the scent is there immediately → a user who over-heats only adds an acrid edge to a note that was already complete. The two materials teach different habits: agarwood teaches you to wait, frankincense teaches you to prepare a gentle, even heat and then step back. Neither is harder; they are hard in different directions.

## The Substitution Question, Asked Honestly

**Direct answer:** The honest question about substitution is not "can I swap one for the other" — they are not substitutes — but "what fills the role each one plays when it is unavailable," and the answers are lopsided.

Agarwood's role is a deep, anchoring base → its nearest stand-in is sandalwood or a dense aged wood → the stand-in fills the base but loses the layered, resinous sweetness that made agarwood distinctive. Frankincense's role is a bright, lifting top → its nearest stand-in is a citrus note or sweetgum → the stand-in fills the lift but loses the balsamic body. The asymmetry matters: agarwood is far harder to imitate because its depth is rare, while frankincense's lift is easier to approximate. A blender who runs out of both faces two very different problems, and the honest answer is that one gap is far harder to paper over than the other.

## A Table of the Two Materials' Different Frauds

**Direct answer:** Both materials are sometimes sold dishonestly, but the fraud takes opposite forms — agarwood is faked by making cheap wood look like the real thing, frankincense is mislabeled by grade and species rather than faked outright.

| The dishonesty | Agarwood | Frankincense |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Oil-injected, dyed, or substituted wood | Lower grade or wrong species under a name |
| What it defeats | Density, color, and a quick sniff | Grade and species expectation |
| How you catch it | Warmed aroma, burn smoke, seller | Tear size, color, clarity, named species |
| How common | Very common | Relatively rare |

The table shows why the two need different defenses. Agarwood fraud is about the material itself being wrong, so the defense is sensory and chemical. Frankincense fraud is about the grade and species being overstated, so the defense is mostly visual and documentary. One is a lie about what it is, the other a lie about how good it is, and they call for different questions at the counter.

## The Two Materials' Histories, and What They Explain

**Direct answer:** The two materials carry different histories — agarwood a story of scarcity and reverence in East Asia, frankincense a story of trade and ritual across the Mediterranean and Arabia — and those histories explain why one reads as a collector's treasure and the other as a ceremonial staple.

Agarwood's history is one of rarity → the wound-formed wood was always scarce → it became the premium anchor of Chinese 合香 and a mark of refined appreciation → scarcity built reverence. Frankincense's history is one of abundance and trade → the tapped resin moved along ancient caravan and sea routes → it became the classic ritual and fumigation resin of temple and church, habitually paired with myrrh. The two histories produced two different relationships: one material was hoarded and graded for its depth, the other was burned freely for its lift. Reading the history recovers the cultural context that a bare botanical comparison leaves out, and it explains why the two feel so different even before they are lit.

## The Blender's View: How the Two Meet in One Formula

**Direct answer:** The two materials coexist in classical 合香 as a base and a top, and the blender's working rule — a heavy base, a light top — is what keeps them from canceling each other out.

In a blended formula, agarwood carries the deep, anchoring base → frankincense opens the top with its bright lift → the base holds the piece together across the whole burn, the top arrives first and fades → the result is a scent that unfolds rather than lands all at once. The ratio is the discipline: a heavy frankincense hand buries the agarwood, a heavy agarwood hand smothers the frankincense. This is the same 君臣佐使 logic that governs all classical 合香, applied to a wood-and-resin pair, and it is the practical reason the two are complements in a blend even though they are never substitutes for each other.

## Oud Oil, the Other Face of Agarwood

**Direct answer:** Agarwood reaches the market in two very different forms — the solid wood and a distilled oil (oud) — and the oil is worth understanding on its own because it behaves, grades, and is faked differently from the wood.

The wood and the oil are two faces of one material. The wood is heated → its resins release their scent gradually → the experience is staged and slow. The oil is produced by steam or hydro-distillation → the volatiles are driven out and condensed → the result is a concentrated liquid that carries the scent in a portable form. The two are graded differently: the wood by resin density and aroma on heat, the oil by its origin, its age, and the depth of its scent as it evolves on skin or on a heater. And they are faked differently — the wood by oil injection and dye, the oil by blending cheap fractions or a synthetic note into a thin base. A buyer who understands the wood but not the oil has understood half the agarwood story.

## The Geography of the Two Materials

**Direct answer:** The two materials come from different parts of the world, and the geography is not trivia — it sets the species, the harvest method, and the legal questions a buyer should ask.

Agarwood grows across South and Southeast Asia → the commercial species run from *Aquilaria sinensis* in southern China through *A. malaccensis* and its relatives in the tropics → the trade follows that range, with CITES listing shaping what is legal to move. Frankincense grows in a different belt → northeastern Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and India → *Boswellia sacra* in Arabia, *B. carterii* in the Horn of Africa, *B. serrata* in India → the trade follows the tapping season and the port. The geography is a buyer's first screen: a seller who cannot say where a material is from has already failed the provenance question, because the place is what pins down the species, the method, and the legality.

## How the Two Are Judged by Eye and Hand

**Direct answer:** Before any heat, a practiced buyer judges both materials by eye and hand — and the two are read by opposite physical cues, which is why the same habits do not transfer between them.

Agarwood is read by weight and darkness → a resin-dense piece feels heavy for its size and reads dark and oily → the hand weighs density, the eye reads resin content → the cue is heaviness. Frankincense is read by the tear → a good tear is large, pale, and clean, with no dust or mixed fragments → the hand feels the tears, the eye reads clarity → the cue is cleanness. The two are mirror opposites: one is prized for being heavy and dark, the other for being light and clear. A buyer who reaches for the same physical cue on both materials — expecting agarwood to be pale, or frankincense to be heavy — is reading each material with the other's habits. ## A Beginner's First Purchase, and Why It Should Be Frankincense

**Direct answer:** A beginner deciding between the two is usually better served by starting with frankincense — it is abundant, rarely faked, and forgiving to heat — while agarwood is a second purchase, made after the nose knows what to listen for.

Frankincense teaches without punishing. It is cheap and genuine → a beginner burns the real material from the first session → there is no fraud to navigate.

**Direct answer:** A gram of incense puts about 45 milligrams of particles into the air; cigarette tobacco is reported at about 10 milligrams. The four-to-one gap measures emission, not risk — and "natural" says what the paste holds, not what the ember gives off.

## Key facts

- Emission: ~45 mg of particles per gram burned versus ~10 mg/g for cigarettes (ALA; JNMA, PMC7654492) — fine solids, aldehydes, volatile organics, carbon monoxide, and PAHs (PMC9058426).
- Hanoi homes burning incense showed indoor PM2.5 about 120% higher — a room average near 201 µg/m³ and a one-minute peak near 825 µg/m³.
- Taiwan childhood exposure was associated with lower lung function; a Singapore cohort linked 20+ years of daily burning to roughly 12% more cardiovascular deaths (PMC8548258). These are associations, not proof of cause in any one person.
- Airflow is the strongest lever a household controls, and warming resin below ignition (隔火焚香) is the lowest-emission method in common use. No burning product is smoke-free, and none is claimed here to improve health.

## Where the 45 mg/g Figure Comes From

**Direct answer:** The figure measures emission — particle release per gram of incense set against particle release per gram of cigarette tobacco. Two independent sources publish the same pair. It is not a risk equivalence.

Start with the Lung Association's own note: some 45 milligrams of particles come off a gram of incense, which it calls four times a cigarette's yield. The JNMA review "Incense Stick: An Overlooked Source of Health Hazard" (PMC7654492) prints the identical pair of averages — 45 mg/g and 10 mg/g. PMC9058426 then widens the inventory to volatile organic compounds, aldehydes, carbon monoxide, and PAHs.

Read them as averages, not constants: product, binder, and laboratory all move the count. What survives is the ranking — gram for gram, incense emits heavily.

| What the comparison states | Incense | Cigarette | What the ratio cannot say |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle mass released per gram | ~45 mg/g | ~10 mg/g | How much reaches a lung |
| What is being measured | Material as it burns | Tobacco as it burns | Whether anyone draws it in |
| Status of the number | Published average | Published average | Anything about one person's risk |

Read the right-hand column: a per-gram ratio cannot speak to duration, room size, or how the smoke is inhaled, and those three decide what a number becomes in a room.

## What the Burn Puts Into the Air

**Direct answer:** Everything in the plume is a combustion product, which is why a costlier or more "natural" plant changes the aroma and leaves the chemistry untouched.

Follow the reaction: plant material and binder meet heat → oxygen runs short before the reaction completes → carbon that would have left as carbon dioxide departs as soot and fine solids instead, carrying partly oxidized gases → the visible haze and the invisible fraction are both residues of an unfinished burn. A stick is built to smoulder, so "unfinished" is the design.

| What is in the smoke | Family | Why a reader should care |
|---|---|---|
| PM2.5 | Fine particles | Small enough to bypass the nose and upper airway and lodge in the alveoli |
| Formaldehyde, acrolein | Aldehydes | Airway irritants; formaldehyde sits on the carcinogen list |
| Benzene, toluene, xylene | Volatile organics | Irritate eyes and airways; benzene is carcinogenic |
| Carbon monoxide | Gas | A starved-combustion product that occupies haemoglobin and crowds out oxygen |
| PAHs | Condensed hydrocarbons | Some members of this family are recognized carcinogens |

Nothing in that table is unusual for fire; a candle, a wood stove, and a hot pan produce overlapping families. The correction worth making is to the assumption that wood powder, resin, and plant binder burn cleanly because the ingredients sound familiar.

## Three Numbers People Confuse: Emission, Concentration, Exposure

**Direct answer:** Emission leaves the stick, concentration fills the room, and exposure is what a body takes in. A strong emitter in moving air can deliver a smaller dose than a weak one in a closed bedroom.

| Term | What it measures | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Emission | Particle and gas release per unit of material burned | The material, the binder, the core, the burn temperature |
| Concentration | Mass of pollutant per volume of room air at a moment | Emission rate, room volume, air turnover, time since ignition |
| Exposure | What a person present actually inhales | Concentration, time in the room, breathing rate |

Most disputes fail in the middle column. Air turnover — how many times an hour the room's volume is exchanged — sets the ceiling that concentration climbs toward. Add particles at a steady rate, remove a fraction of the air each hour, and the reading rises until removal matches input. That plateau is what lungs meet.

Which is why a heavily emitting stick in a cross-ventilated room can sit below a lightly emitting stick in a sealed bedroom. It is also why mitigation here targets the room rather than the label, and why [the ventilation guide](/blog/incense-ventilation-indoor-air/) puts airflow first.

## What the Observational Record Actually Shows

**Direct answer:** The record shows consistent, dose-shaped associations between heavy burning and respiratory or cardiovascular outcomes, and it cannot show that incense caused any individual case.

- **Hanoi, Vietnam.** Homes that burned incense measured indoor PM2.5 about 120% higher — a room average near 201 µg/m³, a one-minute peak near 825 µg/m³.
- **Taiwan.** Children in households burning daily showed lower lung function in work the American Lung Association cites.
- **Singapore.** A cohort that followed long-term daily home burning for 20+ years reported roughly 12% more cardiovascular deaths (PMC8548258).

*Observational* is the word that must travel with all three. Daily-burning households differ in income, cooking fuel, airflow, housing type, and occupation, and a cohort can adjust for only part of that. The pattern repeating across countries earns attention; an individual's risk does not follow from a group figure, which is why no verdict is offered.

The practical reading is about habit, not product. Treated as dose-response, the association makes frequency and airflow the levers that shift a household toward the low end.

## Ordering the Methods by What They Emit

**Direct answer:** The usual ways to enjoy incense form a ladder from almost no emission to heavy emission, and the rungs are set by combustion rather than by how fine the ingredients are.

| Method | Combustion? | Relative emission | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resin on an electric heater | No | Lowest | Needs a heater and a dish; scent is quiet and close |
| Seal-pattern powder (印香) | Smolders | Low to moderate | Short, controlled burn; needs a mould |
| Thin coreless stick | Smolders | Moderate | Less material per stick; softer throw |
| Bamboo-core stick | Smolders | High | Convenient and familiar; more mass burning |
| Charcoal disc plus resin | Yes, coal and resin | Highest | Strongest release, and the coal adds its own smoke |

The ladder is not a quality ranking. A charcoal session is a deliberate choice with a long history, and wanting that volume of scent is not a mistake. The ladder lets you choose the emission before lighting anything rather than discovering it afterwards. [The burners and tools guide](/blog/incense-burners-tools/) covers the hardware.

## Matching the Mitigation to the Pollutant

**Direct answer:** No single step clears the whole plume, because solids and gases leave a room by different routes — a filter catches particles, while dilution or adsorption is the only answer to the gas fraction.

| Step | Particles | Gases and VOCs | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open window or cross-draft | Yes | Yes | Replaces contaminated air with outdoor air |
| Exhaust fan near the burner | Yes | Yes | Removes the mix before it spreads |
| Electric heater instead of a flame | Prevents | Prevents | Keeps material below ignition, so pollutants never form |
| HEPA purifier | Yes | Only with a carbon stage | Traps solids on fiber; gases pass through |
| Thinner, coreless sticks | Reduces | Reduces | Less mass burning per minute |
| Shorter, less frequent sessions | Reduces total | Reduces total | Lowers the accumulated dose |

The HEPA row needs the caveat marketing omits. A mechanical filter is good at fine solids and indifferent to molecules, so a unit without an activated-carbon stage can clear the haze while formaldehyde, benzene, and carbon monoxide stay in the air. That is not a case against purifiers; it is a case for running one after a burn rather than instead of opening a window.

## The Boundary: Lower Smoke Is Not Clean Air

**Direct answer:** A thinner stick or a warmed resin lowers the amount you breathe, and a lower amount is still an amount — "less" is not "none", and this page will not blur the two.

Reduction deserves credit without being renamed. Moving from a charcoal-heavy evening to a thin coreless stick twice a week is a real change in exposure; "harm-free" overstates the evidence, and "pointless" understates it. The honest word is directional: the load fell, and direction is what a household can act on.

Equipment claims need the same discipline. A purifier lowers concentration after the fact without changing the fact of combustion; a "low-smoke" label records a manufacturing choice without certifying the air. Neither point argues against the product — both argue for keeping other levers in play. *The author's view, not a medical claim.*

## Reading a Study Figure Before Trusting It

**Direct answer:** Four details decide whether a published PM figure applies to your room: volume, burn duration, airflow, and whether the number is a peak or a mean.

| Detail | Why it changes the reading | Question to ask of any headline |
|---|---|---|
| Room volume | A smaller volume reaches a higher concentration from the same emission | Was this a bedroom or a temple hall? |
| Burn duration | A longer burn adds mass and extends the plateau | One stick, or a morning of sticks? |
| Ventilation | Faster turnover lowers the ceiling | Were doors and windows open? |
| Peak versus mean | A one-minute spike and a session average differ by multiples | Which one is being quoted? |

The Hanoi pair shows why. 825 µg/m³ is a one-minute peak; 201 µg/m³ is the average across the burn. Both are real and both come from one study; either alone tells half the story. Ask the four questions and a number can be placed rather than admired. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## The Shape of a Burn: Rising, Plateau, Decay

**Direct answer:** A room's particle reading does not rise in a straight line — it climbs while material burns, flattens if the burn lasts long enough, and falls after the ember dies, with the worst minutes usually near the end.

Trace one stick: ignition starts adding solids → removal by leakage and airflow is still working on clean air, so the reading climbs → the climb slows as removal catches up → if the burn lasts, input and removal balance and the reading plateaus → the ember dies, input stops, the curve turns down. The decay half belongs to the room rather than the product, which is why one stick leaves two different afterlives in two different rooms.

Timing has a use. People open a window at lighting and close it when the smell fades — roughly when the particle curve is still falling. Airflow through the decay phase does more than any act at ignition; an extractor left running twenty minutes after the ember dies outlasts a wide-open window for the first two.

## What the Instrument Measures Changes the Number

**Direct answer:** Two instruments in one room can report different figures and both be right, because one weighs the mass it collected and the other estimates mass from scattered light.

| Method | What it records | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravimetric filter | Mass caught on a weighed filter over a sampling period | Direct mass, defensible in papers and in court | Slow; an average rather than a moment |
| Optical counter | Light scattered by airborne particles, converted to a mass estimate | Real-time, portable, shows the shape of a burn | Sensitive to particle type and humidity |
| Condensation particle counter | Number of particles above a size threshold | Catches the ultrafine fraction | Counts, not mass; needs conversion |

A real-time monitor and a laboratory filter are not interchangeable, and comparing them across studies creates contradictions that are really differences of method. A shelf display reading µg/m³ is almost certainly optical: its figure is an estimate calibrated against a reference, good for a trend and weaker as an absolute.

A spike on a consumer monitor says something changed, not that a diagnosis has arrived. Reading a display as medical evidence is the same error as reading silence as clean air. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## Who Is Standing in the Room

**Direct answer:** The same burn is a different event depending on who is breathing, and occupancy is what turns an emission figure into something a household should plan around.

A stick in an empty room adds particles to air nobody is using; beside a sleeping infant or someone with asthma, the same stick reaches lungs that will process them. Material and concentration are unchanged; exposure is not.

| Who is present | Sensible posture | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| Nobody, room airing afterward | Lowest concern | Ventilate after the burn and before the room is reused |
| Healthy adults, window open | Ordinary use | Keep airflow through the burn |
| Children or older adults present | Higher caution | Burn less, ventilate more, or move the burn outdoors |
| Anyone with a respiratory condition | Highest caution | Skip the burn in the shared room; do not treat scent as therapy |

Two boundaries hold the table. No medical claim is made about any group; one concentration lands on bodies of different size and airway sensitivity, which argues for caution rather than diagnosis. And "lowest concern" does not mean the room is clean afterwards — it means nobody was breathing while the air was worst. [The pets and sensitivities page](/blog/incense-pets-sensitivities/) applies the same logic to animals, whose airways and weight put them nearer the sensitive end than most owners expect.

## Sizing the Fix: Airflow Numbers That Actually Apply

**Direct answer:** Device claims become judgeable once converted into air changes per hour for your own room, because a purifier's rating and a fan's rating are measured under conditions no bedroom reproduces.

| Device | Rated airflow | Room of 1,000 ft³ | Changes per hour | What it actually clears |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Window exhaust fan | 120 CFM | 1,000 ft³ | ~7 | Whole plume, solids and gases |
| Kitchen hood, ducted outdoors | 300 CFM | 1,000 ft³ | ~18 | Plume at the source, both fractions |
| HEPA console | 200 CFM clean-air delivery | 1,000 ft³ | ~12 | Solids; gases need a carbon bed |
| Ceiling fan, no outlet | 0 net | 1,000 ft³ | ~0 | Mixes the air, removes nothing |

Divide rated airflow by room volume, multiply by sixty, and you have the fourth column. The arithmetic is rough — furniture, door gaps, and duct losses take a cut — but it ranks hardware honestly. A ducted hood beats every portable appliance because it removes the plume before it spreads, and it alone also expels carbon monoxide. Recirculating filters cannot, whatever their CADR figure says.

Two questions sort a purifier quickly: how many grams of carbon media does it hold, and is that media impregnated for polar gases. A thin carbon fleece saturates in weeks and then releases what it captured — worse than no carbon stage at all. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## What a Room Tells You Before You Light

**Direct answer:** Most of the exposure decision is settled before the match is struck, because volume, openings, and air direction set the ceiling the smoke will reach.

Start with volume: floor area times ceiling height. A 12 by 14 foot bedroom with an eight-foot ceiling holds about 1,300 cubic feet, the figure every airflow rating is quoted against. Then count openings. One window gives dilution but not exchange, since air entering and leaving through the same gap mostly mixes at the frame; two openings on opposite sides — a window and a hallway door, or a window and a bathroom fan — give a path, and a path is what replaces air.

Direction counts as much as size. A kitchen or bathroom extractor pulls air toward itself and leaves slight negative pressure, so a stick burned near it clears faster than one across the hall. A window on the windward wall pushes air in; on the leeward wall it pulls air out.

Two mistakes follow from missing the path: opening a window in a closed room and expecting the smoke to leave (it dilutes, then drifts back), and running a purifier in a sealed room (it processes only the air it receives, and the gases it cannot capture keep circulating). Fix the path first, then add equipment. [The ventilation guide](/blog/incense-ventilation-indoor-air/) covers the layout room by room.

## The Method Names, in Chinese and in Practice

**Direct answer:** The low-emission end of the ladder has its own vocabulary, and the terms make it easier to ask for the right hardware instead of describing a smell.

| Term | Pinyin | What it names | Where it sits on emission |
|---|---|---|---|
| 隔火焚香 | gé huǒ fén xiāng | Resin warmed over a heat source with a barrier between flame and material | Lowest — no combustion |
| 电熏炉 | diàn xūn lú | Electric warmer, usually with an adjustable plate | Lowest, and the most repeatable |
| 香篆 / 印香 | xiāng zhuàn / yìn xiāng | Powder pressed into a seal pattern and lit at one end | Low to moderate, short burn |
| 线香 | xiàn xiāng | Extruded stick, with or without a bamboo core | Moderate to high depending on mass |
| 盘香 | pán xiāng | Coil, burned for hours | High cumulative output |
| 埋炭 | mái tàn | Buried charcoal bed under ash, used to heat resin | High — the coal burns too |

Vocabulary also solves a buying problem. Ask for a 电熏炉 and you will see a temperature-controlled warmer; ask for an "incense heater" and you may get a charcoal burner with a metal screen, a device at the far end of the ladder. ## FAQ

### Q: Is burning incense bad for you?

It adds measurable pollution to indoor air, and whether that becomes harm depends on the dose. Per gram the output is heavy — roughly four times a cigarette's, plus a gas fraction. Per session, most people breathe a dilute version across a room rather than a direct draw. Frequency and airflow decide where a household lands.

### Q: Is "natural" incense cleaner to burn?

The ingredient story and the exhaust are separate questions. Natural material combusts, and combustion makes the fine solids and gases. A wood-powder stick with plant binder goes through the same chemistry as one with synthetic additives. The box describes the paste, not the plume.

### Q: Which method puts the least particulate into a room?

Heating resin on an electric warmer, the 隔火焚香 approach, keeps the material under its ignition point and produces volatiles instead of combustion products. Charcoal discs sit at the far end because the coal is itself a fuel.

### Q: Is quitting the only sensible option?

No. The evidence supports management rather than abstinence: keep air moving during and after the burn, avoid long sessions in sealed bedrooms, choose a lower-output method when air quality matters, and filter the residue with real carbon media. None of it is a licence to burn more.

### Q: How can I tell whether my room is actually clearing?

Watch the air, not the clock. If the haze thins within minutes and the room stops smelling of smoke soon after the ember dies, turnover is working. If the smell sits for hours, or returns when a door opens, the room has little exchange and needs an outlet — a far-side window, an exhaust fan, or a door left ajar.

**Direct answer:** Frankincense and myrrh are both aromatic gum-resins from the same plant family (Burseraceae), but from different genera — and they smell and behave quite differently. Frankincense (乳香) is the resin of *Boswellia* trees, fresh, citrus-bright, and creamy; myrrh (没药) is the resin of *Commiphora* trees, bitter, balsamic, and earthy. They have traveled together for millennia — classically paired in both Chinese materia medica and Western religious tradition — but one lifts and brightens a blend, while the other deepens and grounds it.

**Key facts:** Frankincense is *Boswellia* gum-resin; myrrh is *Commiphora* gum-resin. Both melt under gentle heat rather than burning like wood, and both are comparatively low substitution risk — usually the real resin, though species and grade still vary. Neither is "better"; they are the classic resin pair, complementary rather than interchangeable.

## What Each Resin Actually Is

**Direct answer:** Frankincense is *Boswellia* gum-resin, fresh and citrus-bright; myrrh is *Commiphora* gum-resin, bitter and balsamic. Same family, different genera, different aroma.

- **Frankincense (乳香)** is the gum-resin of *Boswellia* trees, chiefly *B. sacra* (Arabia), *B. carterii* (the Horn of Africa), and *B. serrata* (India). The tree is scored, and the milky resin hardens into pale "tears" collected by hand. See the [frankincense entity page](/ingredients/frankincense/).
- **Myrrh (没药)** is the gum-resin of *Commiphora* trees, classically *Commiphora myrrha*. The bark is wounded, and the resin hardens into reddish-brown tears or irregular lumps. See the [myrrh entity page](/ingredients/myrrh/).

Because both are gum-resins from the same plant family, they are often named together — but the genus is where the difference begins.

## Why Both Are Melted, Not Flamed

**Direct answer:** Both resins are heated on a charcoal disc or electric heater rather than burned on open flame, because resin melts and volatilizes below the point of combustion. An electric heater gives a cleaner, lower-smoke result for both.

The mechanism is shared, and it is the same reason wood and resin are heated differently:

heat reaches the resin tear → the gum-resin softens and liquefies → volatile terpenes flash off below combustion → the aroma releases without the charred note of an open flame.

- **Frankincense** — tears are placed on a lit charcoal disc or warmed on an electric heater; the resin melts and releases a clean, bright scent.
- **Myrrh** — the same method applies; because myrrh is heavier, it rewards smaller pieces and gentler heat to avoid a harsh, overheated note.

## Botanical Source and Naming

**Direct answer:** Both resins come from the family Burseraceae — frankincense from *Boswellia* spp., myrrh from *Commiphora* spp. Their Chinese names record their nature: 乳香 (rǔxiāng) means "milk fragrance," and 没药 (mòyào) is a transliteration of the imported resin's name.

- **Frankincense** — *Boswellia*, small trees and shrubs of northeastern Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and India. The main commercial species are *B. sacra* (Oman/Yemen), *B. carterii* (Somalia), and *B. serrata* (India).
- **Myrrh** — *Commiphora*, shrubs and small trees of the same arid belt (the Horn of Africa and southern Arabia). *Commiphora myrrha* is the classical source, with other *Commiphora* resins (opopanax, guggul, bdellium) traded under related names.

In Chinese, frankincense (乳香) and myrrh (没药) are habitually named together as a matched pair — a pairing that also runs through Western religious and perfumery tradition.

## Aroma: Bright vs Bitter

**Direct answer:** Frankincense is fresh, resinous, and slightly citrus (lemony) over a warm, creamy, earthy base. Myrrh is warm, balsamic, and earthy with a bitter, slightly medicinal edge — darker and heavier. Frankincense lifts a blend; myrrh grounds it.

| | Frankincense (乳香) | Myrrh (没药) |
|---|---|---|
| Genus | *Boswellia* | *Commiphora* |
| Character | Fresh, bright, clean | Dark, heavy, grounding |
| Notes | Citrus, resinous, earthy, creamy | Bitter, balsamic, earthy, slightly medicinal |
| Role in a blend | Top note — lifts heavier woods and resins | Depth note — deepens and anchors |

Because one is bright and the other dark, they are **complements in the same blend, not competitors**: frankincense prevents myrrh from feeling too heavy or medicinal, and myrrh gives frankincense a grounding depth. This is exactly why the two have traveled together through history.

## Traditional Use and Religious Context

**Direct answer:** Both resins have a long record of religious, ritual, and fumigation use — frankincense bright and ceremonial, myrrh deep and funerary. In Chinese materia medica they are classically paired, and the same pairing runs through Western religious tradition. That language is traditional, not clinical.

- **Frankincense** — burned in temple and church, used in ritual and fumigation, and recorded in Chinese materia medica as classically paired with myrrh.
- **Myrrh** — one of the oldest traded resins, with ritual, funerary, and oral-use history across multiple traditions; in Chinese materia medica it is classically paired with frankincense.

**Historical Record:** the frankincense–myrrh pairing is documented in Chinese materia medica and in Western religious tradition. **Scientific Evidence:** frankincense's boswellic acids are the subject of laboratory research on inflammation, and there is some modern research on myrrh (e.g., mouthwash use) — but medicinal claims remain traditional or preliminary, not established clinical fact.

## Regional Use: The Middle Eastern Context

Myrrh shares the region's resin heritage but plays a smaller, more specific role. See the [Middle Eastern incense guide](/blog/middle-eastern-incense/).

Myrrh, from the same producing belt, shares the regional resin heritage — the two resins have always traveled the same trade routes, which is how both reached China as imports.

## Grades, Quality, and Substitution

**Direct answer:** Frankincense is graded by tear size, color, and clarity, plus species and origin; myrrh is graded the same way, with clean, well-formed reddish-brown tears ranking higher. Both are low substitution risk — usually the real resin — though *Commiphora* relatives (opopanax, guggul) can be confused with myrrh.

- **Frankincense quality** — larger, paler, cleaner tears rank higher; dust and mixed fragments are lower grade. Species sets the aroma profile (*B. sacra* classic, *B. carterii* clean and citrus, *B. serrata* earthier).
- **Myrrh quality** — larger, well-formed, reddish-brown tears with little dust or bark debris grade higher. *Commiphora myrrha* is the reference; other *Commiphora* resins have their own profiles.
- **Substitution** — both are comparatively low-risk: usually the real resin. The practical questions for myrrh are *which Commiphora* (opopanax, guggul, and bdellium can be confused with it); for frankincense, *which Boswellia species*.

The shared lesson is the one the [comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) makes: among the classical materials, these two sit at the low end of substitution risk.

## How to Verify Each Resin

**Direct answer:** For frankincense, pin down species and origin, look for whole pale clean tears over dust, and confirm the form. For myrrh, do the same — species, clean reddish-brown tears over dust, and form. Both reward a label that names the species.

- **Frankincense** — *B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, or *B. serrata*; whole, pale, clean tears over dust; form (tears for heating, powder for blending, oil for fragrance). See the [frankincense entity page](/ingredients/frankincense/).
- **Myrrh** — *Commiphora myrrha* and which region; whole, clean, reddish-brown tears over dust; form (tears, powder, or oil). Dosing matters in blending — myrrh's bitterness means a little goes a long way.

## How Tapping Turns Sap Into Tears

**Direct answer:** Both resins begin as a wound response — a living tree bleeds resin to seal a cut, and the same chemistry that seals the wound is what dries into the tears a buyer later weighs.

The chain is one continuous process: a harvester scores the bark → the tree pushes a milky oleoresin out of severed resin ducts to plug the opening → the exudate, exposed to air, loses its volatile water and the lightest terpenes → oxygen cross-links the remaining sap molecules → the pool stiffens and darkens into a hardened tear → a second and third pass over the same wound, days apart, collects the successive beads. Frankincense runs paler and more citrus because its sap carries a high share of light monoterpenes; myrrh hardens darker and more bitter because its exudate holds heavier, more oxidized resins. The tear you finally buy is a dried wound-dressing, which is why size, color, and cleanliness track the harvesting more than any hidden virtue of the tree.

That wound-response origin also explains the grade logic. A fresh, unoxidized tear is pale and clean; a tear that sat through rain, dust, or repeated re-tapping is darker, dirtier, and heavier with bark debris. None of that is fraud — it is the same resin at a different point on the oxidation curve, and the curve is what a buyer reads when they sort tears by color.

## The Species Spread: Not All Frankincense Smells the Same

**Direct answer:** "Frankincense" is four or five species wearing one name, and the difference between them — lemon, pine, lime, or smoky — is the single most useful thing to pin down before buying.

| Species | Region | Aroma lean | Typical tears |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Boswellia sacra* | Oman, Yemen | Clean citrus, slightly green | Large, pale, prized |
| *Boswellia carterii* | Somalia, Horn of Africa | Bright lemon, crisp | Clean white-to-amber |
| *Boswellia serrata* | India | Earthy, woody, less citrus | Darker, grittier |
| *Boswellia papyrifera* | Ethiopia, Eritrea | Sweet, piney, resinous | Amber, sticky |

The table is the honest version of "which frankincense." A label that says only "frankincense" has told you the family, not the fragrance — the same gap as a bottle labeled only "red wine." For myrrh the equivalent split runs across *Commiphora* species, where *C. myrrha* proper sits against opopanax (sweeter), guggul (balsamic, used in incense and traditional preparations), and bdellium (lighter, sometimes called "false myrrh"). Name the species, then compare aromas — the reverse order is a guessing game.

## What the Resin Does on the Burner, Second by Second

**Direct answer:** A resin tear does not smell all at once — it melts, then fractionates, and the aroma a nose catches in minute one is not the aroma it catches in minute ten.

The sequence runs on a temperature ramp: the tear sits on the charcoal or heater → surface heat melts the outer layer first → the lightest monoterpenes (the lemon, the pine) evaporate immediately → as the melt deepens, the mid-weight compounds follow → the heaviest, most resinous fraction releases last, just before the residue chars → the final note is darker and smokier than the first. That is why a burner "blooms" a fragrance instead of shouting it — the material stratifies by volatility as it warms, and the nose hears the layers in order. Myrrh, being heavier, takes longer to open and rewards smaller pieces; frankincense opens fast and bright and then thins. The practical rule follows from the chain: heat gently, use small pieces, and judge the resin by its full arc, not its first five seconds.

## How to Tell a Good Tear From a Dusty One, By Eye

**Direct answer:** Resin grade is read on the surface — tear size, color, and how much bark and dust is mixed in — and the eye test is faster and more honest than any label claim.

For frankincense, the visual sort runs: large, pale, clean tears with a slightly waxy or "frosted" surface grade highest → smaller, amber or greenish tears grade middle → dust, mixed fragments, and bark-embedded pieces grade lowest. Color tracks oxidation: the paler the tear, the less time it spent exposed after tapping. For myrrh, the same logic applies in darker tones: large, well-formed, reddish-brown tears with a smooth, almost glassy fracture rank above dusty, crumbled, bark-heavy material. Neither resin should smell flat or "burnt" when cold, and a clean tear should snap or crumble with a resinous, not woody, feel. The eye is not a lab, but it catches the two things that matter most at purchase: grade and cleanliness — and both are visible before you ever light a match.

## The "Biblical Resin" Label Flattens Two Commercial Products

**Direct answer:** The common framing — "two resins of the nativity" — turns a pair of live commercial commodities into a single museum artifact, and the buyer who accepts that framing skips the question that actually matters: which genus, which species, which grade.

The misconception to correct is that these are interchangeable relics. They are not relics and not interchangeable — one is a bright top note, the other a grounding depth note, and each splits into several species and grades with real price and aroma consequences. The judgment boundary I would draw: a buyer needs the genus and species on the label, not a verse reference — "frankincense" is *Boswellia*, "myrrh" is *Commiphora*, and both names hide a species spread that changes the scent. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

The practical meaning for a buyer or collector: before you pay a premium, ask which *Boswellia* or which *Commiphora* — because the price of a pale *B. sacra* tear and a gritty *B. serrata* fragment are not the same, and neither is the smell. Treat the biblical pairing as history, and the genus-species label as the purchase tool.

## Why Cold Resin Has No Scent

**Direct answer:** A frankincense or myrrh tear in the hand is nearly silent because its aroma molecules are locked inside a solid — heat is what grants them the energy to escape, which is the whole reason these resins are warmed, not merely displayed.

The chain: a resin tear is a glassy solid, a matrix that traps its volatile compounds in place → at room temperature those molecules vibrate but lack the energy to break free of the matrix → the tear smells faint, because almost nothing is reaching your nose → apply gentle heat → the added energy loosens the matrix and raises the vapor pressure of the volatiles → the molecules escape into the air in a steady stream → the aroma becomes vivid. It is the same principle as why a candle has no throw until it is lit: the scent is potential, stored, waiting for heat to spend it.

That stored-potential fact is also why resins last so long in a drawer and why they reward low, patient heat rather than a fast flame. A quick, hot burn spends the whole store in a flash; a low, steady warmth releases it gradually — which is the entire logic of the burner, and of heating over burning.

| Form | What it is | Best used with | Notes |

## Low Substitution Risk Is Not Zero Risk

**Direct answer:** These two resins are rarely faked outright, but "low substitution risk" is a statement about the category, not a warranty on any specific bag — and the genus confusion inside each name is where a careless buyer still loses.

The misconception to correct is that "usually the real resin" means "always, and always the right one." A bag labeled "myrrh" may be a genuine *Commiphora* resin that is not *C. myrrha* — opopanax, guggul, or bdellium — all real, all related, and all subtly different; a bag labeled "frankincense" may be a genuine *Boswellia* but the wrong species for the aroma you wanted. The judgment boundary: authenticity is mostly settled, but identity is not — the risk has moved from "is it resin" to "which resin," and that second question is where the label alone stops helping. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

For the buyer or collector, the meaning is to treat the genus as the starting point, not the finish. Ask which *Boswellia* or which *Commiphora*, and which species, before you treat a purchase as settled — because a real resin of the wrong species is still the wrong resin for your blend.

## The Harvest Calendar, and Why It Shapes the Grade

**Direct answer:** Frankincense and myrrh are not mined on demand — they are tapped on a seasonal cycle, and the timing of the tapping, plus how many passes a tree receives, sets both the yield and the quality of the tears that reach market.

The chain runs on the tree's annual rhythm: resin flows most readily in the season when the tree is actively transporting sap → tappers cut into the bark in a careful sequence of passes → the first pass yields a thin, pale, often watery resin → later passes on the same wound, days or weeks apart, yield the thicker, paler, more aromatic tears that grade highest → over-tapping in a single season stresses the tree and lowers both quality and future yield. A buyer who understands the calendar reads a pale, clean, well-formed tear as the product of patient, correctly timed tapping — and a dark, dirty, fragmented lot as the residue of rushed or excessive cutting.

The timing matters because these are wild and semi-managed trees, not plantations that can be scheduled at will. The quality signal is therefore partly a weather-and-season signal, which is why grade can shift from one year's harvest to the next on the same tree.

## What Is Actually in the Resin

**Direct answer:** Frankincense and myrrh are not single molecules but mixtures — frankincense is dominated by light monoterpenes plus boswellic acids, myrrh by heavier sesquiterpenes and a bitter resin fraction — and that chemical split is the physical reason one is bright and the other dark.

The chain links chemistry to the nose: frankincense's resin carries a high share of small, volatile monoterpenes (the pinene, the limonene) → those small molecules evaporate readily and read as fresh and citrus → its heavier boswellic acids stay behind in the residue → the result is a bright, clean throw over a faintly creamy base. Myrrh, by contrast, is richer in larger sesquiterpene and resin compounds → those heavier molecules evaporate more slowly and carry the bitter, balsamic, medicinal edge → the aroma is darker and longer in the room. The genera differ at the level of what compounds each tree's resin is built from, and everything a buyer smells follows from that.

This is descriptive chemistry, not a therapeutic claim: the compounds are named to explain aroma and behavior, not to promise any effect on the body.

## Over-Tapping Is the Sustainability Question Nobody Puts on the Label

**Direct answer:** The quiet risk behind both resins is not fraud but depletion — wild and semi-managed trees can be tapped too hard, and a buyer who cares about the long-term supply asks about harvesting practices, not just price.

The chain is ecological and slow: these trees grow in an arid belt and regenerate resin slowly → a tapper who makes too many cuts, or cuts too deep, weakens the tree → the tree produces less in later years, or dies → the wild stock, already slow to replenish, thins → the long-term supply and the grade both decline. Reports of over-tapping exist in parts of the range, and the honest position is that the sustainability of a given lot is a question for the supplier, not something the tear itself can tell you.

For a buyer or collector, the practical move is to ask the question directly: is this wild or semi-managed, and how is the harvesting managed? A supplier who can answer with specifics is offering more than a resin — they are offering a supply that might still exist in ten years.

## The Chemistry in Plain Compounds

**Direct answer:** The two resins differ at the level of named compounds — frankincense leans on light monoterpenes and a family of acids, myrrh on heavier sesquiterpenes and its own resin acids — and the names are the physical basis of everything else on this page.

The chain: frankincense's volatile fraction is dominated by monoterpenes such as α-pinene, α-thujene, limonene, and myrcene → these small molecules evaporate fast and read as fresh, piney, and citrus → the resin's non-volatile tail holds diterpenes (incensole and incensole acetate) and boswellic acids → those heavy molecules stay behind in the residue and never reach the nose as vapor. Myrrh flips the profile: its volatiles are mostly sesquiterpenes — furanodiene, curzerene, lindestrene among them → those larger molecules evaporate slowly and carry the bitter, balsamic, medicinal edge → its resin acids anchor the dark base. The chemical names are not trivia; they are the reason one resin is bright and the other dark.

This is descriptive chemistry of the material, not a claim about any effect on the body. The compounds are named to explain aroma and volatility, full stop.

## The Botanical Family Behind Both Resins

**Direct answer:** Frankincense and myrrh both belong to the Burseraceae, a resin-producing family of the arid tropics, and the genus — *Boswellia* or *Commiphora* — is the first fork in any accurate identification.

The chain: the family Burseraceae groups trees and shrubs that exude aromatic gum-resins → within it, the genus *Boswellia* holds the frankincense trees and the genus *Commiphora* holds the myrrh shrubs → species within each genus split the aroma and the grade → the Chinese names, 乳香 and 没药, sit on top of this two-genus structure. ## The Burn, Read as a Chain of Consequences

**Direct answer:** A resin tear's behavior on the burner is a chain — heat melts it, then volatilizes it, then chars the residue — and reading the chain is what tells you whether a tear is good, stale, or adulterated.

The chain: gentle heat softens the tear → the lightest volatiles flash off first, bright and clean → the heavier resin follows, darker and slower → what remains, if the heat climbs too high, chars into an acrid note → the whole arc, from first bloom to final char, is the resin's signature. A good tear walks that chain smoothly; a stale one opens flat, its top notes long since oxidized away; an adulterated one jumps straight to a burnt or perfumey note without ever blooming. The burner is a diagnostic instrument, not just a scent source.

That is why "how does it behave on heat" is the question that separates a real buyer from a casual one. The tear's aroma is the surface; its behavior on the chain is the substance, and the chain is where fakes and stale stock expose themselves.

## A Table: Reading the Burn, Step by Step

**Direct answer:** The table maps each stage of the burn to what it reveals, so the burner reads as a test rather than a ritual.

| Stage of the burn | What it should do | What it reveals if it does not |
|---|---|---|
| First bloom | Open bright and clean | Stale or oxidized top notes |

## Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming the two resins are interchangeable.** One lifts, the other grounds; they are complementary, not substitutes.
2. **Burning either on open flame.** That wastes their appeal — they should be melted under gentle heat.
3. **Skipping the genus check.** Frankincense is *Boswellia*; myrrh is *Commiphora* — and *Commiphora* relatives (opopanax, guggul, bdellium) can be sold under a loose "myrrh" label.
4. **Confusing "myrrh" with unrelated things.** "Myrrh beads" (an unrelated African tree) and the herb sweet cicely (*Myrrhis odorata*) are different things entirely.

## FAQ

### Q: Are frankincense and myrrh the same thing?
No. Both are gum-resins from the Burseraceae family, but from different genera. Frankincense (乳香) is the resin of *Boswellia* trees, fresh, citrus-bright, and creamy; myrrh (没药) is the resin of *Commiphora* trees, bitter, balsamic, and earthy. They are classically paired, but complementary rather than interchangeable.

### Q: Do frankincense and myrrh smell the same?
No. Frankincense is fresh, resinous, and slightly citrus (lemony) over a warm, creamy, earthy base — bright and clean. Myrrh is warm, balsamic, and earthy with a bitter, slightly medicinal edge — darker and heavier. Frankincense lifts a blend as a top note; myrrh grounds it as a depth note. They are often blended together precisely because they contrast.

### Q: How do you burn frankincense vs myrrh?
Both are melted under gentle heat rather than burned on open flame. Place the resin tears on a lit charcoal disc, or more gently on an electric incense heater, and the resin melts and releases its aroma. Small pieces work best, especially for myrrh, whose heavier character can burn harshly if overheated. An electric heater gives the cleanest, lowest-smoke result for both.

### Q: Which is more likely to be faked, frankincense or myrrh?
Neither is heavily faked — both are usually the real resin. The practical questions are species and grade, not authenticity. For frankincense, which *Boswellia* species (*B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, or *B. serrata*); for myrrh, which *Commiphora* — since related resins like opopanax, guggul, and bdellium can be sold under a loose "myrrh" name. See the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).

### Q: Why are frankincense and myrrh always mentioned together?
Because they are the classic resin pair, named together across Chinese materia medica and Western religious and perfumery tradition. Both are gum-resins from the same plant family (Burseraceae) and the same arid producing belt, and they traveled the same trade routes. Their aromas balance each other — frankincense's fresh brightness against myrrh's bitter-balsamic depth — so they are habitually blended and named as a matched set.

### Q: Is myrrh the same as opopanax?
No. Both are resins from *Commiphora* trees, but they are distinct materials. Opopanax, sometimes called "sweet myrrh," is sweeter and more balsamic-sweet with a slightly powdery character, while myrrh proper is bitter, earthy, and medicinal. The two come from different *Commiphora* species and play different roles — opopanax leans sweet and ambery, myrrh leans dark and resinous. Because both are called "myrrh" in some older texts, the terms are easy to confuse. See [myrrh](/ingredients/myrrh/).

### Q: What is the difference between frankincense essential oil and raw frankincense tears?
They are two different products from the same material. Raw tears are the hardened resin you heat on a charcoal or electric burner — the whole, unprocessed exudate, graded by tear size, color, and cleanliness. Frankincense essential oil is steam-distilled from those tears, concentrating the volatile fraction into a liquid that a diffuser can disperse but that a burner cannot heat as resin. The oil loses the heavier, resinous base and keeps the bright top notes; the tears give the full aroma arc when warmed. For incense and fumigation, tears are the working form; for a diffuser, the oil is the working form — pick the form that matches the tool you will actually use.

### Q: Do frankincense and myrrh melt away completely when heated?
Not to ash the way wood does. Resin is not plant fiber; it is a hardened oleoresin, so under gentle heat it liquefies and then volatilizes — the volatile portion drifts off as aroma, and a small sticky residue is what remains on the burner or the charcoal. There is no ember and no pile of ash, just a diminishing puddle. That is the practical difference between heating a resin and burning a wood: resin converts to vapor and a trace of residue, wood converts to smoke and ash. It is also why resins want a low, patient heat — too hot, and the puddle scorches into an acrid char before the aroma can leave.

### Q: Are there different grades within frankincense, and how do I tell them apart?
Yes — frankincense is graded by tear size, color, clarity, species, and origin, and the sort is visible. Large, pale, clean tears rank above small, dark, or dusty fragments; species then sets the aroma (*B. sacra* is the classic clean citrus, *B. serrata* earthier and grittier). Myrrh grades the same way in darker tones: large, well-formed reddish-brown tears over crumbled, bark-heavy material. The eye test — tear size, color, and how much dust and bark is mixed in — is the fastest honest read, and it usually tracks the price. When in doubt, ask for the species name on the label; grade without a species is only half a description.

### Q: Should I heat frankincense and myrrh together, or separately?
Either works, and they are classically paired, but heat them with an eye to their different speeds. Frankincense is lighter and opens fast and bright; myrrh is heavier and takes longer to bloom and can turn harsh if overheated. On the same burner, put the myrrh on the gentler edge of the heat or add it in smaller pieces, and let the frankincense carry the top. Blending them yourself is the whole tradition made practical — one lifts, one grounds — and the only caution is heat discipline, because the heavier resin is the one that punishes a rushed flame.

### Q: Why is myrrh more bitter than frankincense?
It comes down to what each resin is made of. Myrrh is rich in heavier sesquiterpene and resin compounds that evaporate slowly and carry a bitter, balsamic, slightly medicinal edge, while frankincense is dominated by light, volatile monoterpenes that read as fresh and citrus. The bitterness is a property of the resin's chemistry, not a flaw or a sign of poor quality — it is exactly the quality that makes myrrh a grounding, depth-giving note in a blend rather than a bright top note.

### Q: Should I store frankincense and myrrh together or separately?
They can share a drawer, but store each sealed against air and light, because both are resins that slowly oxidize and shed their volatiles when exposed. Keep them in airtight containers away from heat and direct sun, and keep the tears whole rather than pre-ground if you can — powder oxidizes faster and loses its throw sooner. There is no harm in storing the two side by side, since they are dry solids and will not contaminate each other; the real enemy is air and warmth, not proximity.

### Q: What is the difference between oleoresin, gum-resin, and plain resin?
They are three ways of describing what a tree exudes, and the difference is in what is mixed with the resin. Plain resin is the hardened, non-volatile portion. An oleoresin is resin plus a volatile oil — the oil carries the aroma, the resin carries the body. A gum-resin adds a third component: a water-soluble gum, which is what the Burseraceae family — frankincense and myrrh both — is known for. The distinction is definitional, but it explains why these two are described as "gum-resins" and why they behave as they do on a burner, melting and volatilizing rather than burning like wood.

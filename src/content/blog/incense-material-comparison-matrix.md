**Direct answer:** The seven classical materials — agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, borneol, benzoin, myrrh, and clove — separate cleanly along five axes: aroma, botanical source, relative price, primary use, and the risk that the label hides a different material.

**Key facts:** "Relative price" is a directional signal, never a price list, and the column that actually protects a buyer is substitution risk — the chance that the name on the tin is not the material inside it.

A useful way to read the matrix is column by column rather than row by row. Read the "how often it's faked" column top to bottom and you get the fraud landscape of the whole market in one glance; read any single row and you get one material's honest profile. The column-first read is the one that protects a buyer, and it is the reason the matrix exists.

---## Why "Resin" Is Not a Price Signal

**Direct answer:** Agarwood, frankincense, and myrrh all get lumped under "resin," but they sit at opposite ends of the market because agarwood is a heartwood that had to be wounded to form, while the other two are tapped gums.

The mechanism behind agarwood's price is a chain worth spelling out: a wild *Aquilaria* tree gets stressed or infected → it defends itself by saturating its heartwood with resin → that resin-rich wood is what people call agarwood → the process is slow and only some trees respond → supply stays scarce → price climbs → and high price invites fakes. Frankincense and myrrh, by contrast, are tapped from living trees on a regular cycle, so they are abundant and far cheaper. The word "resin" covers both cases, which is why the *which* resin matters enormously and "resin" alone tells you nothing about value.

The same word, two opposite economics. One "resin" forms only after a tree is wounded and fights back over years; the other two are collected like sap on a schedule. That single difference — wound-triggered versus tapped — explains more of the price spread across this entire list than any description of aroma ever could.

## A Second Cut: Same Family, Different Price

**Direct answer:** Within the "resin" family alone, the price gap is enormous, and it is explained entirely by how the material forms, not by how it smells.

| Material | How it forms | Why that sets the price |
|---|---|---|
| Agarwood (沉香) | Wound-triggered resin in heartwood, slowly, in some trees only | Scarce by nature — the supply is capped by biology |
| Frankincense (乳香) | Tapped gum from a living tree on a regular cycle | Abundant — the tree keeps producing |
| Myrrh (没药) | Tapped gum, same renewable cycle | Abundant — cheap and reliably available |

The three columns above are the honest answer to "why is agarwood a hundred times the price of myrrh." It is not that agarwood smells a hundred times better — smell is subjective. It is that biology makes agarwood scarce and harvesting makes frankincense and myrrh renewable. Scarcity, not fragrance, is the price driver, which is a distinction the marketing copy almost never makes.

## The Two Places Fakes Cluster (and One Subtle Trap)

**Direct answer:** Agarwood and sandalwood are where substitution concentrates — both are scarce, both are expensive, and both have cheap stand-ins — while borneol is a subtler trap because the synthetic and natural forms are the same molecule.

1. **Agarwood** — oil-injected fakes and unrelated woods sold under the name. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).
2. **Sandalwood** — other *Santalum* species and synthetic fragrance passed off as the prized *S. album*. See the [sandalwood guide](/blog/sandalwood-incense/) and the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

Borneol deserves its own note. Natural borneol (from *Dryobalanops*) and synthetic borneol (from turpentine or camphor) are chemically the same molecule, so a "natural" claim on borneol is a provenance and price question, not an aroma one — the two smell alike because they are alike. That is a different failure mode from agarwood, where the fake is a genuinely different material.

The two failure modes are worth keeping separate in your head, because they call for different defenses. A fake agarwood is a lie about *what* the material is — you can catch it by looking at the wood. A "natural" borneol may be a lie about *where* it came from — and you cannot catch that by looking or smelling, because the molecule is identical. One is a material substitution, the other a provenance substitution, and only the first is visible on the shelf.

## FAQ

### Q: Which of the seven costs the most?
Agarwood (沉香), and its top grade, kyara (奇楠), sits at the very top of the market — orders of magnitude above everything else on the list. The reason is biological, not aromatic: agarwood only forms when an *Aquilaria* tree is stressed into saturating its heartwood with resin, and that happens slowly, in only some trees, which caps the supply. Scarcity is also why it attracts the most fraud — oil-pumped wood and unrelated substitutes show up constantly. A high price tag is not, by itself, proof that you have the real thing; where it came from matters as much as what it cost.

### Q: How do I tell frankincense and myrrh apart?
Both are tapped gum resins, but they come from different trees and smell nothing alike. Frankincense (乳香) runs fresh, bright, and citrusy over a resin base, and it is usually used as a lifting, clarifying note. Myrrh (没药) runs bitter, earthy, and balsamic — deeper and more grounding — and it carries a long history of ceremonial use. Because both are tapped on a renewable cycle rather than formed by wounding, they are abundant and far cheaper than agarwood. They are also among the least-faked materials; what you buy is usually the genuine resin, though grades still vary.

### Q: Are borneol and camphor the same thing?
No, even though they share a cool, sharp, camphor-edged scent. Borneol (龙脑) is the traditional "snow" note in Chinese blends like 雪中春信, lending a cooling top note. Natural borneol is distilled from *Dryobalanops* resin, but a chemically identical synthetic is manufactured from turpentine or camphor — same molecule, so the only real difference is provenance and price, not smell. That makes "natural borneol" a claim to check rather than trust, which is a subtler problem than a frank fake: the synthetic is not a different material, it is the same one wearing a cheaper origin story.

### Q: Which material should I watch out for most when buying?
Agarwood. Genuine material is scarce and expensive, so the fraud follows the money: sellers inject oil into low-grade or unrelated wood to fake the resin content, or sell other woods entirely under the agarwood name. Sandalwood is the second hotspot — other *Santalum* species and synthetic fragrance oil get passed off as the prized *Santalum album*. Borneol is a quieter case, since its synthetic and natural forms are chemically identical. For all three, the strongest defense is buying from a source that names the species and the method.

### Q: What is the difference between Siam and Sumatra benzoin?
Both are genuine benzoin resin, but they come from different *Styrax* species and are graded differently. Siam benzoin (from *Styrax tonkinensis*) is the finer grade — sweeter, more vanilla-like, and cleaner in profile — so it commands a higher price. Sumatra benzoin (from *Styrax benzoin*) is more common and carries a slightly earthier or smokier edge. Both do the same job as a warm, sweet base and fixative; the choice is really a matter of quality and budget rather than authenticity. A reputable seller will label which grade is in the tin.

## How Substitution Follows the Price, Step by Step

**Direct answer:** Fraud is not random — it concentrates wherever the price-to-cost gap is widest, and the gap widens through a chain of scarcity that starts in the field.

Walk the chain for agarwood. A wild tree must first be wounded or infected → only a fraction of trees respond by depositing resin → the resin takes years to saturate the heartwood → harvest is slow and selective → supply stays far below demand → the price climbs → a wide gap opens between what a fake costs to make and what the real material sells for → forgers move in to capture that gap. The same chain, with a different trigger, explains sandalwood: the tree grows slowly and matures over decades → mature heartwood is scarce → demand for the prized *Santalum album* outstrips supply → the price gap invites cheaper *Santalum* species and synthetic oils. Where the chain is short and the gap narrow — frankincense and myrrh, tapped on a renewable cycle — fraud stays rare. Substitution is a map of scarcity, and the price column is just its shadow.

## A Grade-Ladder Matrix: Seven Materials, Three Tiers

**Direct answer:** If you sort the seven classics by what a buyer actually risks, three tiers emerge — and the tier, not the aroma, is what changes how you should shop.

| Tier | Materials | What you risk | How you should shop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scarce-and-faked | Agarwood, sandalwood, borneol | Wrong material or wrong provenance | Demand species + origin + method |
| Abundant-and-genuine | Frankincense, myrrh | Grade variance, not fraud | Choose by tear size and species |
| Cheap-and-blended | Benzoin, clove | Grade and origin confusion | Confirm the grade on the label |

The matrix reframes the usual "which smells best" question. The scarce-and-faked tier is where you spend your verification effort; the abundant tier is where you spend it on grade; the cheap tier is where a one-word label can silently swap Siam for Sumatra benzoin, or bud for bark. Shopping effort should track substitution risk, not price.

## The "Natural" Word on Borneol Is a Provenance Claim

**Direct answer:** A correction worth writing down: for borneol, "natural" is a claim about where the molecule came from, not about how it behaves — and it is a claim you cannot verify by looking or smelling.

Most people read "natural" as a quality mark. For borneol that reading breaks, because natural borneol (distilled from *Dryobalanops*) and synthetic borneol (made from turpentine or camphor) are the same molecule. They smell alike because they are alike. The only real difference is the supply chain and the price, and neither is visible on the shelf. My position: treat "natural borneol" the way you treat any provenance claim — it needs a supplier who can trace the material, and it is worth paying for only if that trace matters to you. The word "natural" here describes a history, not a substance. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## More Questions Answered

### Q: Should I ever pay more for a lower-substitution-risk material?
That frames it backward. Substitution risk tells you how hard to verify, not how good the material is. A low-risk frankincense can still be a cheap, dusty grade, and a high-risk agarwood, once verified, can be worth every cent. The rule is to spend your verification effort, not your money, in proportion to the risk: for agarwood and sandalwood you confirm species and origin before paying; for frankincense and myrrh you confirm grade and form. A high price tag is never a substitute for that check, and a low one is never a reason to skip it.

## What the Scent Column Cannot Do

**Direct answer:** Aroma descriptors are the friendliest part of any comparison and the least reliable — two people can smell the same agarwood chip and write different words, which is why the scent column should be read last.

A descriptor like "sweetly balsamic" is a pointer to a memory, not a measurement. It helps you recognize a material you have already met; it cannot settle a dispute about which material is in the tin. The columns that do real work are the objective ones — botanical source and substitution risk — because they survive disagreement. The practical order from the buyer's chair: read source and substitution risk first, price second, and let the aroma column arrive last as a confirmation of what you already decided on other grounds.

## Reading the Matrix Column by Column

**Direct answer:** The matrix rewards a column-first read, because each column is a different lens on the same seven materials — and three of the five columns will surprise you if you only ever read rows.

Reading the source column top to bottom gives you seven plants in one sweep and shows that two of the "seven classics" (benzoin and clove) are not woods or resins at all but a balsam and a bud. Reading the substitution column top to bottom draws the fraud map of the whole market. Reading the role column shows how the seven split into anchors, lifts, and accents. The price column alone, read in isolation, misleads, because it says nothing about why a number is high. The discipline is to let the column, not the row, carry the comparison — rows describe one material, columns describe the market.

## The Five Axes Are Not Equal

**Direct answer:** The matrix sorts the seven materials along five axes, but the axes do not carry equal weight — two of them are objective and durable, while three are soft and can drift with the market or the sniffer.

Split the five columns by what they can actually decide. Botanical source and substitution risk are objective → a species is a fact and a fake is a fact → these two survive argument. Relative price, aroma, and role are soft → price shifts with region and season, aroma is a subjective descriptor, and role changes with the blend → these three are guides, not verdicts. The practical rule: read the two hard columns first and let the three soft ones refine the picture. A comparison built only on soft columns — price and aroma — is a tasting note, not a decision tool, which is exactly the trap most comparison content falls into.

## What a "Fixative" Actually Does in the Matrix

**Direct answer:** Two of the seven — benzoin and, in some blends, clove — double as fixatives, and understanding that role explains why a material can be cheap and still sit at the heart of a formula.

A fixative does not add much scent of its own; it holds the other notes in place. Benzoin's sweet vanilla warmth slows the evaporation of the more volatile top notes → the blend's bright lift lasts longer → the whole piece reads fuller and rounder → a small, inexpensive dose of benzoin can extend a costly agarwood or sandalwood base. Clove works the same way in spice-forward blends. The mechanism is physical — heavier, slower-volatilizing molecules tether the lighter ones — and it is why "cheap" and "essential" are not opposites in this matrix. A buyer who sees benzoin as merely a sweetener has missed its structural job.

## A Judgment Boundary on "Price Band"

**Direct answer:** The price column is a directional band, and it stops being useful the moment a buyer reads it as a price list — the band describes where a material sits, not what you should pay.

My position is that the "price band" column exists to rank materials against each other, not to quote a number. Local markets, grades, and harvest years all move the actual figure, so a band of "mid to high" tells you sandalwood sits above clove and below agarwood, and nothing more. The boundary to hold: use the band to set expectations, then let a real seller's price for a named species and grade be the number that matters. Anyone who treats the band as a price tag is asking the matrix to do a job it never claimed to do. *The author's view, not a verified fact.*

## More Questions Answered

### Q: Why are there exactly seven materials, and not eight or ten?
The seven are a deliberate editorial choice, not a natural law: they are the classics that appear again and again in Chinese incense writing and cover the four roles a blend needs — base, lift, top, and fixative. A fuller catalog would add musk, operculum, and several herbs, but those sit outside the everyday shelf, and adding them would dilute the point of a fast comparison. Treat the seven as a curated working set, not an exhaustive list; if a material you use is missing, the same five-axis logic applies to it without change.

## The Botanical Families, and Why They Predict So Much

**Direct answer:** The seven materials sit in seven different plant families, and the family does more than name a plant — it hints at the part, the chemistry, and the likely character before a single word about aroma is written.

Sort the seven by family and a pattern appears. Thymelaeaceae gives agarwood, a heartwood that turns fragrant under stress → a wound-triggered material, scarce by nature. Santalaceae gives sandalwood, a hemiparasitic tree whose heartwood oil builds over decades → slow and dear. Burseraceae gives both frankincense and myrrh, tapped gum-resins → renewable and cheaper. Myrtaceae gives clove, a dried flower bud → a bright spice. Styracaceae gives benzoin, a balsam → a sweet fixative. Each family carries a built-in story about how the material forms, and that story predicts scarcity, price, and substitution risk before the aroma is even mentioned.

## What a Fixative Is, in Plain Terms

**Direct answer:** A fixative is the ingredient that slows a blend down — it tethers the fast, volatile notes so they do not evaporate in the first minute — and benzoin is the classic example among the seven.

The mechanism is vapor-pressure physics. The light, volatile top notes want to fly off the moment heat touches them → a fixative is a heavier, slower-evaporating molecule mixed into the same paste → the two interact and the light notes are held back → the whole scent releases more slowly and evenly → the blend reads fuller and lasts longer. Benzoin's sweet vanilla warmth does exactly this, which is why it appears in so many blends despite its modest price. A buyer who sees "fixative" on a label is looking at a structural ingredient, not a minor note — it is the difference between a scent that arrives and a scent that stays.

## The Wound, the Tap, and the Harvest

**Direct answer:** The seven materials come into being by three different routes — wounding, tapping, and picking — and the route is the single best predictor of how scarce and how faked a material will be.

Sort the seven by their harvest route. Wounding produces agarwood → a tree must be injured or infected and then respond over years → the slowest, scarcest, most-faked route. Tapping produces frankincense, myrrh, and benzoin → the tree is scored and exudes on a cycle → renewable and cheaper. Picking produces sandalwood (harvested heartwood), borneol (distilled), and clove (dried buds) → a spectrum of effort and cost. The route predicts the price ladder almost perfectly: the wound-formed material tops the market, the tapped ones sit in the middle and low bands, and the picked ones scatter by difficulty. Read the route and you have read the economics before any price tag appears.

## The Fixative's Quiet Job, Expanded

**Direct answer:** A fixative earns its place not by smelling good but by holding the other notes down, and it does so through plain vapor-pressure physics — a slower-evaporating molecule that tethers the faster ones.

The blend heats and the volatile top notes try to leave at once → the fixative, a heavier molecule, sits among them and slows their exit → the two interact so the light notes are released over minutes instead of seconds → the scent reads fuller and lasts longer → a small dose of benzoin stretches a costly base or a bright top across the whole burn. The job is invisible because the fixative barely smells on its own, but the blend would collapse without it. When a label lists a fixative, it is naming a structural part, not a perfume note.

## A Table of the Seven by Their Harvest Route

**Direct answer:** The harvest route sorts the seven into three tiers, and the tier — not the aroma — is what predicts scarcity and fraud.

| Route | Materials | Scarcity | Fraud risk |

The table makes the route-to-risk link explicit. A buyer who reads this table once never again treats agarwood, myrrh, and clove as if they lived on the same economic footing.

## The Ember's Two Jobs: Fuel and Fragrance at Once

**Direct answer:** A stick's glowing tip does two jobs at the same time — it is both the fuel source and the fragrance release valve — and that double duty is why a stick cannot be judged by its smoke alone.

The tip is a moving balance. As the ember advances, it burns the binder and the carrier → that combustion supplies the heat → the heat then volatilizes the fragrant oils held in the paste just ahead of the ember → the scent releases into the air in front of the glow. Two materials, two jobs, one tip. A stick with a weak binder burns the fragrance too slowly and the note comes out thin; a stick with too much binder burns hot and scorches the note before it can release. The ideal is the middle: enough fuel to carry the heat, enough fragrance to ride the heat out cleanly. Read the tip and you have read the whole recipe in miniature.

## The One-Question Sort for Any New Material

**Direct answer:** The whole matrix reduces to a single question you can ask about any aromatic — how did this material get here? — and the answer lands it in the right tier before you read a word of description.

Ask "how did this material get here" and three answers cover the field. It was wounded into being → agarwood, the slow, scarce, heavily-faked tier. It was tapped from a living tree → frankincense, myrrh, benzoin, the renewable, cheap, low-fraud tier. It was picked or distilled → sandalwood, borneol, clove, a mixed tier sorted by effort. The question works because the origin route sets scarcity, price, and fraud risk all at once. A buyer who internalizes this one question never needs to memorize the whole table again — the route to market is the table, compressed.

## Reading a Seller's Answer, Not Just Their Label

**Direct answer:** The label names a material; the seller's answer names a source — and it is the second that tells you whether the first is worth trusting.

A label says "agarwood" or "sandalwood," and that is a claim. The test is whether the seller can stand behind it: name the species, the origin, the grade, and the method. A seller who answers those four without hedging has given you a claim you can verify; a seller who waves at the question has given you a label and nothing else. This is the practical heart of the comparison matrix — substitution risk is not visible on the tin, it is visible in the seller's willingness to be specific. Buy the answer, not the adjective.

## The Label's Silent Column: What It Omits

**Direct answer:** A label tells you what it wants you to see, but the column it leaves out — substitution risk — is the one that decides whether the price is honest, and reading the omission is a skill in itself.

Every material label names a scent and a price; almost none name the risk that the scent is not what it claims. That absence is itself information. A tin that says "agarwood" and nothing else → the seller is asking you to trust the name alone → the omission is the red flag. A tin that names the species and origin → the seller is volunteering the very facts a fraud would hide → the inclusion is the green flag. Read the omitted column first, and the price tag stops being a number and becomes an honesty signal. The material that hides nothing is the one worth paying for.

## Why the Matrix Should Be Read Before You Ever Open a Tin

**Direct answer:** The matrix's best use is not at the moment of purchase — it is the five minutes before, when you decide what kind of claim you are even willing to hear, and that framing is what makes it a decision tool rather than a reference.

Read the matrix before the shop, not in it. Decide in advance that for a scarce material you will demand species and origin → decide that for a renewable one you will check grade → decide that for a provenance-trap like borneol you will not pay a "natural" premium without a traceable source. Walking in with those rules already set means the seller's pitch does not write your questions for you. The matrix, read this way, is a script you bring to the counter, not a chart you consult after the fact. That is the difference between using it and merely having it.

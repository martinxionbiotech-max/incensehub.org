**Direct answer:** Seven classic materials — agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, borneol, benzoin, myrrh, clove — separate along five lines a buyer uses: smell, plant source, relative cost, job in the formula, and how far the name can be trusted.

**Key facts:** Treat "relative price" as a direction, never a quote. The column worth reading first is substitution risk: how likely the label names one material while the tin holds another.

## Seven materials in one view

> **Direct answer:** The matrix is the page in one view: each material's scent, its plant source, its price band, its job in a formula, and how often it is faked.

| Material | Smell | Botanical origin | Cost tier | Function | Fake risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agarwood (沉香) | Heavy, dark, sweetly resinous, unfolding in layers | Resin-loaded heartwood from *Aquilaria* | Top of the list; 奇楠 is the peak | Foundation; the most prized | Very high — pumped wood, foreign species |
| Sandalwood (檀香) | Creamy, smooth, softly woody | *Santalum album* heartwood | Upper middle; true *album* is scarce | Foundation; the calm classic | High — cousin species, scented substitutes |
| Frankincense (乳香) | Lively, clean, citrus-resin | Gum from *Boswellia* trees | Lower middle | Opening lift; ritual use | Low — usually the real resin |
| Borneol (龙脑) | Cold, piercing, camphor-like | *Dryobalanops* distillate, or synthetic from turpentine or camphor | Middle | Cooling top note; the "snow" in 雪中春信 | High — synthetic passed off as natural |
| Benzoin (安息香) | Sweet, creamy, vanilla-warm | Balsam from *Styrax* trees | Lower middle | Fixative; sweet base | Medium — Siam and Sumatra grades differ |
| Myrrh (没药) | Bitter, soil-like, balsamic | Gum from *Commiphora* trees | Low | Grounding base; ritual use | Low — usually the real resin |
| Clove (丁香) | Hot, sweet, spicy | Dried flower buds of *Syzygium aromaticum* | Low | Spice accent; fixative | Low to medium — bud versus clove bark (丁皮) |

A row teaches one material; a column teaches how the market behaves. Read the substitution column downward and you have a map of where fraud concentrates. Read the source column and you see that the seven are not one kind of thing: two woods, four resins or distillates, one flower bud.

## Reading the matrix by column

> **Direct answer:** Read the matrix column-first, because each column answers a different question and the answers do not trade off evenly.

The scent column is the softest. Words such as "creamy" or "balsamic" describe a family, not a grade; two sandalwood pieces can both be creamy and differ sharply in tenacity.

The source column is the hardest. *Santalum album*, *Aquilaria* spp., *Boswellia* resin, *Dryobalanops* distillate, *Styrax* resin, *Commiphora* resin, *Syzygium aromaticum* buds are botanical identities, and a seller who cannot name one is asking to be trusted on a word.

The price column is directional. Agarwood sits at the top because it forms slowly and is scarce; sandalwood below it because true *S. album* is scarce but does not need injury to make scent; frankincense and myrrh low because they are tapped from living trees and regrow; clove low because it is an agricultural bud. The band follows biology and supply, not quality.

The role column is a design decision. Agarwood and sandalwood anchor a base; frankincense lifts an opening; borneol cools; benzoin fixes; myrrh grounds; clove accents. A genuine material used in the wrong role can still ruin a blend.

The substitution column is the one to read before the price. It tells you where to spend verification effort: frankincense does not need the scrutiny agarwood does, and borneol needs a provenance question even though its price is modest.

## Three price tiers, and why they exist

> **Direct answer:** The seven sort into three economic tiers — slow-formed woods, tapped resins, and agricultural buds — and the tier explains most of the price gap before quality enters.

| Tier | Materials | Why the tier sits where it does |
|---|---|---|
| Slow-formed woods | Agarwood, sandalwood | Decades of growth; injury or heartwood development; scarce wild supply |
| Tapped resins | Frankincense, myrrh, benzoin | Harvested from living trees; trees regrow yield; supply is broader |
| Distillate or bud | Borneol, clove | Distillation or flower harvest; annual or short-cycle production |

Agarwood is the extreme case: injury, time, and a low yield of usable wood. Sandalwood is dear but less extreme, because the scent is already in the living heartwood and the tree can be cultivated. Frankincense and myrrh are cheaper because the tree survives tapping. Borneol is a distillate, natural and synthetic alike; clove is a seasonal flower bud.

The tier model prevents a common mistake: comparing materials as if price were a quality score. A cheap frankincense can be excellent at its job; an expensive agarwood can be a poor piece. The tier tells you what the material costs to produce, not how well it performs in a blend. A formulation may use a low-tier material as a fixative and a high-tier material as an accent, and the cheap material may be doing more structural work.

## Harvest route: wound, tap, or bud

> **Direct answer:** The harvest route explains the material's economics and its fraud pattern: wounding produces agarwood, tapping produces the gum resins, distillation produces borneol, and picking produces clove.

| Route | Materials | What the producer does | What can go wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wounding | Agarwood | Injures or inoculates a tree and waits years for resin | Early harvest, pale wood, false grade claims |
| Tapping | Frankincense, myrrh, benzoin | Cuts the bark and collects the exuded gum | Grade mixing, debris, species confusion |
| Distillation | Borneol, agarwood oil | Steam or water distillation of resinous material | Synthetic substitution, yield claims |
| Picking | Clove | Harvests and dries flower buds | Stems, exhausted buds, clove-bark substitution |

The route shapes price and fraud together. Wound-based material is slow and unpredictable, so its top grade is scarce and its price volatile; tapped resin renews each season and stays steadier; distillation is tied to the yield of the still; picking follows the harvest calendar. Fraud runs along the same lines: added weight and color in agarwood, grade mixing and debris in the gum resins, a synthetic-versus-natural claim for borneol, stems and clove bark (丁皮) in place of buds.

## Where faking concentrates

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood and sandalwood carry the highest substitution risk, and the two risks take different forms: agarwood is usually adulterated, while sandalwood is usually replaced.

Agarwood fraud is additive. A seller takes a cheaper wood and adds something — dye, oil, resin, a denser foreign species — to make it look and weigh like agarwood. The tells are visual and physical: dye sitting in cracks, oil weeping from the surface, density that is too uniform, a scent that is strong at the start and flat at the end. The [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) sets out the screening logic in detail.

Sandalwood fraud is substitutive. A seller offers a different *Santalum* species, or a scented unrelated wood, under the name of *Santalum album*. The tells are botanical and sensory: grain, oil content, the specific creaminess of true *album*, and the documentation that names the species. The [sandalwood guide](/blog/sandalwood-incense/) covers the comparison, and the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/) maps the common swaps.

## A substitution table with tells

> **Direct answer:** Each high-risk material has a characteristic substitution and a characteristic tell; matching the two is faster than memorizing a long list of fakes.

| Claimed material | Common substitute | Tell |
|---|---|---|
| Agarwood | Dyed or oil-soaked non-resinous wood | Dye in cracks; oil on the surface; flat dry-down |
| Agarwood | Unrelated dark wood scented with oil | Wrong grain; acrid burn; no resin seepage |
| Sandalwood | Other *Santalum* species | Weaker creaminess; different grain; vague species label |
| Sandalwood | Scented unrelated wood | Perfumed top note; little oil; short finish |
| Borneol | Synthetic borneol | Provenance is vague; price is too low for the natural claim |
| Benzoin | Lower grade or mixed resin | Earthier, smokier profile; inconsistent lumps |
| Clove | Clove bark (丁皮) or stems | Woody fragments; less oil; harsher bite |

The table is a screening aid, not a laboratory protocol. A single tell can occur in genuine material; two tells together are a reason to stop. The same logic applies to any material on the list: the question is not "does it look right?" but "which specific substitution does this look like, and does the evidence match?"

## What "natural" means on a borneol label

> **Direct answer:** Natural and synthetic borneol are chemically identical, so the word "natural" describes a supply route and a price premium, not a different molecule.

Natural borneol is distilled from *Dryobalanops* resin; the synthetic route starts from turpentine or camphor. The molecule is identical, and a laboratory assay may not separate the two, so a premium for "natural" buys traceability and a different minor-compound background rather than a different primary ingredient.

Ask for the route in writing. A supplier who can name the still, the species, and the country is making a checkable statement. A supplier who repeats the adjective without a paper trail is selling atmosphere. The same test works for "wild" agarwood, "pure" sandalwood, and "handmade" anything: the word is a claim, the document is the proof, and the price premium should follow the proof rather than the word.

## The fixative's job

> **Direct answer:** A fixative slows the evaporation of the more volatile materials around it, so a blend holds its shape for longer instead of collapsing into a thin top note.

Benzoin is the classic fixative in this set. Its resin is heavy and slow to release, and it gives a blend a sweet, vanilla-warm base that lingers. Agarwood and sandalwood also act as fixatives in practice, because their own scent is tenacious and their molecular weight is high. Clove can fix in a different way: its eugenol content is strong and persistent, and a small amount can anchor a spice accord.

The mechanism is physical. A fragrance is a mixture of molecules with different vapor pressures. The light molecules leave first, which is why a top note is bright and brief. A fixative adds heavier molecules that remain in the mixture longer, and it can also slow the diffusion of the lighter ones by changing the mixture's overall behavior. The result is a longer dry-down and a smoother transition from opening to base.

The practical mistake is to add too much fixative. A blend with a heavy benzoin base can smell syrupy and flat; a blend with too much clove can taste medicinal. The fixative should support the structure, not dominate it. This is where the 君臣佐使 logic is useful: the fixative is often a 佐 or 使, not the 君.

## Storage differences across the seven

> **Direct answer:** The seven behave differently in storage: woods and resins are durable, distillates and buds are not, and the difference follows from chemistry rather than price.

A dense wood chip can sit sealed for years with little change; some pieces improve as the surface oxidizes. A gum resin such as frankincense or myrrh is softer and can dry, clump, or absorb neighboring odors, so it wants a cool, dark container. Benzoin is sticky and can fuse into one mass in warmth. Borneol is volatile and wants a tight lid, away from heat. Clove loses its bite as the essential oil evaporates, so an old jar smells flatter than a fresh one. The rule is the same for all seven — cool, dark, dry, sealed — but the rate of change is not.

## A buyer's reading order

> **Direct answer:** Read the matrix in the order that matches your decision: role first, then risk, then price, then scent — because the first three narrow the field and the last one chooses within it.

Start with the role. A base points to agarwood or sandalwood; a lift to frankincense; cooling to borneol; a fixative to benzoin; grounding to myrrh; a spice accent to clove. This step eliminates most of the table without any sensory judgment.

Then read the risk. If the role points high, budget time and money for verification; if it points low, a simpler check may be enough. Risk does not mean "avoid"; it means "allocate attention."

Then the price band, where a formulation can fail commercially: a blend built on a top-tier material may be beautiful and unsellable.

Last, the scent. Descriptors tell you the family to expect; a warmed sample or a trial batch is worth more than any adjective, because scent is the easiest column to be seduced by and the hardest to verify from a label.

## FAQ

### Q: Which material is dearest?

Agarwood, with 奇楠 at the summit. The number is high because the wood forms only after injury and takes years to accumulate, so dense genuine pieces are rare. Sandalwood comes next: mature *Santalum album* is scarce, but the tree makes scent without being wounded, which makes supply less extreme. The tapped resins and the clove bud sit far below both.

### Q: Frankincense or myrrh — what separates them?

They are both gum resins collected by cutting bark, but the trees and the smells are different. Frankincense lifts: bright, fresh, citrus over resin. Myrrh settles: bitter, earthy, balsamic, with a heavier base. One is useful at the opening of a blend, the other at the foundation. Neither is a substitute for the other, and both are comparatively rarely adulterated.

### Q: Are borneol and camphor interchangeable?

Different compounds, similar sensation. Borneol has its own structure and supplies the cooling "snow" note in blends such as 雪中春信; camphor is a separate molecule with a harsher edge. Borneol itself arrives either from *Dryobalanops* distillate or from turpentine and camphor feedstocks, and those two routes give the same molecule — so "natural" describes provenance, not chemistry.

### Q: Where is fraud most likely?

At the top of the price scale. Agarwood attracts dye, oil, injection, and unrelated woods because the gap between genuine and fake is wide. Sandalwood attracts species substitution and scented substitutes. Borneol attracts a labeling problem rather than a fake molecule. The lower-priced resins are usually what they claim to be, though grade mixing still happens.

### Q: Siam versus Sumatra benzoin?

Two species, two grades. *Styrax tonkinensis* yields Siam benzoin, the finer sort — sweeter, closer to vanilla, cleaner in the melt. *Styrax benzoin* yields the Sumatra material, more widely sold and often earthier or smokier. Neither is fake; the gap is quality and cost, not authenticity.

## Summary

> **Direct answer:** The seven materials are best understood as a matrix of role, risk, price, and scent — not as a ranking — and the highest-risk names are the ones that reward the most verification.

Agarwood and sandalwood are the slow-formed woods, agarwood at the top of the market and the highest fraud risk. Frankincense, myrrh, and benzoin are tapped resins, cheaper and generally more straightforward. Borneol is a distillate with a natural-versus-synthetic label problem; clove is an agricultural bud with modest substitution risk. The tiers follow biology, not a quality score.

Read column-first: role narrows the field, risk allocates attention, price tests commercial viability, and scent makes the final choice. The buyer who understands the columns can ask of any material: what is it, where did it come from, what is it doing in the blend, and what would a fake look like?

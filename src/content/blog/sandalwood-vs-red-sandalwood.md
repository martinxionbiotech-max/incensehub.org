**Direct answer:** One written character ties together two trees from different plant families. 檀香 (sandalwood) is the scented heartwood of *Santalum album*, an evergreen in Santalaceae that taps neighboring roots; 紫檀 (red sandalwood) is the crimson heartwood of *Pterocarpus santalinus*, a legume in Fabaceae. Scent buys the first; dye and density buy the second.

**Key facts:** 檀香 is the reference incense wood — creamy, soft, and used either as a formula's backbone or on its own. 紫檀 is a dense red timber from southern India, sold for pigment and carving, with a minor incense role as a background powder. 檀 is an honorific in both names, not a genus label. Only the binomial identifies the material.

## Key Takeaways

- *Santalum album* (Santalaceae) and *Pterocarpus santalinus* (Fabaceae) belong to unrelated plant families.
- 檀 in 紫檀 is a borrowed honorific from the prized-timber vocabulary, not a genus name.
- Sandalwood carries a creamy, milky scent built from slow-forming santalols; red sandalwood carries santalin, a red pigment, and dense timber.
- *P. santalinus* is CITES-listed; species-level paperwork is part of the purchase.
- The two are not interchangeable in a blend: one is structural, the other a seasoning.

## One Character, Two Plants

**Direct answer:** The confusion begins in writing, not in the forest. A single character pulls two unrelated trees into the same English bucket.

Trace the trap as a chain:

a reader meets 檀 in both 檀香 and 紫檀 → 檀 reads as a mark of the aromatic-timber register → the reader treats both names as sandalwood → the English phrase "red sandalwood" repeats the assumption → a buyer expects a red version of the incense wood → the family-level split disappears behind one shared word.

Each link strengthens the next. 檀 operates as a register word, not a genus: classical usage tags fragrant, prized, or high-status woods with it. 檀香 ("fragrance of 檀") points to the real incense wood. 紫檀 ("purple 檀") borrows that prestige for a dark, heavy timber with no *Santalum* in it and no creamy smell.

The mistake is documented, not modern. 香乘 preserves 苏颂's line: 「檀香有数种黄白紫之异」 — several kinds of sandalwood, yellow, white, and purple. Within that pre-Linnaean scheme, 紫 described a shade-and-density profile inside one broad category, not a species. Botany later split the category: the shades came from different trees, not from one tree's grades.

The English layer compounds the problem. "Red sandalwood" sounds like a variety, and the adjective invites a buyer to think in grades rather than species. A spec that says only "sandalwood" has not identified anything yet.

## The Botany Behind the Names

**Direct answer:** The two trees differ in family, habit, native range, harvested part, and economic purpose — a full set of differences, not a shade.

*Santalum album* is an evergreen that partly parasitizes its neighbors, tapping their roots for water and minerals while keeping its own photosynthesis. Its heartwood accumulates sesquiterpene alcohols called santalols over decades. Slow accumulation is the reason mature wood smells soft rather than sharp, and the reason good material is expensive.

*Pterocarpus santalinus* is a nitrogen-fixing legume of the dry deciduous forests of the Eastern Ghats in southern India. Its heartwood holds santalin, a red pigment, and its density is high enough that a clean block will typically sink. The sapwood is pale and nearly worthless; the crimson core is the traded part.

| Feature | 檀香 — Sandalwood | 紫檀 — Red sandalwood |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *Santalum album* | *Pterocarpus santalinus* |
| Family | Santalaceae | Fabaceae |
| Growth habit | Root-tapping evergreen, partly parasitic | Nitrogen-fixing legume |
| Native range | South Asia and Indonesia; planted far beyond | Eastern Ghats dry forest, southern India |
| Traded part | Aromatic heartwood | Red heartwood; pale sapwood discarded |
| Defining trait | Slow santalol load, creamy aroma | Santalin pigment, high density |
| Incense role | Backbone or solo wood | Background powder with warm spice |
| Trade status | Managed harvest, plantation supply | CITES Appendix II, permit-bound |

The table is the argument in compressed form. Every row differs. The only shared item is the character in the name.

## Two Kinds of Value: Scent Against Pigment

**Direct answer:** Sandalwood was domesticated for how it smells; red sandalwood was harvested for what it colors. Those are separate economies that happened to borrow the same word.

Sandalwood's worth sits in the heartwood until heat or friction frees it. Oil accumulates over decades, so the chain is built around waiting — plant, tend, harvest, then mill or distill. Red sandalwood's worth spills at contact: grinding or soaking sends santalin into cloth, finish, or dye bath, and the dense timber serves carving and inlay. Incense is its secondary market.

santalin-bearing tissue → grinding or soaking → pigment leaves the wood → the medium takes the color.

Sandalwood's value leaves as aroma under heat, never as color. Color-on-contact against scent-on-heat keeps the two in different aisles.

## What Each Does in a Blend

**Direct answer:** Sandalwood is a structural material that can carry a formula or stand alone; red sandalwood is a small seasoning that adds warmth and a faint spice.

Sandalwood earns its reference status because its scent is warm, milky, sweet, and softly woody. It opens gradually under low heat, holds a blend together, and ages without turning sharp. In the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) logic of 合香 it often takes the chief role, and it also works as a single-material incense.

Red sandalwood stays in the background: woody, sweet, faintly rosy, gently spicy, and slow to reveal itself under low heat. It supports rather than leads. A pinch rounds a formula's edges; too much turns it into thin woody haze. Ask whether the material must hold the structure or adjust its border — sandalwood can do the first, red sandalwood only the second.

## Where the Two English Names Came From

**Direct answer:** The fragrance word reached English through Sanskrit, Persian, and Greek; the red name arrived as a translation of 紫檀.

The fragrance term descends from Sanskrit *candana*, the scented wood, and passed through Persian and Greek before "sandal" forms settled in European languages. Each borrowing carried the smell, not a color. The red name came later: traders translated 紫檀 into English and attached "sandalwood" because the wood moved through the same luxury channels. One name is a borrowing, the other a translation, and neither is a botanical statement.

## Conservation: Two Different Pressures

**Direct answer:** Both woods are scarce, for unrelated reasons. Sandalwood's constraint is time; red sandalwood's is geography and regulation.

A sandalwood trunk needs decades to load worthwhile oil. Harvest long outran regrowth, and plantations — often intercropped with host plants for the parasitic roots — became the answer. Farmed wood and other *Santalum* species, including Australian *S. spicatum* and Pacific *S. austrocaledonicum*, now fill much of the market.

Red sandalwood is constrained by a small wild range and slow growth, and it sits on CITES Appendix II: cross-border movement needs permits and species-level paperwork. The questions differ accordingly. For sandalwood, ask species, origin, and age. For red sandalwood, ask whether the shipment is permitted and whether the permit names the species.

## Telling Them Apart at the Bench

**Direct answer:** Color, weight, surface scent, and paperwork separate the two before any laboratory is involved.

Begin with color. Sandalwood heartwood runs pale yellowish-brown, fine-grained and even. Red sandalwood runs deep purple-red and darkens as air reaches it — obvious at arm's length.

Then weight. A clean *P. santalinus* block is dense enough to sink; sandalwood floats. Density is awkward to fake cheaply, but oil injection can weight pale wood, so a sink result does not mean the piece is untreated. It sorts a batch rather than proving a piece.

Then scent. Sandalwood smells creamy and milky even cold, and rubbing deepens it. Red sandalwood stays nearly silent until ground, shaved, or heated, then gives soft spice rather than cream. Paperwork closes the gap: at commercial volume, species statements and CITES documents outrank all three checks.

## A Confusion Table: Other Red Woods

**Direct answer:** "Red sandalwood" is not the only dark-red timber sold under a loose name, and several unrelated woods travel the same channels.

## What Belongs on a Specification

**Direct answer:** Four fields settle the identity of either wood: binomial, origin, form, and — for red sandalwood — permit documentation.

Put four things on the order: the Latin binomial, the harvest origin, the form, and — for red sandalwood — the permit reference. The binomial is non-negotiable, because no trade name can separate the two. Origin and species together explain the aroma and the price; Indian *S. album* is the benchmark, while Indonesian, Australian, and Pacific wood reads differently. Form decides use: powder for incense, chips for gentle heat, with powder the easier one to stretch, so burn a sample before a bulk order. A legitimate *P. santalinus* shipment carries CITES paperwork that names the species; red powder without it is a legal question before a quality question.

## A Misconception and Two Bad Habits

**Direct answer:** Three familiar ideas cause most of the bad buying in this corner of the trade.

Three ideas cause most of the bad buying here. The first is a misconception worth naming outright: that red sandalwood is a red kind of sandalwood. It is wrong at the family level, and wrong about where the color and the scent come from. The second is that a high price guarantees authenticity; it does not, and both markets carry adulteration. The third is that the Chinese name resolves the question; 檀 is precisely the word that created the confusion. For a buyer, the binomial is the cheapest insurance available.

*These are editorial judgments about buying practice, not laboratory findings.*

## FAQ

### Q: Are sandalwood and red sandalwood the same plant?

No. One is a sandalwood-family evergreen; the other is a legume. *Santalum album* sits in Santalaceae and yields scented heartwood; *Pterocarpus santalinus* sits in Fabaceae and yields dye-rich red timber. Nothing joins them except the character 檀, which 紫檀 uses as a title rather than a taxonomic label.

### Q: Why is red sandalwood called sandalwood at all?

The English phrase is a translation, not a botanical description. 紫檀 ("purple 檀") applied the honorific 檀 to a dense dark wood, and English traders rendered it literally. Because older texts had already sorted 檀香 by shade — yellow, white, purple — the translation looked like a variety name. It is a naming layer, not a family relation.

### Q: Can red sandalwood replace sandalwood in a blend?

Not in a structural role. Red sandalwood brings woody-sweet warmth and a soft spice, and it sits behind other notes. Sandalwood is creamy and milky enough to carry a formula on its own and to hold other materials in place. Red powder can sit beside a sandalwood base, but it cannot become the base. A swap changes the architecture, not just the surface.

### Q: Which one costs more?

Grade and origin move the number more than the category does. Documented *P. santalinus* is scarce and CITES-restricted, so good red wood carries a serious price. High-grade Indian *S. album* is also costly: it grows slowly and was heavily harvested, though plantations have widened supply. Neither price proves identity, because both markets carry adulteration.

### Q: How do I tell them apart without a lab?

Start with hue: a red block runs deep crimson and reads dense, while sandalwood stays pale yellowish-brown. Then weight: a clean red block typically sinks, while sandalwood floats. The nose separates them next, milky and creamy on one side, softly spicy-sweet on the other. At commercial scale, the species statement on the paperwork outranks all three checks.

### Q: The red powder I bought — is it genuine or dyed filler?

A photograph cannot answer that. Red sandalwood powder is commonly cut with dyed lighter wood, so uniform color proves little. Ask for the species name, request origin and permit documentation, and heat a small sample: genuine material reads softly spicy-sweet, while filler turns flat or chemical. If the seller cannot name the species, treat the powder as unidentified.

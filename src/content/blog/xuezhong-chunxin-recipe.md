> **Direct answer:** 雪中春信 ("Snow in Spring") is a classical 合香 (hé xiāng, compounded incense) linked by tradition to 苏轼 (Su Shi), though the link is legend rather than record. Its signature is a camphor (樟脑) note that reads cold against a warm wood base — winter turning into spring, rendered as scent.

## Key facts

- **Type:** a classical 合香 (compounded incense), not a single-material formula.
- **Signature note:** 樟脑 (camphor) at one 钱 against a base measured in 两 — a cold surface over a warm wood core.
- **Chief material:** 檀香 (sandalwood) at one 两 in Version A; 沉香 (agarwood) at one 两 in Version B.
- **Mineral base:** 羊脛灰 (sheep-shin-bone ash) at four 两 in Version A — a heat-buffering carrier, not a scent material.
- **Recorded in:** 香乘 (*Xiang Cheng*), the Ming manual, which preserves more than one formula under the name; the concept is stable while the materials shift.
- **Attribution:** the link to 苏轼 (Su Shi) is traditional and unverified, and the manual postdates him by centuries.
- **Proportion:** four 两 of cyperus against one 钱 of camphor makes the naming note roughly one fortieth of the chief mass — the material that names a blend need not be the material that dominates it.

## The Formula, and the Trick Inside It

**Direct answer:** The camphor-forward version reads 香附子四两、鬱金二两、檀香一两、麝香少许、樟脑一钱、羊脛灰四两 — four liǎng of cyperus, two of turmeric, one of sandalwood, a trace of musk, one qián of camphor, and four liǎng of sheep-shin-bone ash — ground fine, bound with refined honey, aged, and burned in the usual way.

Read the roles rather than the shopping list:

| Role | Material | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| 君 (chief) | 檀香 sandalwood | The warm woody body the blend is built on |
| 臣 (minister) | 香附子 cyperus, 鬱金 turmeric | Herbal middle tones that round out the wood |
| 佐 (assistant) | 樟腦 camphor | The cool snap that gives the blend its name |
| 使 (guide) | 麝香 musk, 羊脛灰 ash, honey | Fixes, buffers, and binds the powder |

The mechanism is a contrast, not a flavor. A very small charge of a volatile, sharp-smelling solid sits inside a much larger warm mass → heat releases the camphor before the woods begin to give up their oils → the first thing the nose meets is cold and clean → the sandalwood and the cyperus-turmeric middle arrive a beat later → the mind reads the sequence as a cold surface over something warm and growing. That is the "snow in spring" effect. Increase the camphor and the sequence collapses into a single cold note, because a contrast needs two sides.

Version A is a three-material aromatic core with a mineral base: cyperus, turmeric, sandalwood, a trace of musk, a touch of camphor, and a large share of bone ash. Version B replaces that core with agarwood and spreads the middle across a dozen herbs and spices. The surviving transcription of B does not list camphor, even though the manual's own description treats the two forms as the same idea; that gap in the text is worth naming rather than filling in with a guess. What the pair proves is narrower and more useful: the recipe had a stable concept and fluid materials. A household substituted what its region supplied.

The two versions also teach different things. A has nowhere to hide → one cool note against three warm materials makes the assistant role visible at a glance. B buries the same logic in a crowd → it shows how a signature note behaves when the base is rich and wide. Neither is the "real" one. They are two experiments on one question: how little cold does it take to change the season of a warm blend?

## Reading the Old Units Without Pretending to Precision

**Direct answer:** The quantities are written in 两, 钱, and 分, and the defensible reading is relational — ratios between materials — because the absolute weight of a 两 drifted across dynasties and regions.

The ladder itself is simple: one 两 divides into ten 钱, and one 钱 into ten 分. The gram value is the unstable part. In the Song–Ming window a 两 sat somewhere near 37 g, within a broad range of roughly 31–41 g depending on period and place. Any single modern gram figure is therefore an editorial convenience, not a fact recovered from the page.

Ratios survive that drift. Version A's four 两 of cyperus against one 两 of sandalwood is 4:1 whatever a 两 weighed. The camphor at one 钱 against four 两 of cyperus is one part in forty of the chief herbal mass. That second ratio is the recipe's whole argument in arithmetic: the naming note is a fortieth of the blend by weight. Convert the numbers to grams and you manufacture a precision the manual never offered; keep them as proportions and the design becomes legible.

The same discipline applies to the ash. Four 两 of 羊脛灰 is not a spice measurement to be halved casually; it is a structural share of the mixture, closer to the amount of a base material than to the amount of an aromatic.

## The Ash That Looks Like Filler

**Direct answer:** 羊脛灰 — ash from burned sheep shin bones — reads as a medieval oddity to a modern eye, but in this formula it is a mineral base with a thermal job, not inert padding.

Bone ash is largely calcium phosphate, a fine, non-combustible powder. Its function follows from that: it does not burn, so it holds heat and spreads it → the aromatic particles sit against a slow, even mineral bed instead of a fast, uneven one → the camphor's volatility is moderated and the woods release over a longer window → the burn stays cooler and more controlled than the same aromatics would manage alone. The ash also gives the oils a large surface to cling to, which helps carry the scent rather than merely dilute it.

A filler adds weight and subtracts character. This material adds weight and changes the burn's physics. The distinction matters for anyone adapting the recipe: if you remove the ash and change nothing else, you have not simplified the formula, you have removed its temperature control. A clean, heat-stable mineral carrier can stand in for the historical ash, but the function should be replaced, not deleted. *Editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## Three Kinds of Cold

**Direct answer:** Camphor is not the only material that smells cold, and confusing it with borneol or menthol changes the blend more than most substitutions do.

| Cool material | Source | Character | Place in this recipe |
|---|---|---|---|
| 樟脑 camphor | *Cinnamomum camphora* | Sharp, clean, faintly medicinal, highly volatile | The recorded "snow" note |
| 龙脑 / 冰片 borneol | Dipterocarp resin | Cool but smoother, sweeter, less aggressive | A separate classical cool note |
| 薄荷脑 menthol | Peppermint oil | Cold and minty, explicitly food-like | Rare in classical incense |

The three occupy different sensory rooms. Camphor reads as weather and medicine; borneol reads as a refined, almost creamy chill; menthol reads as candy and toothpaste. A maker who swaps menthol in because "snow means cold means mint" has not intensified the effect, only changed its genre. The specificity is the point: 雪中春信 names 樟脑, and the name depends on that particular kind of cold. *Editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## The Legend, and the Cost of Chasing It

**Direct answer:** The Su Shi attribution, including the story of collecting plum-blossom snow water for seven years, is a romantic later invention; the formula itself appears in Ming-era manuals compiled long after his lifetime.

That does not make the recipe worthless. It makes the recipe a different kind of object. A signed work invites reconstruction — find the author's hand, reproduce it exactly. An attributed formula invites reading — understand the structure, then adapt it honestly. The legend obscures the craft by shifting attention from the compounding to the biography. What survives is a design lesson about restraint, and that lesson does not need a famous name attached.

The boundary is worth stating plainly: "traditional attribution" is not a synonym for "documented authorship." A page can honor the tradition and still tell you the attribution is unverified. Those two positions are compatible, and holding both is more useful than choosing between a credulous story and a dismissive one.

## Reconstructing the Structure, Not the Recipe

**Direct answer:** A modern maker can build a workable study version by keeping the skeleton — warm wood body, herbal middle, trace cool note, mineral base, honey binder — and marking every restricted or archaic part as a substitution.

The skeleton translates cleanly. Sandalwood powder carries the 君 role. Cyperus and turmeric, both obtainable, carry the herbal middle. Camphor stays, at a fraction of the total; borneol is the finer substitute if camphor's sharpness is too coarse. A clean mineral powder replaces the bone ash. Musk is the one material to drop or replace outright: it is restricted or unavailable in most markets, and modern reproductions commonly omit it or use a tiny synthetic accord. The blend still works, because musk was the 使 role, not the identity.

The finishing chain deserves its own attention. Honey wets the powder into a workable mass → it seals the volatile particles to some degree → the shaped incense rests → over weeks the materials interdiffuse and the sharper edges oxidize and soften → the camphor's menthol-like bite rounds into the wood → the blend stops smelling assembled. Ageing is not a garnish on the recipe; it is the step that turns four ingredients into one scent. Judge a fresh batch on its first day and you will reject a blend that a month of rest would have finished.

## Reading It Before You Grind

**Direct answer:** Before buying or milling anything, answer three questions: what is the core the blend is about, which single material gives it its identity, and which parts are you substituting or marking.

Those questions prevent the two standard failures. The first is treating the ingredient list as a shopping list and discovering halfway through that musk is restricted or that the unit conversion was a guess. The second is boosting the camphor because it is "the point" — when the point is precisely that it stays small. Reading for roles catches both before money is spent.

There is a further judgment boundary that separates a study from a replica. A replica asks to be judged by fidelity to a text that disagrees with itself. A study asks to be judged by whether the structure works. For a recipe with multiple surviving versions, a restricted fixative, and units that no longer convert cleanly, the study is the only honest target. The craft transfers; the literal formula does not. *Editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## Evidence status

The page rests on two kinds of statement, and they do not carry the same weight. The formula text, its units, its variant versions, and the gap where Version B omits a camphor line are documentary — they can be checked against the 香乘. The behaviour claims — camphor volatilizing ahead of the woods, bone ash moderating the burn, ageing rounding the blend — are a reader's physical reconstruction, marked here as editorial view or traditional belief. No burn test, no headspace analysis and no comparative trial is cited, because none appears in the record. Where the text disagrees with itself, the disagreement is reported rather than resolved by guesswork.

## FAQ

### Q: What does 雪中春信 mean?
The four characters say "snow within, spring's message." The name describes a hinge in the year: the ground still cold, something underneath already moving toward warmth. The blend tries to make that image smellable — a thin cold surface over a warm core. Camphor supplies the cold surface; sandalwood and the herbal middle supply the warmth. The name is not a flavor description but a seasonal one, which is why a minty reading misses it.

### Q: Did Su Shi really create this recipe?
The attribution is traditional and unverified. Su Shi is associated in incense lore with the romantic story of gathering plum-blossom snow water over seven years, but that story is a later invention rather than a documented source. The formula survives in the 香乘, a Ming compilation, and that manual records multiple versions that disagree. Treat the author as a literary frame around the recipe, not as evidence about its origin.

### Q: Why is the camphor measured so small if it gives the blend its name?
Because the effect is a contrast, and a contrast depends on disproportion. At one 钱 against a base of several 两, camphor is a small, sharp event inside a large warm field. The nose notices the cold precisely because it is rare. Raise camphor to the level of the woods and the blend becomes a camphor incense with a wood background — the seasonal image disappears even though the material is more present.

### Q: Can the recipe be made today?
The structure can, with substitutions. Sandalwood, cyperus, turmeric, camphor, and honey are all obtainable; a clean mineral powder can replace the bone ash; musk is restricted or unavailable and is usually omitted or replaced. The result is not a faithful reproduction of a disputed text, but it is a legitimate study of the formula's design. Read the ratios, keep the cool note small, bind with honey, and let the blend rest before judging it.

### Q: Does the recipe specify camphor in both versions?
Version A lists one 钱 of camphor explicitly. The legible transcription of Version B does not include a camphor line, even though the manual's description treats the two forms as variations on one idea. That is a gap in the surviving text, not a license to add ingredients. The honest reading is that B is agarwood-forward and broader, while the cool-note logic is stated for the recipe as a whole.

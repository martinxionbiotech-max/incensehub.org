**Direct answer:** Incense packaging uses a small vocabulary to describe a much larger set of materials. One word can name a plant group, a rank inside it, a physical form, or nothing testable at all. This reference separates those jobs and shows what a buyer can verify before money changes hands.

**Key facts:** The pressure concentrates where the price gap is widest — Indian sandalwood, kyara-grade agarwood, natural ambergris, true frankincense. A category noun can stay technically true while the contents change, which is why the noun alone is weak evidence.

---

## Key Takeaways

- A label word is a **claim of a particular size**. Some claims fit on the packet; others need a document behind them.
- The scarce end of the trade attracts the most invention, because the margin pays for it.
- Ask **which plant** and **which form** before arguing about price.
- A cheap price is a **question**, not a verdict; an expensive one is not proof either.
- A declared substitute is a product. An undeclared one is a claim the buyer cannot audit.

## Four jobs a label word can do

**Direct answer:** Label language is not one kind of statement. It can name a group, rank an item, place it geographically, describe a form, or simply set a mood — and each job demands a different check.

A **category word** names a family, not a species. "Sandalwood," "agarwood," "benzoin," and "frankincense" all work this way. The word can be true while the contents vary enormously, because the family contains several plants and, in practice, several non-plant substitutes.

A **rank word** places an item inside a category: kyara, Mysore, 老山檀, first grade. Rank implies scarcity and price, and it is the easiest claim to print because the object rarely proves or disproves it. Rank claims need paper or a very experienced nose.

A **place word** names a origin: Hainan, Assam, Cambodia, Mysore. Place cannot be read from an object, so the claim rests entirely on the supply record. A place word is cheap to add and expensive to disprove.

A **form word** describes a physical state: powder, tears, chips, oil, absolute, distillate. Form is checkable through behaviour, and it changes what the product can do. An oil is not a wood, even when the scent family matches.

A **mood word** — natural, pure, premium, aged, artisan — makes no testable claim. It is atmosphere. The word costs nothing because there is nothing to verify.

The practical move is to sort the words on a package into these five jobs before judging the product. A label with one category word and four mood words has said very little. A label with a binomial, a plant part, a form, a lot, and a producer has said a great deal.

## How a substitution actually works

**Direct answer:** A substitution is a change in one layer of a product while the label keeps describing another layer. The four common layers are species, rank, form, and origin.

A **species swap** keeps the family word and changes the plant. Nothing printed is false, because the family word never named a species. The tell is behavioural: a relative often burns drier, flatter, or faster than the benchmark.

A **rank swap** keeps the species and changes the position inside it. Species claims remain true, which is why identity tests cannot catch it. The tell is documentary or physical: grade markers, resin distribution, a lot record that does not exist.

A **form swap** replaces a solid with an oil or a compound. A cold sniff can be convincing; the burn is where the difference shows. A coating releases early and fades, while material held inside wood releases slowly and develops.

An **origin swap** keeps the material and changes the place. The object cannot reveal its forest, so the claim lives or dies on paperwork. This is the swap most likely to be honest in substance and false in premium.

The layers can combine. A product can use the right species at the wrong rank, or the right rank from the wrong place, or the right material in the wrong form. Each combination leaves a different evidence trail, and a buyer who knows which layer is being claimed knows which check to use.

## Where the swap hides, by format

**Direct answer:** The same substitution looks different depending on what is on the shelf. A stick, a powder, a chip, and a bottle of oil each give a seller a different place to hide the gap between name and contents.

**Sticks.** A stick can be ground aromatic wood, a charcoal or sawdust core coated with fragrance, or a mixture. The core decides how much of the scent is carried by the burn itself. A wood-powder stick tends to hold its middle; a coated core opens fast and thins out. Breaking one stick across the grain shows whether the interior is fibrous or uniform.

**Coils and cones.** These are the same dough in different shapes. A coil adds binder and length; a cone adds mass and a short, hot burn. Shape can conceal a low aromatic load because the first release is strong. A coil that smells vivid for ten minutes and then turns ashy is telling you where the aromatic fraction sits.

**Loose powder.** Powder is the easiest format to extend. Fine mineral or wood filler can be coloured and scented, and the result looks uniform. Genuine aromatic powder usually shows some fibre and variation under a lens. The cold smell can be strong in both cases; the burn is the better test, because filler leaves a flat, dusty tail.

**Raw chips.** A chip can be oiled, dyed, or both. The cut face is the tell, because surface treatment stops at the surface. Resin distribution inside the grain separates a naturally resinous piece from one fed oil from outside. Weight and sink tests measure density, and density can be manufactured.

**Resin tears.** Natural tears form irregularly as the tree exudes and the gum hardens. Re-formed resin is melted and shaped into uniform pellets or discs, a legitimate process when declared and a misleading one when sold as natural tears. Re-formed resin tends to soften all at once; a natural tear can soften unevenly.

**Oils and absolutes.** A bottle is the least inspectable format. The label can name a species and still contain a blend, a dilution, or a reconstitution. The plant part matters — clove bud, leaf, and stem oils are different materials — and a true essential oil usually carries the species and the part on the label. Without both, the bottle is a scent, not an identity.

The pattern is consistent: the more processed the product, the less the object itself can tell you. Sticks and powders hide composition; chips hide treatment; oils hide almost everything. The burden shifts from inspection to documentation as the format becomes more refined.

## Why the price gap exists

**Direct answer:** A swap becomes worth attempting when the gap between a named material's price and a relative's cost is wide enough to pay for the ambiguity. The gap supplies the motive; the label supplies the cover.

Agarwood shows the range. Trade accounts put ordinary medicinal chips at a few yuan per gram and top kyara at prices compared with gold. That spread is not secret; it is why the category attracts so much invention. Sandalwood's range is narrower, between Indian heartwood and Australian plantation material, and still wide enough to support a steady trade in vague labels.

Testing has its own economics. A laboratory bill can exceed the retail value of a small purchase, so verification is rational only above a certain price. Cheap sticks therefore live on category words; expensive pieces live or die on documents. The buyer's task is to know which side of that line the purchase sits on.

The gap also explains why some substitutes are honest products and others are frauds. Australian sandalwood is not a counterfeit. Sumatra benzoin is a real resin with a legitimate role. Ambroxide is a standard perfumery ingredient. The trouble starts when a label claims the prestige material while the box holds the relative, or when the price is anchored to a name the contents never earned.

## What the burn can show

**Direct answer:** The burn is a better witness than the cold sniff, but it is a witness to behaviour, not identity.

A wood-based stick usually opens gradually and holds a middle. A stick built on a scented core tends to arrive fast and thin out. A resin tear softens unevenly; a re-formed pellet softens all at once. A natural chip releases aroma as heat moves through the grain; an oiled chip releases a burst from the surface and then goes quiet.

These differences are real, and they are not proof. A skilled compound can imitate a plausible curve for a while. A genuine material can burn badly because it is old, damp, or badly stored. The burn narrows the field; it does not name the plant.

Ash carries less information than the scent. Colour, weight, and texture vary with the material, the binder, and the temperature, so ash is a weak signal on its own. It becomes useful only in comparison: the same stick, the same holder, the same room, two different samples.

The practical rule is to compare like with like. A stick against a stick, a resin against a resin, a blend against a blend. Comparing a stick to a raw chip tests the format, not the material.

## Checks and their limits

**Direct answer:** Every home check can eliminate something. None can establish species, rank, and origin together, and each has a known way of being defeated.

**Rested cold sniff.** Useful for telling whether aroma sits on the surface or inside the material. It cannot name a species, and a good compound can pass it.

**Marked burn.** Shows the shape of the scent curve: front-loaded or developing, where the middle sits, where the aroma dies. It cannot tell you whether a good curve came from good material.

**Water dip.** Gives relative density, which loosely tracks resin or oil load. It cannot tell whether density comes from resin or from injection.

**Broken face.** Shows whether colour is a coating and whether the grain is fibrous or uniform. It cannot identify a species on its own.

**Resin shape.** Separates an irregular natural tear from a uniform re-formed pellet. It cannot tell whether a re-formed resin is genuine resin or a cheaper gum.

**Powder feel.** Distinguishes fibrous ground wood from uniform dust or a cut powder. It cannot name the plant.

**Paper cross-check.** Shows whether a seller can tie a claim to a lot. It cannot prove the paper itself is honest.

**Laboratory analysis.** Reports a molecular profile, species markers, or adulterants. It says nothing about other batches.

The ceiling deserves emphasis. A dense chip is dense; it is not necessarily resinous. A dark bead is dark; it is not necessarily old. Each test produces a property, and the buyer supplies the interpretation. That interpretation is where self-testing usually goes wrong.

## Paper, lots, and the chain

**Direct answer:** A document is useful only when it ties a claim to the exact unit being sold. Most weak paperwork fails at that link, not at the laboratory.

| Document | What it establishes | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Seller, buyer, value, terms | Whether the goods match the description |
| Packing list | Carton count, net and gross weight | Identity of the contents |
| Bill of lading | Carrier, route, consignee, date | Quality or species |
| Certificate of origin | Country of manufacture or export | Country of harvest |
| Phytosanitary certificate | Plant-health inspection before export | Aromatic quality or grade |
| Fumigation certificate | A treatment, with date and agent | Residues or later contamination |
| Import permit | Authorised entry | Whether it covers this lot |
| CITES permit | Legal trade in a listed species | Species identification of every piece |
| Chain-of-custody record | Each handoff, with dates and signatures | Whether a handoff was omitted |
| Batch record | Inputs, process, output for a run | Whether your sample came from that run |
| Retained sample | A physical reference from a lot | Whether it was stored correctly |
| Specification sheet | Parameters the seller agrees to meet | Whether the goods meet them |
| Certificate of analysis | Test results for a named sample | Whether the sample represents the lot |
| Safety data sheet | Handling hazards | Identity, purity, or origin |

Each document answers one question and leaves the next open. A certificate of origin does not identify the species. A certificate of analysis does not prove the lot. A CITES permit does not grade the wood. Reading paper means knowing which question it was designed to answer, then asking for the next link.

The smallest useful set is identity, form, lot, and origin, each with a date and an issuer. For an everyday purchase, that set is not worth the cost; the label and the burn are the practical substitute. For a high-value piece, the set is the difference between a purchase and a bet.

For agarwood, legality is part of the product. *Aquilaria* is CITES-listed, so the permit chain is not an optional extra. The [CITES checklist](https://checklist.cites.org/#/en/search/output_layout=alphabetical&level_of_listing=0&show_synonyms=1&show_author=1&show_english=1&show_spanish=1&show_french=1&scientific_name=Aquilaria) is the reference for the listing.

## Following a material up the chain

**Direct answer:** A name can change hands six times between tree and shelf, and each handoff is a place where a precise claim can soften into a category word.

- **Harvest.** Wood cut, resin tapped, bark stripped. Species, place, and date exist; a label usually does not. The roadside buyer may be the only person who saw the standing tree.
- **Aggregation.** A local dealer combines many small lots into one consignment. Twenty sacks from twenty ridges become one invoice, and individual provenance blurs. This is the cheapest point to record origin.
- **Processing.** Milling, screening, drying, melting, filtering, distilling. Every change of form is also a change of claim: powder is not chips, a re-formed pellet is not a natural tear.
- **Export and import.** Tariff codes, plant-health rules, and for *Aquilaria*, CITES permits. A permit covers a shipment, not every chip in a workshop.
- **Blending or repackaging.** Binders, other aromatics, or fragrance compounds are added. "Contains" becomes ambiguous; the specification sheet, not the raw invoice, describes what the consumer receives.
- **Retail.** Decanting, cutting, repackaging under a shop name. Each step can lose the lot number. A retailer who keeps the original bag can answer questions; one selling from an unlabeled jar cannot.

Provenance is cheaper to preserve than to reconstruct. Records created at harvest cost little; records assembled after a dispute cost a great deal and often cannot be assembled at all. The buyer's question is not "does this look old?" but "where would the lot number have been written down, and can the seller show that record?"

## A buyer's notebook

**Direct answer:** The cheapest verification tool is a record. A buyer who writes down the claim, the lot, the price, and the burn result builds a reference library that no single test can provide.

Keep the packaging. A photograph of the front label is useful; a photograph of the back label, the lot stamp, and the receipt is better. Packaging is evidence of what was claimed at the moment of sale, and it is the first thing discarded.

Record the claim in the seller's own words. "Indian sandalwood" and "*Santalum album* heartwood powder" are different claims, and the difference matters if the product disappoints. Note the price, the weight, and the date, because a price without a date says little in a market that moves.

Write a short burn note. How the aroma opened, how it changed, where it faded, whether the ash was fine or gritty, whether the smoke was thin or heavy. These observations are subjective, and that is fine: they are comparisons against your own earlier samples, not laboratory results. A notebook with twenty burn notes is a better guide than a web page with two hundred opinions.

Keep a retained sample. A small sealed bag of each purchase, labeled with the date and the seller, lets you re-smell a material months later and check whether your memory of it was accurate. It also gives you something concrete to compare when the next bag arrives.

Separate the claim from the conclusion. In the notebook, write "seller said X" in one column and "I observed Y" in another. The habit prevents a plausible story from hardening into a remembered fact. It also makes disputes easier, because the original claim is still visible in writing.

None of this requires expertise. It requires the patience to write four lines before the packaging goes in the bin. Over a year, those lines become the most reliable reference a non-specialist can own.

## Six questions for a seller

**Direct answer:** The fastest way to learn what a seller knows is to ask six questions in a fixed order and listen for specifics.

**What plant, in Latin?** A dealer usually knows the binomial. A reseller may not, which is not a fault, but it changes how much of the chain they can describe.

**Which part?** Heartwood, sapwood, leaf, bud, root, resin. The part often explains the price more than the species does.

**How was it processed?** Milled, screened, distilled, extracted, melted, re-formed, or left whole. Processing is where form changes and a claim can quietly change with it.

**Which lot, and when?** A batch code and a packing date are the minimum for traceability. A seller who can name the lot can usually produce a record.

**What can you document?** Species, form, rank, origin, and the issuer of any test. Ask for the smallest set that closes the chain.

**What would make you refuse this sale?** A specific answer — a failed moisture check, a permit that did not match, a batch that smelled off — shows a standard. A shrug shows the standard is the transaction.

The order matters. Starting with "is this good?" invites an opinion; starting with the binomial invites a fact. A seller who answers all six is not necessarily honest, but one who cannot answer two is asking you to buy a story.

## Three purchases, three levels of evidence

**Direct answer:** The right amount of checking depends on what is at stake. The examples below are illustrative, not listings.

**A box of everyday sticks.** The cost of a laboratory test exceeds the cost of the box. Rely on disclosure, consistency across the box, and the burn curve. Walk away from a prestige name sold below the plain version, because the contradiction has no innocent explanation at this tier.

**A sampler or a repeat order.** Here a conversation is worth having. Ask for the species, the plant part, the binder, and a lot number. Compare two batches of the same product; consistency is evidence of a controlled process. Refusal to name a species at this tier is a reason to look elsewhere.

**A single high-value piece.** Paper, lot, origin, and an independent opinion are all proportionate. The object alone cannot prove rank or place, and the price makes the missing document expensive. Pressure to decide quickly is a warning, not a courtesy.

The tiers are not about snobbery. They are about the economics of checking: below a certain price, verification costs more than the product; above it, the absence of verification costs more than the paperwork.

## Why grades resist comparison

**Direct answer:** Grade words feel objective, but most of them describe a position in a local sorting convention rather than a measurable property. Two sellers can both be honest and still mean different things by "first grade."

A grade usually combines several visible traits: density, colour, oiliness, grain, size, and the absence of defects. Different markets weight those traits differently. A producing region may sort by size because size is easy to show at a roadside sale. An export broker may sort by density because weight pays. A retailer may sort by appearance because the customer sees the object before smelling it. The same parcel can land in different grades depending on who is doing the sorting and for whom.

This is not necessarily manipulation. It is what happens when a trade skill is passed down through practice rather than written into a standard. The buyer's difficulty is that a grade word travels without its sorting rule. "Top grade" from one workshop may be a middle grade by another workshop's rule, and neither seller has lied.

The remedy is not to abolish grades but to ask for the traits behind them. Which property puts this piece in the top rank: density, oil content, scent at room temperature, or size? A seller who can name the trait has a rule. A seller who can only repeat the rank has a label. The named trait also gives the buyer something to inspect, which is more useful than a rank with no definition.

A second remedy is to compare inside a single seller's stock. If a seller offers three grades, the difference between them is observable even when the absolute labels are idiosyncratic. The top grade should differ from the middle in a way the seller can state and the buyer can see. That internal comparison is often more informative than a cross-market rank, because it holds the sorting convention constant.

The limit is obvious: grades cannot be compared across markets without a shared standard, and most incense materials do not have one. That is why documentation tends to replace grade words at the high end. A certificate does not settle whether a piece is beautiful, but it can settle which species and which lot were tested. Grade remains a craft judgment; identity can be papered.

## What honest labeling looks like

**Direct answer:** A strong label is dull. It names the plant, the part, the form, the binder, the lot, and the producer, and it does not spend words on atmosphere.

| Field | Weak version | Strong version |
|---|---|---|
| Plant | "Sandalwood" | *Santalum album* |
| Part | "Wood" | Heartwood |
| Form | "Incense" | Milled powder, 40 g |
| Binder | "Natural binder" | Makko, stated ratio |
| Lot | Absent | Batch code matching the package |
| Date | Absent | Production or packing date |
| Origin | "Imported" | Region and country, with a supply record |
| Producer | A brand name only | Named workshop or processor |

Not every product can carry every field. A small maker buying from a local processor may not know the harvest region, and saying so is better than inventing one. The strong label is not the one with the most words; it is the one where each word corresponds to something the seller can defend.

This is also a way to judge a product page. A page that lists species, part, form, and lot is doing the work of a label. A page that lists only benefits and moods is asking the buyer to fill the gaps. The gap is where substitution lives.

## Seller scripts, and the questions they dodge

**Direct answer:** Most vague incense listings reuse a small set of phrases. Each phrase sounds like evidence, and each can be translated into the question it leaves unanswered.

**"Lab tested."** This says a test happened, not what was tested or whether the sample came from your unit. The follow-up is concrete: which laboratory, which method, which lot, and can the report be matched to the package? A seller with a real report can answer all four. A seller with a slogan answers none.

**"Premium grade."** Grade words are internal to a material and a market. "Premium" without a species is a mood. Ask which grade in the trade's own terms, and what distinguishes it physically from the grade below. If the answer is "it's the best," the word has no content.

**"Direct from the source."** This can mean a farm, a cooperative, a dealer, or a reseller who once visited a farm. The useful question is where the material changed hands and which document traveled with it. Directness is a supply-chain claim, and supply chains leave records.

**"Small batch."** Small batches are real and can be excellent. The phrase says nothing about species, grade, or adulteration, and a small batch can be cut as easily as a large one. Ask what makes the batch small — a single harvest, a single distillation, a limited material — and whether the lot number reflects that.

**"No additives."** For a stick, this is close to meaningless without a definition. Binders, water, and processing aids are additives under some definitions and not others. The checkable version is an ingredient list with named materials and a named binder.

**"Traditional recipe."** Tradition describes a method and a lineage, not a material identity. A traditional blend can contain a substituted ingredient and still follow the method. Ask which materials, in which roles, and whether the recipe is historical or inspired by one.

The pattern is not that these phrases are lies. Most are shortcuts that sellers repeat because buyers respond to them. The buyer's move is to accept the phrase as an opening and ask for the fact behind it. A seller who enjoys the subject will usually answer; one who only has slogans will circle.

## When a blend hides the swap

**Direct answer:** A blend is the hardest place to audit a single material, because the named ingredient can be a small fraction of a recipe the seller has no obligation to publish.

"Contains sandalwood" and "sandalwood blend" are different claims. The first says the material is present; the second says it is part of a mixture. Neither says how much. A blend can be honest and still contain only a token amount of the material that gives it its name, because the name describes a character rather than a proportion.

Traditional formula thinking helps here. In a 合香 recipe, each material has a role: chief, minister, assistant, guide. A substitution is judged by whether the replacement performs the role, not by whether it matches a label. A cheaper wood that holds the base is a successful substitution inside the formula. The same wood sold as a single-material product would be a different claim entirely.

For a buyer, the practical question is whether the product promises a material or a character. A single-material stick promises a material, and the label should name it. A blend promises a character, and the label should describe the family without borrowing a prestige name it cannot support. When a blend uses "kyara" as a mood word, the claim has drifted from material to atmosphere, and the price should be judged accordingly.

The burn gives one more clue. A blend built around a real base tends to change across its length; a compound built to smell like that base tends to stay flat. Development is not proof of quality, but the absence of any change is a warning that the scent is a coat rather than a structure.

## Choosing a stand-in

**Direct answer:** A substitution decision starts with the role the material plays in the blend, not with the prestige of its name. Match the role, demand a name, and accept the cheaper material only when it is declared.

| The job | A workable choice | What to refuse | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow creamy base | Sandalwood heartwood powder, or declared *S. spicatum* | A "sandalwood" oil sold as wood | Oil gives an opening, not a base |
| Deep resinous heart | *Aquilaria* at a stated rank | Oiled chips with no papers | Colour and weight are what oiling adds |
| Sweet balsamic fixative | Siam benzoin for smoothness, Sumatra for bite | Unnamed blended "benzoin" | The two gums behave differently |
| Cool top note | Natural borneol with a stated source | 机制冰片 under the bare word 冰片 | The cooling is similar; the origin claim is not |
| Reliable binder | Makko at a tested ratio | "Natural binder" with no name | Binder controls the burn |

Role-first thinking prevents a common mistake: buying by reputation instead of function. A base note needs persistence; a top note needs lift. The same substitute can be wrong for one job and right for the other. The one rule that holds across both is disclosure.

## After a bad purchase

**Direct answer:** A disappointing purchase is more useful as evidence than as a grievance. Keep the packaging, record what was claimed, and separate the material problem from the expectation problem.

First, check the expectation. A stick that smells thin may be a cheap product, or it may be a style you do not like. Compare it with a known sample of the same format before concluding that the material is wrong.

Second, preserve the evidence. Keep the box, the lot code, and the receipt. Photograph the label before it is discarded. If the product was sold with a specific claim — species, rank, origin — write the claim down in the seller's words.

Third, test the simplest hypothesis. If the stick is weak before burning, storage or aromatic load is likely. If it smells harsh when burning, the base or the binder may be the problem. If it smells fine cold and flat hot, a surface coat is likely. Match the symptom to the layer.

Fourth, decide what remedy is proportionate. A cheap box is usually a lesson, not a dispute. A high-value piece with a false species or rank claim is worth a documented conversation, and possibly an independent opinion. The cost of the remedy should be proportional to the loss.

Fifth, record the outcome. A note that a seller answered precisely, or refused to, is worth more than a vague memory of liking or disliking the product. The notebook is the buyer's own reference collection, and it improves with every entry.

## Five-minute audit

**Direct answer:** Before price or appearance, run six checks on the wording.

**Find the plant.** A binomial or a clear species. If the front and back both say only "sandalwood," the label has stopped at a category.

**Find the part.** Heartwood, sapwood, powder, resin, leaf, bud. The part is usually the first detail omitted.

**Find the form.** Wood-based, oil-based, or compound. "Incense" describes a format, not a material.

**Find the binder.** For a stick, the binder is engineering. Makko, a charcoal core, and a synthetic binder produce different burns.

**Find the lot.** A batch code, a packing date, a producer. Without one, any certificate describes a sample rather than your object.

**Find the contradiction.** A scarce material at an implausible price, a top rank with no paper, a famous origin with no record. The contradiction is not proof; it is the place to ask the next question.

Score the label out of six. Four or more answers mean the seller is communicating. Two or fewer mean you are looking at atmosphere. The score is not a quality rating; it tells you how much of the price is being asked for on trust.

## FAQ

### Q: Where does substitution concentrate?

In the expensive end of the trade. Indian sandalwood, agarwood at the kyara rank, natural ambergris, and true frankincense all carry prices far above their nearest usable relatives. The margin pays for ambiguity. Cheaper spices and gums are sometimes mislabeled too, but the motive is weaker and the loss is smaller.

### Q: What is the shortest useful check on a sandalwood stick?

Break one stick and look at the interior. A fibrous, pale centre suggests ground wood; a uniform, darker core suggests a coated or dyed base. Then burn a marked stick and watch the middle: a wood-based stick tends to develop, while a surface coat opens fast and flattens. Neither check names the species, but together they separate a wood product from a scented one.

### Q: Is a declared cheaper material a bad buy?

No. *Santalum spicatum*, Sumatra benzoin, and ambroxide are legitimate materials with their own uses. The problem is an undeclared swap or a price set by a prestige name the contents do not earn. A fair price for a declared relative is a normal transaction.

### Q: Does the choice of benzoin change a formula?

It changes the finish. Siam resin (*Styrax tonkinensis*) is soft, sweet, and powdery; the Sumatra resins (*S. benzoin*, *S. paralleloneurus*) carry a sharper, cinnamic edge. One smooths a base, the other gives it bite. A blend that names only "benzoin" leaves the choice invisible. The [benzoin entity](/ingredients/benzoin/) covers the materials in more detail.

### Q: What does a certificate of analysis settle?

It settles what a named laboratory found in a named sample on a named date, using a stated method. It does not settle whether the sample came from your package unless the lot number matches, and it says nothing about other batches. Read the identity, form, rank, lot, issuer, method, and date before treating the paper as evidence about your purchase.

### Q: Which claims can no home test reach?

Origin and rank. A chip cannot reveal the forest it came from, and the same species can produce several grades whose difference lives in resin distribution rather than surface appearance. Species can sometimes be narrowed by an experienced nose, but confirmation usually needs a laboratory.

## Summary

- Label words do different jobs: category, rank, place, form, and mood.
- Botanical names pin a material; rank and origin need documents.
- Substitution follows price gaps, and the widest gaps sit around sandalwood and agarwood.
- The burn shows behaviour, not identity; paper ties a claim to a lot.
- A declared substitute is a product. An undeclared one is a claim the buyer cannot audit.

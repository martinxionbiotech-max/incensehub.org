**Direct answer:** Agarwood (沉香 chénxiāng) is rated on two separate scales that English-language write-ups keep crushing into one ladder. Scale one is physical — the volume of resin the wood absorbed, read by submerging the piece in water (the 沉水 test). Scale two is structural — whether the fiber converted into the soft, oil-saturated condition named 奇楠 (qínán / "kyara"), or stayed as ordinary resin-veined wood.

**Key facts:** A tag reading "沉水" or "奇楠" is a seller's assertion, not a certificate. The water test reads how *much* resin is present; the 奇楠 label describes what the resin *did to the wood*. Those are two distinct questions, and fusing them into a single "better or worse" axis is the most repeated error in English explanations of the system.

---

## The Two Yardsticks, and Why Mixing Them Causes the Confusion

**Direct answer:** A complete agarwood grade answers two things at once — "how full of resin is this?" and "what condition did that resin leave the fiber in?" — and a label that answers only one has answered only half the question.

Introductory write-ups sketch a single staircase: cheap chips at the bottom, sinking wood in the middle, 奇楠 at the top. That picture persists because it is tidy, not because it is accurate. It breaks the moment you meet a 奇楠 piece that rides high in water, or a heavy sinking piece that smells flat. Those are not freak exceptions — they are the grading system telling you that you were gripping one dial while the material runs on two.

| Question | What it measures | How it is read | Label words |
|---|---|---|---|
| How much resin? | Resin load inside the fiber | Sink, half-sink, or float | 沉水, 半沉半浮 |
| What state is the wood in? | Whether the fiber itself converted | Softness, pliability, cold scent | 奇楠, kyara |

Keep that table in view through the rest of this page. The water test and the 奇楠 divide answer separate questions, and a complete grade has to address both.

## What Is Actually Being Sorted

**Direct answer:** Beneath all this terminology sits the resin-heavy heartwood of *Aquilaria* trees — wood that starts pale and nearly odorless, and only becomes precious after an injury pushes the tree to flood its own damaged tissue with dark resin.

An untouched *Aquilaria* trunk is, in aromatic terms, unremarkable: its heartwood is light in color, light in weight, and carries almost no smell. Agarwood is not a routine product of growth. The tree does not make it the way a pine yields sap as a matter of course; it secretes resin only after something breaches its defenses.

That breach starts the formation chain the entire grading system rests on:

the wood is wounded or a fungus gets in → the tree registers the breach → it exudes defensive resin into the wounded heartwood → the resin saturates the surrounding fiber and plugs the wood's air pockets → over years the tissue darkens, gains mass, and picks up scent → the resin-bearing section is cut out as agarwood.

The chain matters because its two outputs — resin quantity and degree of fiber conversion — are exactly the two dials being measured. Grading, in short, is reading the aftermath of the wound response. The scent chemistry is documented in the specialist literature (see [Naef's review of agarwood volatiles](https://doi.org/10.1002/ffj.2034)); the broader "what is agarwood" picture lives in the [agarwood scent guide](/blog/agarwood-incense/).

## Why the Sorting Has Teeth: The Price Gulf

**Direct answer:** Agarwood grading matters because the price gap between the material's floor and ceiling is so wide — thousands of times — that mislabeling becomes profitable enough to support an entire industry.

Lay the spread out as a rough, directional sketch (a market hint, not a price list — values swing, grades are argued over, and counterfeits inflate the apparent supply):

| Tier | Rough price signal (RMB/g, varies by origin & market) |
|---|---|
| Ordinary medicinal chips (中药铺 grade) | ~0.4–1.4 — a low-cost herb, not a treasure |
| Burnable / gradeable agarwood (可品沉香) | tens to thousands, tracking resin density |
| 沉水 (sinking) agarwood | a steep premium over floating grades of the same piece |
| 奇楠 (qínán / "kyara") | ~3,000–8,000 at market; top white 奇楠 can pass 16,000 |
| India "old mountain" sandalwood (老山檀) | ~10–150, by age and density |
| Plantation sandalwood (澳洲/印尼) | well under ~3 |

The spread is the entire story. When the distance between a medicinal chip and a kyara piece is counted in thousands of times, the payoff for smudging the line between them is enormous — which is why grading and authentication cannot be kept apart. A grade is exactly as honest as the person who assigned it.

## The Water Test, Traced as Cause and Effect

**Direct answer:** The 沉水 test ranks wood by resin load, because resin outweighs plain wood — the fullest pieces sink, and the name 沉香 ("sinking fragrance") writes that test into its own characters.

Skip the rote memorization and follow the mechanism:

injury or infection → the tree exudes resin into the heartwood → resin fills the spaces between the wood fibers → the piece's average density climbs past water's → it sinks.

The bottom tiers are the same chain run backward: a lightly-resined piece keeps its air-filled fiber → it stays buoyant → it floats → it lands below the sinkers.

| Grade | What it does in water | Resin load |
|---|---|---|
| 沉水 (sinks) | Drops to the bottom | Densest, costliest |
| 半沉半浮 (half-sinks) | Sits partway submerged | Middling |
| 浮 (floats) | Rides on top | Lightest (historically 黄熟香) |

Hold one boundary firmly: the water test is a sound rule of thumb for *ordinary* agarwood, but it carries a documented exception at the very top. "It must sink" is a heuristic with an edge case, not a law — that edge case arrives next.

## 奇楠: A Separate State of Matter, Not a Higher Rung

**Direct answer:** 奇楠 (qínán, Japanese "kyara") is not simply "more" ordinary agarwood — it is a structurally different substance. Ordinary 沉香 is "wood wraps oil" (木包油); 奇楠 is "oil wraps wood" (油包木).

- **Ordinary 沉香 = 木包油.** Firm wood threaded with resin veins. The wood is the frame; the oil runs through it.
- **奇楠 = 油包木.** The fiber is converted so thoroughly by resin that the piece turns soft and pliable. The classical phrase is that it "shaves into curls and kneads into pellets" (削之成卷、捏之成丸), and it carries scent even when cold.

This is the divide English material most often drops, and it is the one that counts when you read a grade.

**Direct answer:** The two dials are not swappable — the water test reads resin *concentration*, while 奇楠 marks resin *transformation*.

The wrinkle that exposes the divide: the "sinking equals best" rule holds for ordinary 沉香, but some sources maintain that top 奇楠 is often half-sinking (半沉半浮) rather than fully submerged. The cause is structural — 奇楠's resin is soft and spread uniformly through the fiber instead of packed into dense veins, so a piece can be superb in aroma while not fully sinking. A 奇楠 piece can outscore a denser ordinary piece on every sensory measure and still ride higher in the water. Treat "it must sink" as the ordinary-agarwood rule, never the 奇楠 rule.

## Four Bands Instead of One Ladder

**Direct answer:** Combine the two dials into one table and the material sorts into four recognizable bands — a cleaner map than any single-axis "good to best" staircase.

| Band | Resin load (water test) | Structural state | What you are actually holding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medicinal / commodity | Floats | Wood wraps oil (木包油) | Pale, lightly-resined chips; an herb, not a treasure |
| Gradeable agarwood | Half-sinks to sinks | Wood wraps oil (木包油) | Fragrant resin-veined wood for warming |
| Dense 沉水 | Sinks | Wood wraps oil (木包油) | Heavy, resin-packed pieces; the ceiling of the ordinary tier |
| 奇楠 | Often half-sinks (not always fully) | Oil wraps wood (油包木) | Soft, pliable, cold-fragrant; a different material state |

This four-band view is my own framing, not a classical source — it exists to show how two dials produce four outcomes rather than a single straight line. Note the asymmetry: the top band is defined by transformation, not density. A grader armed with only a water bowl will never see the difference between the third and fourth bands — and that is precisely the difference that matters at the high end.

## How the Resin Forms, and Why the "How" Winds Up in the Grade

**Direct answer:** The formation route — the specific stress that produced the resin — feeds into naming and grading, because different stresses leave behind different resin character and density.

Four classical formation routes:

- **熟结 (shú jié)** — resin from a tree that died and aged, often buried.
- **生结 (shēng jié)** — resin from a tree still living.
- **虫漏 (chóng lòu)** — resin gathered around insect boring.
- **脱落 (tuō luò)** — resin from an exterior wound or break.

These are four origin stories, not four rungs. They cross the density dial and the 奇楠 category: the same measured density can carry a different reputation depending on whether the resin came from a buried dead tree or a bored living one. Grading, then, is partly measurement and partly narrative.

## Cultivated vs. Wild: The Grading Factor English Omits

**Direct answer:** A piece's resin tells you how much resin and what state it is in, but it does not by itself tell you whether the wood came from a wild tree or a plantation — and that distinction changes the price, not the grade.

Natural agarwood forms slowly and unpredictably; a tree may live decades before a wound, if one comes at all, produces resin. Cultivated agarwood shortens that wait by wounding or inoculating plantation trees on purpose, then harvesting after a handful of years. The resulting wood is genuine resin-bearing *Aquilaria*, so it grades on the same two dials — but collectors price wild material differently, partly for aroma reputation and partly for scarcity.

The practical rule for a buyer: "wild" and "cultivated" describe provenance, not a third grading axis. A cultivated piece can sink; a wild piece can be light. When a seller lists "wild" as though it were a grade, treat it as a provenance claim that needs documentation, exactly like an origin claim. See the [agarwood scent guide](/blog/agarwood-incense/) for the formation background.

## The Five-Color 奇楠 Hierarchy (Traditional, and Argued Over)

**Direct answer:** 奇楠 is traditionally split into five color grades — white, green, purple, yellow, black — but sources disagree on whether the purple and yellow tiers are separate grades or just aging stages of the green tier.

| Color | Chinese | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| 白 (white) | 白奇楠 | Highest |
| 绿 (green) | 绿奇楠 / 莺歌绿 | Widely regarded top tier |
| 紫 (purple) | 紫奇楠 / 兰花结 | Disputed — distinct grade or aging stage? |
| 黄 (yellow) | 黄奇楠 / 金丝结 | Disputed — distinct grade or aging stage? |
| 黑 (black) | 黑奇楠 / 铁结 | Lower |

**Direct answer:** This hierarchy is traditional, not settled taxonomy — treat it as possibly non-distinct rather than as established fact.

Color is a naming convention with real market power and shaky evidentiary footing. When a seller leans on a particular color grade to justify a price, the useful question is what the color is actually claimed to signal — and whether it is anything beyond a naming convention. ## A Naming-Trap Table: Words That Get Confused

**Direct answer:** A short list of near-identical labels carries very different meanings in agarwood, and the traps cluster around the words that sound most authoritative.

| Trap word / phrase | What a buyer assumes | What it can actually mean |
|---|---|---|
| "沉香" | Fine, sinking agarwood | Any resin-bearing *Aquilaria* wood, including commodity chips |
| "沉水" | The best there is | Sinks — but oil-injection fakes sink too |
| "奇楠" | Guaranteed top grade | A claim; the identifying features only survive in whole pieces |
| "kyara" | The Japanese synonym | The cognate, with the same grading ambiguity as 奇楠 |
| "old mountain sandalwood" (老山檀) | Ancient, rare wood | A density/age claim that overlaps with graded plantation material |

The pattern matches the one the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/) documents across all incense materials: a prestige word is reused until it stops meaning one thing. The antidote is to ask which dial the word points at — density, or transformation — and refuse to accept a single word as an answer to both.

## The Name 奇楠: An Honest "Uncertain"

**Direct answer:** The origin of the word 奇楠 is genuinely disputed, and this page declines to crown a winner.

The competing accounts:

1. Sanskrit **Kāla** (काल, "black/time").
2. Sanskrit **tagara**, "a fragrant plant," shortened to 伽罗/伽楠.
3. A **"temple" (伽蓝)** reading.

More rigorous 考证 argues that **tagara is a mistranslation** — the Sanskrit word for agarwood is **agaru / aguru**, not tagara. The honest position is "etymology uncertain; multiple theories." The Japanese "kyara" (伽罗) is the cognate and carries the same ambiguity.

## How Counterfeits Attack Each Signal

**Direct answer:** The same logic that grades genuine agarwood also grades the fakes, because fakes are built to defeat the exact signals the system leans on.

Lay out the counterfeit playbook as a chain:

oil-boiling or oil-injection adds weight → the treated wood sinks → the water test passes → the density reads as high grade.

dye-soaking darkens the wood → it imitates the look of resin density → the eye is fooled before the nose gets consulted.

synthetic fragrance is added → the aroma clears a first sniff → a buyer who never warms the material never catches the deception.

That is why a grade label alone is a claim, and authentication is the check on whether the material backs the claim. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) and the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

## Reading a Seller's Grade: A Decision Table

**Direct answer:** Turn a grade label into questions rather than accepting it as an answer.

| Label claim | Question to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| "Sinking" | Verified how? | Oil-injection fakes are engineered to sink |
| "奇楠" | On what basis — soft/pliable/room-temperature-fragrant, or just price? | The identifying features only survive in whole pieces |
| Origin named? | Which region? | A specific origin is more informative than "premium agarwood" |
| Price vs grade | Consistent? | A 奇楠 label at ordinary prices is a contradiction |

The label is a starting point for questions, not an ending point for trust.

## Grading Vocabulary Across Traditions

**Direct answer:** Chinese, Japanese, and Vietnamese traditions sort the same material with different vocabularies — knowing they overlap helps you read across sources.

| Tradition | System | What it grades |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese | sink/half-sink/float (沉水) + 奇楠 color grades | Resin density and the 奇楠 distinction |
| Japanese | rikkoku gomi (六国五味) | Six "countries," five "tastes" — a classical descriptive framework |
| Vietnamese | regional/origin grades (e.g., Nha Trang) | Origin and aroma reputation |

The Japanese rikkoku gomi "countries" are traditional labels, not verified modern geography — much like the Chinese 奇楠 color hierarchy. Neither should be read as a modern provenance certificate. For the Japanese side, see [kōdō](/blog/japanese-incense-kodo/).

## What Else a Grader Weighs

**Direct answer:** Density is the most objective dial, but aroma is what collectors actually pay for — grading is partly measurement and partly tasting.

Beyond the water test, a grader weighs several factors at once:

- **Resin density** — the core axis, read by sink/float and visible oil content.
- **Oil distribution** — veins through hard wood (木包油) versus saturation of the fiber (油包木).
- **Aroma on gentle heat** — is it complex and sweet, and does it open in stages (头香/本香/尾香)?
- **Formation type** — 熟结 / 生结 / 虫漏 / 脱落.
- **Origin** — a Hainan or Vietnamese piece and a plantation piece can carry very different prices for similar density.

**Direct answer:** A dense but dull-smelling piece does not automatically beat a lighter but vivid one — the measurable backbone and the sensory judgment run on separate tracks.

In that respect agarwood grading resembles tea or wine grading: there is a backbone you can measure, and a final judgment that is unavoidably subjective at the top end. The measurable part gets you close; the tasting part separates a merely dense piece from a memorable one.

## A Field Sequence for Walking Through a Piece

**Direct answer:** Five steps, in order, keep the objective test first and the subjective test decisive.

1. **Sink test** — does it sink, half-sink, or float? A first read on resin concentration, with the 奇楠 caveat noted above.
2. **Look at the resin** — veins through hard wood (ordinary 沉香) or saturation of soft, pliable fiber (奇楠)?
3. **Smell it warm** — on gentle heat, is the aroma complex and sweet, and does it open in stages?
4. **Note formation and origin** — 熟结 / 生结 / 虫漏 / 脱落, and the claimed region.
5. **Check price against grade** — do the label, the material, and the price tell the same story?

The sequence matters because it keeps the objective test (density) first and the subjective test (aroma) decisive, without letting either carry the judgment alone. It also shows where the fakes strike: at the sink test, at the color, and at the price-to-grade consistency.

## The Author's View

**Direct answer:** The water test is the single most useful and the single most overrated signal in agarwood grading — because the counterfeiter builds for it.

The test is objective in a way nothing else on the label is: the piece sinks or it does not, and there is no argument. But that same objectivity is what makes it the counterfeit's favorite target. An oil-injection fake exists to sink; it defeats the test at the exact moment the test looks most scientific. My position: for ordinary agarwood, the water test is a decent first filter and a poor last word — and for 奇楠, it is the wrong instrument entirely. The better first question is how the material smells when gently warmed, and who is selling it to me.

## The Sink-Test Physics, Run in Reverse

**Direct answer:** The 沉水 test is usually read forward — resin makes wood sink — but run it backward and it exposes its own blind spot: a fake engineered to sink defeats the very signal that makes the test look objective.

Trace the genuine chain first, then its mirror. The honest path:

injury or infection → the tree floods its heartwood with resin → resin fills the air pockets between fibers → density climbs past water's → the piece sinks.

The counterfeiter's mirror runs the same chain without the tree:

oil or resin is injected or boiled into ordinary wood → the wood's air pockets fill with added weight → density climbs past water's → the piece sinks → the water test reads "high grade" on a manufactured lie.

The two chains end at the same visible result — a piece that sinks — and that is the whole lesson. The sink test measures density, and density is the one thing a counterfeiter can fabricate for a fee. The test is objective only about what it measures; it is silent about how the density got there, and the difference between the two chains is exactly the difference between agarwood and a wet stick. That is why the water test is a first filter, never a verdict.

## A Formation-Route Cross-Table

**Direct answer:** The four formation routes — 熟结, 生结, 虫漏, 脱落 — cross the density dial and the 奇楠 category independently, and a cross-table shows the two axes do not line up.

| Formation route | What it describes | How it maps to density | How it maps to 奇楠 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 熟结 (shú jié) | Resin from a dead, aged, often buried tree | Can be light or dense | Can be ordinary or 奇楠 |
| 生结 (shēng jié) | Resin from a still-living tree | Usually firmer, lighter | Usually ordinary 沉香 |
| 虫漏 (chóng lòu) | Resin around insect boring | Variable | Can reach 奇楠 character |
| 脱落 (tuō luò) | Resin from an exterior wound or break | Variable | Usually ordinary 沉香 |

The table's point is that the route and the grade are two different questions. The same measured density can carry a different reputation depending on whether the resin came from a buried dead tree or a bored living one — and the 奇楠 transformation can arrive by more than one route. Grading is part measurement and part narrative, and the narrative does not sit on the same axis as the sink test.

## The Misconception: "Sinking" Is Not a Synonym for "Best"

**Direct answer:** The most repeated error in English agarwood writing is to treat the sink test as a complete ranking — but "sinking" names a density, not a verdict, and at the very top of the material the rule bends.

The staircase picture — floating at the bottom, sinking in the middle, 奇楠 at the top — is tidy and wrong. It breaks the moment you meet either of two common pieces:

a heavy sinking piece that smells flat → dense, but dull on gentle heat → the water test would crown it and the nose would demote it.

a 奇楠 piece that rides half-sunk → superb aroma, soft and cold-fragrant → the water test would demote it and the nose would crown it.

Both exist, and both tell the same thing: the sink test answers one question — how much resin — while "best" is a judgment that also weighs aroma, transformation, and origin. My position: for ordinary 沉香, the sink test is a decent first filter and a poor last word; for 奇楠 it is the wrong instrument entirely. The better first question is how the material smells when gently warmed, and who is selling it.

## The Oil-Distribution Chain: Why 油包木 vs 木包油 Matters Visually

**Direct answer:** The difference between ordinary 沉香 and 奇楠 is visible as well as tactile — one shows veins of oil running through firm wood, the other shows the fiber itself saturated — and that visual split is the same physical difference the names encode.

Follow what the resin does to the wood's structure in each case. The two outcomes of the wound response diverge in where the resin ends up:

in ordinary 沉香 → the resin deposits along the vessels and gaps → it forms veins through a hard, unchanged fiber → the wood stays firm and keeps its skeleton → the piece reads "wood with oil in it."

in 奇楠 → the resin saturates the fiber itself → the wood's structure softens and swells with oil → the piece turns pliable and cold-fragrant → the piece reads "oil with wood in it."

The visual test mirrors the tactile one: look for veins versus saturation. A piece that shows distinct dark streaks through pale wood is ordinary 沉香; a piece that reads uniformly soft and oily throughout, with no clear boundary between wood and resin, is pointing toward 奇楠. The eye and the hand are reading the same fact — how thoroughly the resin converted the fiber.

## The Judgment Boundary: Where the Water Test Stops Being Useful

**Direct answer:** The water test earns its keep in the ordinary tier and loses it at the top — the boundary is the 奇楠 line, and crossing it means putting the bowl down and picking up the nose.

The sink test has a domain, and outside that domain it misleads. Draw the line clearly:

for ordinary 沉香 → density tracks resin load → sinking is a meaningful signal → the bowl is a fair first filter.

for 奇楠 → the resin is soft and spread, not packed into dense veins → top pieces often half-sink → the bowl reads "lighter" where the nose reads "better."

The practical rule for a buyer or collector: use the water test to sort the ordinary tier, and switch to warm-aroma and texture the moment 奇楠 enters the conversation. A grader who refuses to cross that line will rank a superb half-sinking 奇楠 below a dense flat 沉香 — which is not a fine distinction but a full inversion. The instrument must match the tier.

## The Aroma Ladder of a Warmed Piece

**Direct answer:** Agarwood is judged on heat in three successive waves — 头香 (the head), 本香 (the body), and 尾香 (the tail) — and a piece's rank is read as much from how those waves unfold as from any single measurement.

The warm test is temporal, not static. A sliver on gentle heat releases its character in order:

the head note arrives first → light, quick, the first impression → it fades as the body builds → the body is the piece's core character, the resinous middle → it holds for the bulk of the warming → the tail follows, slower and deeper → the tail is what remains as the heat fades.

A piece that opens beautifully but dies at the tail is incomplete; a piece whose three waves are all present and balanced is the real article. The ladder is why a single sniff never grades agarwood — the material is a sequence, not a chord, and the grader who stops at the head has judged only the opening act. The full read is the three waves together, in order.

## A Buyer's Decision Table: Matching the Question to the Grade

**Direct answer:** Different buyers need different answers from a grade, and a table that maps the buying goal onto the right signal prevents the most common mismatch — paying for a label that answers a question you did not ask.

| What you are actually buying for | The signal that matters | The signal that misleads |
|---|---|---|
| Aroma on gentle heat | The three-wave warm read | The sink test alone |
| Density / resin load | The 沉水 sink test | The five-color name |
| The 奇楠 transformation | Softness, pliability, cold scent | The sink test |
| Provenance | A documented origin and formation route | "Premium agarwood" |
| Investment-grade rarity | Documented grade plus authentication | A color name alone |

The table's logic is one sentence: pick the signal that answers your actual question, and ignore the one that answers someone else's. A buyer chasing aroma who fixes on the sink test, or a buyer chasing 奇楠 who fixes on a color name, is reading the wrong dial — and the mismatch is where the overpay hides. The grade is only as useful as the question it is matched to.

## The Origin Question, Read as a Reputation, Not a Certificate

**Direct answer:** A named origin — Hainan, Nha Trang, and the like — is a reputation claim, not a laboratory fact, and it should be read the way collectors read a vineyard: as a hint, not a guarantee.

The origin names carry real market weight and no verification built in. Trace what an origin label does and does not do:

a named region → associated with a certain aroma reputation → collectors pay for that reputation → the name predicts a style, not a grade → the same region yields flat pieces and fine pieces alike.

Read the origin the way you would read a wine's appellation: it tells you where the material came from and what character to expect, but it does not certify that this particular piece is good. A Hainan label on a dull piece is a dull piece with a Hainan label. The reputation is real; the individual lot is the only thing you can actually smell. Origin narrows the search, not the verdict.

## The Counterfeit Chain, Rebuilt Step by Step

**Direct answer:** Fakes are not random — they are built to defeat specific grading signals in order, and laying the playbook out as a chain shows where each defense is aimed.

Each counterfeit method targets one signal, and the sequence maps onto the grader's own checklist:

the water test is defeated first → oil-boiling or injection adds weight → the treated wood sinks → the density signal reads high.

the eye is defeated next → dye darkens the wood → it mimics resin saturation → the visual signal reads rich.

the nose is defeated last → synthetic fragrance is added → the first sniff clears → a buyer who never warms the material never catches it.

Read the chain backward and it tells you where to test. Because the first two forgeries are cheap and the third is the weak point, the warm-aroma test is the one the counterfeiter fears — it is the signal that costs the most to fake. ## The Weighing-Room Sequence: What a Grader Does in Order

**Direct answer:** A working grader moves through a fixed order — sink, look, warm, note, price — and the order is not arbitrary, because each step's result tells you which dial to read next.

The sequence is a decision tree, not a checklist. Walk it once:

sink the piece first → the water read sorts density → it tells you whether you are in the ordinary tier or looking at something that needs the 奇楠 question → look at the resin next → veins mean ordinary 沉香, saturation points toward 奇楠 → warm it third → the three waves confirm or overturn the visual read → note formation and origin fourth → they add the narrative the measurements lack → check price against grade last → the label, the material, and the number must agree.

The order earns its place because each early step narrows what the later steps must answer. A piece that sinks and shows saturation but smells flat on heat has just told the grader something the sink test alone would have missed — and that is why the nose comes after the bowl, not before it. The sequence is the grader's whole method in miniature.

## The Cold-Scent Signal: 奇楠's Room-Temperature Tell

**Direct answer:** The cleanest 奇楠 signal is that it smells before any heat is applied — a cold, room-temperature fragrance that ordinary 沉香 does not show — and that cold scent is what the classical phrase about shaving and kneading is pointing at.

The cold test is the fastest way to separate the two states, and it follows from the structural difference:

ordinary 沉香 → the resin sits in veins through firm wood → little evaporates without heat → the cold piece reads nearly silent.

奇楠 → the fiber is saturated with soft, volatile oil → the scent escapes even at room temperature → the cold piece reads fragrant before a match is struck.

The classical description — "shaves into curls and kneads into pellets" — is the same fact read by hand: soft, pliable, oil-saturated fiber that behaves more like a resin than a wood. Cold scent and soft texture are two names for one condition, and that condition is the 奇楠 divide. A piece that is fragrant cold, soft to the hand, and only half-sinking in water is not a contradiction; it is 奇楠 behaving exactly as its structure predicts.

## The Wet-Weight Trap: Why a Freshly Cut Piece Reads Dense

**Direct answer:** A piece still carrying moisture sinks or rides lower than it will once dried, so a water test run on freshly cut wood overstates the resin load — and the trap is that the test looks objective while the moisture is quietly padding it.

The sink test assumes a dry piece, and freshly cut wood is not dry. The moisture works against the test's honesty:

freshly cut wood holds bound water in its fibers → that water adds weight → the piece sinks or rides low → the density reading is inflated by water, not resin → once dried, the same piece floats higher and grades lower.

The correction is procedural, not theoretical: dry the piece before you sink it, or at least know the test was run wet. A seller who sinks a green, freshly cut piece and quotes the result as a grade is letting water do the resin's job. The water test is only as honest as the dryness of what it drops in the bowl.

## The Resin-Vein Geometry: Why Distribution Beats Total

**Direct answer:** Two pieces can carry the same total resin and grade differently, because how the resin is distributed — as concentrated veins or as even saturation — changes the burn and the scent more than the raw quantity does.

Total resin is one number; distribution is another, and the second matters more on heat. Trace the two geometries:

resin packed into dense veins → the oil sits in concentrated channels → the burn flares where a vein is hit and stalls between → the scent reads patchy, bright then thin.

resin spread evenly through the fiber → the oil saturates the whole mass → the burn runs steady across the surface → the scent reads even and continuous.

That is why a visually veined piece and a saturated piece of the same measured density do not smell alike, and why the 奇楠 state — even saturation — is prized above raw density. The distribution is the material's real structure; the total is just its accounting. A grader who counts oil without seeing how it lies has read the number and missed the wood.

## The Time-Dimension of Grading: Why Fresh vs Aged Agarwood Diverges

**Direct answer:** Agarwood changes after harvest — fresh pieces are rawer and sharper, aged pieces rounder and deeper — and the grading that ignores the time axis is grading a moving object.

The piece is not finished when it is cut. Its character continues to shift as it rests:

freshly harvested agarwood → carries residual moisture and bright, raw top notes → the scent reads green and slightly aggressive.

aged agarwood → the moisture leaves, the volatiles settle → the scent reads rounder, deeper, more coherent → the same piece grades differently a year later.

The practical point for a buyer: a piece's grade is a snapshot, not a constant. A fresh lot that reads harsh may settle into something fine; an aged lot that reads smooth has already done its settling. Judging a freshly cut piece against an aged benchmark is comparing two different points on one curve — and the curve, not the snapshot, is what the material actually is.

## The Sensory Vocabulary: A Reader's Map of Descriptors

**Direct answer:** Agarwood grading leans on a small set of sensory words — sweet, cooling, woody, resinous, and a few more — and a reader who learns what each points to stops being steered by adjectives alone.

| Descriptor | What it points to | The caution |

A buyer who holds the map can read a seller's description as a set of testable claims rather than a cloud of flattery — and the testable claim is the only kind the material can actually answer.

## The Heating-Vessel Difference: Charcoal vs Electric vs Mica

**Direct answer:** Agarwood reads differently on charcoal, on an electric heater, and on a mica plate, because each tool delivers a different temperature and pace — and the grade a piece earns can shift with the tool it is read on.

The tool is part of the measurement, not a neutral backdrop. Three common warm-up routes diverge:

charcoal buried in ash → hot and fast → it lifts the bright top notes first and can scorch the surface → the piece reads sharp and short-lived.

an electric heater with a thermostat → low and steady → the resins release slowly without burning → the piece reads round and full.

a mica plate over charcoal → an even sheet of heat → it tempers the charcoal's spike → the read sits between the two extremes.

The same sliver on charcoal and on a low electric heater can sound like two different grades — the charcoal emphasizes the head, the heater the body and tail. A grader who quotes a result without naming the tool has left out half the measurement. Hold the tool constant, and the grade means something; vary it, and the grade measures the heater as much as the wood.

## The Burn vs Warm Distinction: Why Some Pieces Are for the Fire and Some for the Heater

**Direct answer:** Agarwood splits by intended use — some pieces are meant to be burned as incense, others only warmed to release scent — and a grade that does not say which is being read is incomplete.

The material has two jobs, and they pull the grading in different directions:

burning-grade agarwood → chosen for how it smolders and fills a room with smoke → the grade weighs the burn's evenness and the smoke's character.

warming-grade agarwood → chosen for how it releases on gentle heat without igniting → the grade weighs the three waves and the tail, with no smoke at all.

The two are not interchangeable, and a piece can score high on one and low on the other. A dense, smoky piece may be superb burned and unremarkable warmed; a soft 奇楠 may be wasted in the fire and sublime on a heater. A label that says only "high grade" without saying high grade *for what* is leaving the use out of the grade — and the use is half the question.

## The Buyer's Timeline: When to Buy, When to Wait

**Direct answer:** Agarwood buying rewards patience twice over — a piece that reads harsh fresh may round out with aging, and a buyer who waits for the right piece, rather than the first available one, buys better.

The material's time-axis, noted earlier, has a purchasing corollary. Two kinds of patience pay:

patience with the piece → a freshly harvested lot reads green and sharp → left to rest, it settles → a buyer who rejects it instantly may have rejected a fine piece a year too early.

patience with the market → the same grade appears repeatedly, at different prices and different honesty → a buyer who compares across sellers and waits for documentation rather than grabbing the first "奇楠" label buys materially better.

The timeline lesson is that agarwood is not a fast market and does not reward a fast buyer. The piece changes after harvest, and the buyer's own knowledge changes with each comparison. Both curves favor the person who waits — for the material to settle and for the right lot to surface.

## The Label's Load: What a Grade Tag Actually Carries

**Direct answer:** A grade tag carries three separate claims at once — what the material is, how much of it there is, and what it is worth — and a buyer who reads all three as one is accepting a bundle that was never verified as a whole.

The tag is a compound assertion, and each part needs its own check. Unpack it:

the identity claim → "this is 奇楠" or "this is 沉水" → checked by texture, cold scent, and the water test.

the quantity claim → "this piece weighs this much and holds this grade" → checked by the scale and the density read.

the value claim → "therefore it is worth this price" → checked by comparing against documented lots, not by trusting the tag.

A buyer who treats the tag as one sealed unit has skipped two of the three checks. The tag is a hypothesis, not a finding — and the whole discipline of grading exists to test the hypothesis against the material. Read it as three claims, check each, and the tag becomes what it should be: a starting point for questions, never an ending point for trust.

## The Smoke Character: Grading What the Burn Releases

**Direct answer:** Burned agarwood is graded partly on its smoke — how it rises, how it smells in the air, and how it settles — because the smoke is the material's delivery system, not a byproduct to be ignored.

The smoke is where burned agarwood actually reaches the nose, so it carries its own signals. Watch and smell it in three acts:

the smoke's body → thin and pale reads clean, thick and dark reads heavy or adulterated → the column is a first visual read.

the smoke's scent → the aroma in the air, not just on the sliver → a piece that smells fine up close but flat in the room is half the material it seemed.

the smoke's finish → how it lingers and what it leaves → a clean, fading trace reads better than an acrid, clinging one.

The smoke character is a distinct axis from density and transformation, and it is the axis a burner, rather than a collector, most needs. A grade that only quotes the sink test and the color has said nothing about how the piece actually performs in the air — and for a material that is burned, the air is where it lives. ## The Piece-by-Piece Reality: Why a Batch Grade Is a Statistical Claim

**Direct answer:** A lot is not one grade — it is a spread of pieces, and a seller's single grade for a batch is a summary of that spread, which is why buying by the lot and buying by the piece are two different purchases.

Grading a batch and grading a piece answer different questions. The difference:

a single piece → its density, texture, and warm read are fixed → the grade describes one object → it can be checked directly.

a batch → pieces vary in density, formation, and aroma → the quoted grade is a central tendency, not a description of any one piece → the buyer gets the spread, not the headline.

The practical rule: a batch grade is a statistical claim, and the buyer should ask where the piece they are holding sits in the spread. A "沉水 lot" may contain pieces that float; a "奇楠 lot" may contain ordinary 沉香 at the edges. ## The Authentication Sequence, for a Collector

**Direct answer:** A collector authenticates in a fixed order — provenance first, then the physical tests, then the warm read, then the price — because each later step only means something if the earlier one has not already failed.

The sequence works because each step can stop the process early. A piece that fails provenance still gets tested — but the collector already knows the stakes. ## The Harvest-Versus-Resin Age Question

**Direct answer:** A piece's age and its resin's age are two different clocks — the wood may be old while the resin formed recently, or vice versa — and confusing the two is a quiet source of misgrading.

The word "aged" slides between two meanings. Pull them apart:

the tree's age → how long the trunk lived → it shapes the wood's structure and density.

the resin's age → how long ago the wound triggered the resin → it shapes the oil's character and depth.

A young tree can carry old resin; an old tree can carry fresh resin. A label that says only "old agarwood" has not said which clock it means, and the two point at different qualities. The formation route — 熟结 from a long-dead tree versus 生结 from a living one — is partly this distinction, dressed in classical terms. The buyer's question is which age is being claimed, and whether the evidence supports that one specifically.

## The Color-Chart Trap, and the Word That Carries It

**Direct answer:** Color is the most visually seductive and least reliable agarwood signal, because it can be darkened with dye, lightened with treatment, and described with prestige words that do no measuring at all.

The eye is the counterfeiter's first target and the buyer's first reflex. The trap runs:

darker reads as richer → dye darkens pale wood → the visual signal is manufactured → the buyer pays for color that cost pennies to add.

The deeper issue is that color is a cosmetic axis, not a grading axis. It loosely correlates with oil in genuine material, but the correlation is loose enough that any single piece can break it. The antidote is the same rule repeated through this page: watch color, never trust it. The sink test, the texture, and the warm read are the real instruments; color is the one signal the seller can repaint, and the buyer should know it.

## The Wound-Response Detail: Why the Trigger Matters to the Grade

**Direct answer:** Not all resin is equal, because not all wounds are equal — the trigger that started the resin shapes its character, and the formation route is the classical record of that trigger.

The tree makes resin in response to stress, and the stress leaves a signature. Trace the triggers:

a fungal entry → the tree fights an infection → the resin is a defense → it tends to run deep and complex.

an insect bore → the tree seals the tunnel → the resin concentrates around the wound → it reads dense and localized.

a physical break → the tree scabs the breach → the resin is a scar → it reads variable, often lighter.

The formation route — 熟结, 生结, 虫漏, 脱落 — is the classical shorthand for these triggers. The grade is partly the aftermath of the wound, and the route is the record of what kind of wound it was. A buyer who knows the route knows something about the resin's likely character that density alone never reveals.

## The Market's Two Voices: Aroma Reputation vs Measured Grade

**Direct answer:** The agarwood market speaks in two registers at once — a measurable grade and an aroma reputation — and a buyer who hears only one is pricing half the material.

The two registers describe different things and sometimes disagree. Lay them side by side:

the measured grade → density, sink test, 奇楠 state → objective, repeatable, but silent about how the piece actually smells.

the aroma reputation → region, formation, the three-wave read → subjective, sensory, but hard to quote on a tag.

The price is set where the two meet, and that meeting point is where the arguments happen. A buyer who pays for density alone misses the aroma, and one who pays for reputation alone can be sold density the piece does not have. ## The Grading-By-Ear Risk: How Descriptions Drift

**Direct answer:** Agarwood grades drift in retelling, because a sensory description passed from seller to seller loses precision the way a rumor does — and the buyer should hold descriptions loosely until the material itself is on the table.

Grading is partly a written and spoken tradition, and language is lossy. Trace what happens to a grade as it moves through hands:

a grader smells a piece → writes "sweet, cooling, top-tier" → the next seller repeats "top-tier" → the buyer hears only the rank, not the notes → the precision that justified the rank is gone, and only the rank travels.

The drift is why a buyer should insist on the material, not the description. ## The Small-Sample Rule: Why One Piece Cannot Grade a Seller

**Direct answer:** A single good or bad piece proves little about a seller, because agarwood varies piece to piece even within one honest lot — the buyer's judgment should track the seller across samples, not a single draw.

One piece is a sample of one, and the material's own variance makes it a weak sample. The reasoning:

a lot is a spread of pieces → any single draw can land high or low by chance → one good piece does not make a seller honest → one bad piece does not make a seller a fraud → only a pattern across several purchases is evidence.

The practical habit: buy small, compare, and judge the seller on the pattern, not the draw. A seller who delivers consistently across three or four samples has earned a read; a seller judged on one lucky or unlucky piece has not. ## The Final Frame: Two Dials, One Material, No Shortcut

**Direct answer:** Everything in agarwood grading reduces to two dials — resin load and fiber transformation — and the buyer who refuses the single-ladder shortcut, and reads both, is the one who grades honestly.

## The Resin's Own Ladder: From Sap to Scent

**Direct answer:** The resin that makes agarwood precious passes through stages of its own — first a wound response, then a slow saturation, then a chemical ripening — and the grade is a read of where on that ladder the piece stopped.

The tree's resin is not one substance but a process. Follow it from trigger to finished wood:

the wound or infection triggers a defense → the tree secretes raw resin at the site → over years the resin spreads and saturates the surrounding fiber → the volatile compounds ripen and complexify → the scent deepens from raw and green to rich and layered.

A piece harvested early sits low on that ladder — light, raw, sharp; a piece harvested late sits high — dense, ripened, deep. The grade is, in part, a guess at where the ladder stopped when the wood was cut. That is why formation route and age matter: they are the classical proxies for a position on the ripening ladder that no modern tag can measure directly.

## The Density Dial's Floor and Ceiling

**Direct answer:** The density dial has a real floor and a real ceiling — below the floor is commodity wood, above the ceiling is the 奇楠 state — and the middle band between them is where the ordinary grade actually lives.

## The Water-Bowl Limits: What the Test Cannot See

## The Warm-Read Protocol, Written Down

**Direct answer:** The warm read is repeatable, and writing the protocol down turns a vague sniff into a consistent test — same heat, same distance, same three notes, same order, every time.

The protocol is deliberately boring, and the boredom is the point — a repeatable test is the only kind whose result can be trusted a week later or against another piece.

## The Small-Piece Arithmetic: Why Size Skews the Read

**Direct answer:** The size of a piece changes every test run on it — a small sliver sinks and warms differently from a large block — and a grade quoted without the piece's size has left out a variable that moves every number.

The piece's dimensions are not neutral. Trace what size does to each test:

a large block → its surface-to-volume ratio is low → it warms slowly and holds heat → the body and tail dominate the read.

the sink test → a thin sliver can trap air and float deceptively → a chunky piece sinks on its own mass → the same resin load reads differently at two sizes.

A grader who quotes a warm read or a sink result without the piece's size is quoting a number that belongs to that particular piece, not to the material generally. Size is part of the measurement — hold it constant when comparing, and note it when it changes. The same lot, cut two ways, can look like two grades.

## The Comparison Habit: Grading Against a Known Reference

**Direct answer:** A buyer's most reliable instrument is a reference piece — a sample of known grade kept on hand — because grading against a fixed reference beats grading against memory every time.

The nose remembers poorly and compares well. The reference method:

keep a small, known piece → one whose grade you have already established by full testing → when a new piece arrives, warm both side by side → the difference between them is the grade of the new piece → no memory required.

The reference turns a vague judgment into a direct comparison, which is the same principle the whole page has leaned on: the material reads best against a known quantity, not against a recollection. A buyer who owns even one well-characterized reference piece has upgraded every future purchase, because "better than my reference" and "worse than my reference" are verdicts the nose can actually deliver.

## The Curing-After-Harvest Question, for Agarwood

**Direct answer:** Agarwood, like the woods it resembles, settles after harvest — the sharp top notes dull and the deeper body comes forward — and a buyer should know that a fresh piece's grade is a moving target.

The harvest is not the finish line. The piece keeps changing once cut:

freshly cut agarwood → carries residual moisture and bright, raw volatiles → the note reads green and forward → the grade, read now, skews toward the head.

rested agarwood → the moisture leaves, the volatiles settle → the note reads rounder and deeper → the grade, read a year later, skews toward the body and tail.

The practical read: a piece judged harsh on arrival may be a piece judged fine after a season of rest, and a seller who grades a fresh cut against an aged benchmark is comparing two points on one curve. The buyer's patience, noted earlier, applies here too — the material, and its grade, are still moving when the box is opened.

## The One-Word Antidote: Ask "On What Basis?"

**Direct answer:** The single most useful question in agarwood buying is three words — "on what basis?" — asked of every grade, color, and origin claim, because it forces the seller back to an instrument instead of a word.

The question is the buyer's whole defense, compressed. Deploy it against every claim:

"sinking" → on what basis? → verified how, by whom, on a dry piece? → the answer or its absence tells you what the claim is worth.

"奇楠" → on what basis? → soft, pliable, cold-fragrant, or just a price? → the basis is the difference between a fact and a fee.

"premium" or "rare" → on what basis? → density, transformation, aroma, or scarcity alone? → the basis is where the money hides.

The question works because a grade is only as good as its basis, and most retail tags have none. A seller who can answer "on what basis" has done the work; a seller who cannot is selling a word. Ask it every time, and the market's most common overcharge loses its cover.

## The Four Routes, Read as Risk Profiles

**Direct answer:** The four formation routes carry different risk profiles for a buyer — a 熟结 piece from a dead, aged tree is a different purchase from a 生结 piece from a living one — and the route is a clue to what you are betting on.

Each route implies a different story, and each story carries a different uncertainty. Lay the four out as risk profiles:

熟结 (shú jié) → resin from a tree that died and aged, often buried → the longest, deepest formation → reputation for depth → also the hardest to verify, because the tree is gone.

生结 (shēng jié) → resin from a still-living tree → a clearer, more checkable provenance → often lighter and firmer → a more transparent, if less storied, purchase.

虫漏 (chóng lòu) → resin around insect boring → localized and dense → a specific, recognizable character → the trigger is visible if the piece is whole.

脱落 (tuō luò) → resin from an exterior wound → variable and often lighter → the most ordinary of the four routes.

The route is a bet on what the resin is and how it got there. A buyer who knows the route knows what evidence to ask for — a living-tree provenance is checkable, a buried-tree claim is mostly a story — and that is the difference between buying a fact and buying a legend.

## The Shape of the Wound, and Why It Shows

**Direct answer:** The resin follows the shape of the wound that made it, so a whole piece often shows its formation route in its own structure — a bored tunnel, a broken edge, a uniform saturation — and reading that shape is a free clue.

The wound leaves geometry behind. Look at a whole piece and the trigger is often legible:

a bored tunnel → a clean, round channel where an insect worked → the resin hugs the tunnel wall → the piece reads as 虫漏.

a broken or split edge → the resin concentrated at the breach → the piece reads as 脱落 or a wound scar.

a uniform saturation with no clear wound → the resin spread evenly through the fiber → the piece points toward the 奇楠 condition rather than a single wound.

The shape is a clue, not a certificate — treatment and cutting can obscure it — but on an uncut piece it is the most direct evidence of how the resin formed. A buyer who can read the wound's shape is reading the material's own record, written before any label was printed.

## The Counterfeit's Economy: Why the Water Test Is the Cheapest to Beat

**Direct answer:** The counterfeiter attacks the cheapest signal to fake first — and the water test is that signal, because adding weight costs pennies while faking a warm read costs real skill.

The economics of forgery explain the playbook. Rank the signals by what they cost to fake:

the sink test → cheapest to fake → oil or resin injection adds weight for almost nothing → the density signal is the first to fall.

the visual read → cheap to fake → dye darkens pale wood → the eye is fooled for pennies.

the warm read → expensive to fake → a convincing three-wave aroma needs real material or sophisticated synthetics → the nose is the last line that holds.

That ranking is why the counterfeiter's playbook, laid out earlier, runs in exactly this order — sink, then look, then smell. The buyer's defense is the mirror: spend the most trust on the test that costs the most to fake. The warm read is expensive for the forger, which is precisely why it is the buyer's most reliable instrument. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

## The Drying-Before-Testing Rule, Applied to the Shop

**Direct answer:** A piece bought still carrying moisture will read denser and grade higher than the same piece dried — so the buyer's first move at a stall is to ask, or infer, whether the material is dry.

The moisture caveat from earlier has a shop-floor form. Before trusting any sink result or weight read:

wet or freshly cut wood → holds water in its fibers → the water adds weight → the piece sinks or rides low → the grade is inflated.

dry, settled wood → the water is gone → only the resin and fiber remain → the piece floats where it actually belongs → the grade is honest.

The buyer's move is to notice the material's condition before trusting its numbers. A piece that feels cool and damp, or that was recently cut, is reading a water bonus on the scale and in the bowl. Dry it, or discount the read — and treat a seller who sinks a green piece without saying so as one who is letting moisture do the resin's work.

## The Origin-and-Formation Pairing: Two Names, One Story

**Direct answer:** Origin and formation route are often quoted together, and they should be read together — a Hainan 虫漏 and a Hainan 脱落 are two different bets, and the pairing is what carries the real information.

The two labels combine into a single story, and the combination matters more than either alone. Read them as a pair:

origin alone → "Hainan" → a reputation for a cooling, refined style → but no information about the wound or the resin's age.

formation alone → "虫漏" → a bored-tunnel resin → but no information about where the tree grew.

origin plus formation → "Hainan 虫漏" → a specific regional style, formed by a specific trigger → a much fuller picture than either name alone.

The pairing is the unit of meaning. A buyer who reads origin and formation as one compound claim gets a real description; a buyer who reads them as two separate, interchangeable adjectives loses exactly the combination that tells the piece's story. The names are meant to be held together.

Four lines, and they hold every error this page has walked through. The staircase is the mistake; the two dials are the correction. The bowl is one instrument, not the whole kit. ## The Wet-Weight Correction, in One Move

**Direct answer:** A buyer can correct for a wet piece's inflated density in one move — let it dry before believing the sink result — because the water bonus disappears with the water, and the grade drops with it.

The correction is procedural and costs only time. The single move:

take the piece home or set it aside → let it dry fully, in open air, away from heat → re-run the sink test once the moisture is gone → the second read is the honest density → the first read, if the piece was green, was water.

The move matters because it is the difference between grading the material and grading the moisture it happened to be carrying. A buyer who corrects for water grades the wood; a buyer who does not grades the weather. The correction needs no instrument and no expertise — only the patience to let the piece reach its true weight before trusting the bowl.

## The Two-Kind Smoke Read: Burn vs Warm, Again

**Direct answer:** Agarwood graded for burning and agarwood graded for warming are read by different smokes — one by the smoke it makes, the other by the scent it gives without any — and a buyer should know which kind of piece is in hand before judging it.

Restate the distinction with the smoke made explicit. The two uses diverge at the fire:

burning-grade → the piece ignites and smolders → the grade reads the smoke's body, scent, and finish → a piece is judged by the column and the room it fills.

warming-grade → the piece never ignites → the grade reads the scent released on gentle heat alone → a piece is judged by the three waves with no smoke at all.

A piece can be superb at one and wasted at the other. A soft 奇楠 is sublime warmed and nearly pointless burned; a dense, smoky piece is superb burned and flat warmed. The grade, to be complete, must say which fire the piece was judged by — and the buyer who skips that question is comparing a burner's grade to a warmer's grade without noticing the two have different rulers.

Hold the two columns apart, and the word "grade" stops meaning a single staircase and starts meaning two questions answered honestly. That table is the material's true shape.

## The Region-Reputation Boundary, Held Firm

**Direct answer:** A region's reputation is a prior, not a verdict — it tells a buyer what to expect before smelling, and it must give way the moment the actual piece disagrees with it.

The boundary between a reputation and a fact is where origin-reading goes wrong. Hold the line:

before smelling → the reputation sets a prior → "Hainan tends cooling, Vietnam tends sweet" → a useful starting guess.

after smelling → the piece is the fact → a dull Hainan piece is dull, a vivid plantation piece is vivid → the reputation yields to the material.

The buyer who keeps the two in order uses the reputation and the buyer who inverts them is used by it. An origin name should narrow the search and shape the expectation, never override the warm read. The piece is the only thing that actually smells, and the reputation is only a bet about what it might do — a bet the nose settles, not the label.

## The Buyer's Standby Rule: Prefer the Whole Piece

**Direct answer:** Where a choice exists, prefer the whole piece over powder, chips, or a pre-cut lot — because the identifying features of a grade survive only in an intact piece, and every cut erases evidence.

The whole piece is the evidence-rich form. The reason:

a whole piece → shows its formation route, its wound shape, its texture, its cold scent → every signal is present to be read.

a powder or chip lot → the route and wound shape are gone → the texture is homogenized → the cold scent is spread thin → the buyer is left with density and a warm read, minus the structural clues.

The rule is a standby, not an absolute — a buyer often has no choice but chips or powder, and those can still be read. But where the whole piece is available, it is the version that carries the most information, and information is the buyer's only real protection. Prefer the piece that can still tell its own story. ## The Closing Read, for a Collector

The closing line, aimed at the collector rather than the casual buyer: a serious agarwood collection is an accumulation of warm reads, not of grade tags. Each piece earns its place by how it behaves on gentle heat and how its density and transformation actually read — not by the word printed on its box. The collector who builds that way owns a reference library of genuine, tested material; the collector who builds on labels owns a shelf of claims. ## The Resin-Load Continuum, Marked at Three Points

**Direct answer:** The density dial is a continuum, but three points on it do most of the practical work — the commodity floor, the gradeable middle, and the sinking ceiling — and a buyer who can place a piece among the three has already graded it roughly.

The continuum reads as a line with three markers. Place the piece on it:

the commodity floor → light, floating, faint → the material is an herb, not a treasure → priced accordingly.

the gradeable middle → half-sinking to sinking, resin-veined → the everyday working agarwood → where most honest lots sit.

the sinking ceiling → fully submerged, dense, heavy → the top of the ordinary tier → where the premium for density is real.

Above the ceiling the dial changes character — the 奇楠 state is a different axis, not a higher point on the same line. But for the ordinary tier, the three markers are the whole map, and placing a piece among them is the first, fastest grade. The bowl, run once, tells you which marker the piece is nearest — and that is already more than most labels say.

## The Textural Read, in Two Steps

**Direct answer:** The hand can separate ordinary 沉香 from 奇楠 in two steps — press, then flex — because the whole difference between the two states is how the fiber behaves under pressure.

Texture is the transformation dial's instrument, and it takes two moves:

press the piece → ordinary 沉香 is firm and unyielding → 奇楠 yields slightly, like a dense resin → the first read points one way or the other.

flex or attempt to shave a sliver → ordinary 沉香 resists and splinters → 奇楠 shaves into soft curls and kneads toward a pellet → the second read confirms the first.

The two moves are the classical test — "shaves into curls and kneads into pellets" — translated into a buyer's hands. Neither requires heat or a bowl; the hand alone reads the structural difference that the whole 奇楠 divide is built on. A piece that is soft, yielding, and curls under a knife is not ordinary 沉香, whatever the label says.

## The Cross-Check Rule: No Single Signal Decides

**Direct answer:** The graver error in agarwood is not a wrong signal but a single signal trusted alone — and the buyer's rule is that no one test, no matter how objective it looks, closes the question by itself.

The discipline is cross-checking. Every signal has a failure mode, and they fail in different directions:

the sink test → beaten by injected weight → fails by over-trusting density.

the visual read → beaten by dye → fails by over-trusting color.

the warm read → beaten only by real material or costly synthetics → fails least, but still not never.

The rule: run them together, and let them cancel each other's blind spots. A piece that sinks, looks saturated, and warms beautifully is as close to certain as the buyer gets; a piece that passes one and fails the others is already exposed. No single signal decides — the agreement among signals is the grade, and the disagreement is the warning. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

## The Cut-End Reading: What a Fresh Face Reveals

**Direct answer:** A fresh cut end is the most honest surface on a piece — the grain, the resin distribution, and the oil sheen all read there unpolished — and a buyer who asks for a fresh cut is asking to see the material without its cosmetics.

The cut end is where the wood shows its true interior. Read it:

the grain across the cut → tight, even rings versus wide, irregular ones → a growth-history clue.

the resin in the cut → distinct veins versus even saturation → the ordinary-versus-奇楠 divide, visible raw.

the sheen under a breath → an oily film or a dry matte face → the oil load, read before any polish hides it.

A polished or waxed outer surface is a dressed face; a fresh cut is an undressed one. Sellers prefer the dressed face; buyers should ask for the undressed one. The cut end costs nothing to produce and reveals more than the whole polished exterior, because it is the only surface the seller has not prepared for the eye.

## The Buyer's Order of Operations, Revisited

**Direct answer:** The buyer's moves have a correct order — provenance, then physical, then sensory, then price — and running them out of order lets a strong early signal mask a weak later one.

The order protects against the most common self-deception. Run it top to bottom:

provenance first → who, where, what paper → it sets the stakes before any test is run.

the physical tests second → sink, look, texture, cold scent → they test the material against the label.

the warm read third → the three waves on gentle heat → the final sensory verdict.

the price last → does the number match the first three findings → the last, and only then, a buying decision.

Each step's result conditions the next. A piece that fails provenance still gets tested, but the buyer already knows to weight the result differently. The order is the buyer's defense against being talked into a purchase by a single loud signal — the sink, the story, or the price — before the quieter ones have had their turn.

## The Price-to-Grade Consistency Check

**Direct answer:** The final check is consistency — the label, the material, and the price must tell one story — and a mismatch in any direction is the signal to stop and ask why.

The three should agree. Lay them side by side:

the label says 奇楠, the material reads soft and cold-fragrant, the price is high → consistent → the story holds.

the label says 沉水, the material sinks, the price is mid → consistent → ordinary but honest.

the label says 奇楠, the material is firm and ordinary, the price is low → inconsistent → either the label or the price is lying, and the buyer should find out which.

A mismatch is not automatically fraud — it can be a seller's error, a hopeful description, or a genuine bargain — but it is always a reason to pause. The consistency check is the last line of the whole sequence, and it catches what every earlier test, run in isolation, might miss: the point where the three parts of the purchase stop agreeing with each other.

## The Smoke-Test Caveat: When Not to Burn the Evidence

**Direct answer:** A buyer should think twice before burning the very piece they are grading, because the warm read on a whole piece preserves the evidence while a test burn destroys the signals that authentication depends on.

The warm read and the burn are different acts with different costs. The distinction:

the warm read → gentle heat, no ignition → the piece survives intact → its texture, cold scent, and structure remain checkable afterward → the evidence is preserved.

the test burn → the piece ignites and is consumed → the smoke and the room read are gained → the piece is gone → the physical signals are destroyed.

For a piece of any real value, the burn should be the last act, not the first — and often it should be skipped in favor of the warm read, which answers the same sensory question without spending the evidence. A buyer who burns a piece to test it has spent the material to answer a question the warm read would have answered for free. Burn the ordinary stuff; warm and keep the pieces that still have something to prove.

## The Seasonality Question, Answered Honestly

## The Grain-and-Oil Pairing: Reading Density Without the Bowl

## The Weighing-Practice Note: Dry, Then Trust the Scale

## FAQ

### Q: How do agarwood (沉香) and qinan (奇楠) actually differ?
Ordinary 沉香 is "wood wraps oil" (木包油): hard wood with resin veins running through it. 奇楠 is "oil wraps wood" (油包木): the fiber is so thoroughly resin-converted that the material goes soft and pliable — shaving into curls and kneading into pellets — and smells at room temperature. The practical difference is that the water test is a reliable signal for ordinary 沉香, but top 奇楠 is sometimes described as half-sinking rather than fully sinking, because its resin is soft and evenly spread rather than densely concentrated.

### Q: What does grading actually weigh?
Primarily resin density via the 沉水 water test — sinking is highest, half-sinking is medium, floating is lower — then the 奇楠 distinction, then (traditionally) the five-color 奇楠 hierarchy. In practice a grader also weighs oil distribution, aroma on gentle heat, formation type (熟结/生结/虫漏/脱落), and origin. Density is the most objective dial; aroma is what collectors pay for.

### Q: Where does the name "sinking fragrance" come from?
沉香 literally means "sinking fragrance" — the name records the primary grading method. Resin is denser than wood, so the most resin-saturated pieces sink in water while less-resinous wood floats. The test grades resin concentration, not aroma or the 奇楠 transformation, so it is a useful rule of thumb rather than a complete judgment.

### Q: Is the five-color qinan hierarchy settled?
No — sources disagree on whether the purple (紫) and yellow (黄) tiers are distinct grades or aging stages of the green (绿) tier. The hierarchy is a traditional subdivision, not a laboratory taxonomy. When a seller leans on a specific color grade to justify a price, ask what the color is actually claimed to signal.

### Q: Where does the word qinan (奇楠) come from?
It is genuinely uncertain — competing theories include Sanskrit "Kāla" (black/time), Sanskrit "tagara" (a fragrant plant), and a "temple" (伽蓝) reading. Some scholarship argues "tagara" is a mistranslation and that the Sanskrit word for agarwood is "agaru/aguru." The Japanese "kyara" is the cognate and carries the same ambiguity.

### Q: Why can't I just trust the word "sinking" on a label?
Because fakes are engineered to sink. Oil-boiling and oil-injection add weight to ordinary wood, so a treated piece passes the water test and reads as a high grade. The test measures density, but a counterfeit pays to have density manufactured for it. Trust the sink test only as a first filter, and lean on warm-aroma and seller verification for the final call.

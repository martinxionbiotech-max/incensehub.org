**Direct answer:** Agarwood grading uses two different measurements that English guides usually collapse into one. The water test measures how much resin a piece carries; the 奇楠 question asks what the resin has done to the wood fibre. A single "grade" cannot answer both, and a tag that tries is a claim rather than a result.

## Key facts

| The question | What is measured | Words attached |
|---|---|---|
| How much resin? | Load inside the fibre | 沉水, 半沉半浮, 浮 |
| What state is the wood? | Whether the fibre has transformed | 奇楠, kyara |
| Where did the resin form? | Living tree, dead tree, insect bore, break | 生结, 熟结, 虫漏, 脱落 |
| Where did the tree grow? | Wild forest or plantation | Wild, cultivated, inoculated |

沉香 means "sinking fragrance." The name records an old test, not a compliment. 奇楠 is a condition of the material, not a promotion within the same ranking. Once those two sentences are clear, most of the market's grade language becomes readable — and most of its marketing becomes visible.

## Key Takeaways

- **The bowl measures resin load.** It does not measure aroma, age, species, or legality.
- **奇楠 is oil-wrapping-wood.** Ordinary agarwood is wood-wrapping-oil. That is a different material state, not a higher score.
- **The five-colour 奇楠 scheme is traditional and partly disputed.** Two tiers are better treated as descriptions than as a settled hierarchy.
- **Route names describe formation.** 生结 and 熟结 are not quality grades, and neither is 虫漏.
- **Wild and plantation is a separate axis.** It cuts across every grade word rather than sitting above them.
- **A fake usually wins one test.** Oil loading defeats the bowl; dye defeats the eye; a species substitute defeats the nose. Test in sequence, not in one move.
- **Grade should follow use.** The best piece for heating is not automatically the best piece for carving or for a large blend.

## Two questions, two gauges

**Direct answer:** Ask "how much resin?" and "what happened to the fibre?" in that order. The first is cheap to test; the second requires handling, warmth, and sometimes a cut.

The common mistake is to imagine a single staircase running from cheap chips to 奇楠. The staircase is tidy, and it fails the first time you meet a 奇楠 piece that rides high in water or a heavy piece that smells of almost nothing. Those are not anomalies. They are the system telling you that two measurements were being made all along.

The two gauges also answer different buyer questions:

- Resin load predicts **yield**: how much scent you get per gram when heated, and how long a piece lasts.
- Fibre state predicts **behaviour**: how the piece cuts, how it warms, how it releases aroma at low temperature, and whether it can be kneaded or shaved.

A seller who gives you one word for both is either simplifying for a beginner or hiding the distinction. Neither is a good basis for a high-value purchase.

## The bowl: a fast sort with a blind spot

**Direct answer:** Resin is denser than the dry wood it replaces, so a resin-rich piece displaces less water and may sink. The test needs no equipment, which is why the trade adopted it and why it is so easily gamed.

The physical logic is simple. Dry *Aquilaria* heartwood is light and porous. As resin fills the cells, density rises. A piece with enough resin can exceed the density of water and go down. The traditional bands are:

- **沉水** — sinks.
- **半沉半浮** — half-sinks or stays suspended.
- **浮** — floats.

The test has three limits. First, it measures average density, not aroma. A dense piece can be dense because of injected oil rather than natural resin. Second, it is sensitive to moisture and to the piece's shape; a dry, thin chip and a damp, thick block are not comparable. Third, it says nothing about species or legality. A sinking piece of the wrong species is still the wrong species.

Use the bowl as a **sorting tool**, not a verdict. Record the result, then keep testing. The [formation and species guide](/blog/agarwood-formation-and-species/) explains why density and resin chemistry are related but not identical.

## 奇楠: a material state, not a grade

**Direct answer:** 奇楠 is agarwood in which the resin has permeated and softened the fibre — 油包木, oil wrapping wood — rather than filling veins inside hard wood. It is a different material condition, and that is why it behaves differently.

Ordinary agarwood is often described as 木包油: hard fibre with resin in bands and pockets. 奇楠 is described as 油包木: the fibre itself has been transformed, so the piece can be soft, pliable, and aromatic at room temperature. Traditional descriptions say it can be shaved into curls or kneaded into pellets. Those are handling claims, not poetry, and they are testable.

The practical consequences are large:

- **Low-temperature aroma.** A genuine 奇楠 piece can smell complex without flame. Ordinary high-resin agarwood is often quieter until warmed.
- **Cutting behaviour.** A soft, resin-rich piece cuts differently from a hard, banded one. A clean slice can reveal whether the interior matches the surface.
- **Water behaviour.** Some high-grade 奇楠 is described as half-sinking rather than sinking, because the resin is evenly distributed and the fibre is soft. This contradicts the simple "sinking equals best" rule and is one reason the two-gauge model matters.

The word's origin is unresolved. Sanskrit-derived theories include *kāla* ("black"), *tagara* (a fragrant plant), and a temple-related reading. A rigorous Chinese philological argument treats the *tagara* derivation as a mis-translation, since the Sanskrit word for agarwood is *agaru*. This page does not pick a winner. The etymology is a historical question; the material behaviour is the buying question.

## The five colours and the argument about them

**Direct answer:** The traditional 奇楠 scheme uses five colour names — white, green, purple, yellow, and black. The names describe appearance and aroma tendencies, but the hierarchy between them is not settled.

The common sequence runs 白奇楠, 绿奇楠 (莺歌绿), 紫奇楠 (兰花结), 黄奇楠 (金丝结), 黑奇楠 (铁结). Each name bundles a colour impression with a scent description and, in some accounts, a distinct grade.

The argument is whether the middle colours are separate grades or stages of the same material. Some sources treat 紫棋 and 黄棋 as aging stages of 绿棋; others list them as distinct categories. The disagreement is not trivial, because a "purple" label can carry a price premium that a "green" label does not.

A defensible buying position is to treat the colour names as **descriptive vocabulary** rather than a certified scale. Ask what the seller means by the word, then inspect the piece. If the answer is a colour chart with no material evidence, the word is decorative.

## Route names: where the resin formed

**Direct answer:** 生结, 熟结, 虫漏, and 脱落 describe the wound context. They are formation categories, not grades, and they interact with age and species rather than replacing them.

- **生结** — resin in a living tree. Most plantation wood. The resin may be mature or immature; the word does not say.
- **熟结** — resin in a dead or fallen tree. The wood has weathered, and the resin has concentrated. The category carries a wild-forest premium, and therefore a verification problem.
- **虫漏** — resin around insect borings. Small, repeated wounds can produce concentrated pockets and unusual shapes.
- **脱落** — resin at an external break or fallen branch.

These words are useful when they come with a provenance story. They are weak when they arrive alone. A "熟结" claim without a forest origin, harvest year, or chain of custody is a mood, not a fact.

## Wild or planted: a different axis

**Direct answer:** Wild and plantation describe origin and method. They cut across the grade words: a plantation piece can be high-resin, and a wild piece can be mediocre.

The wild premium is real but composite. It pays for scarcity, older resin, and a verifiable forest story. Plantation supply pays for consistency and legality, and it has improved substantially. The useful question is not "wild or planted?" but "how mature is the resin, and can the seller show the chain?"

A piece can be:

- wild, old, and high-resin;
- wild, old, and low-resin;
- plantation, mature, and high-resin;
- plantation, young, and heavily oil-loaded.

Only the first and third are likely to be good value. The label alone does not sort them.

## The three ways a fake wins

**Direct answer:** Most fakes are designed to beat one test. Oil loading beats the bowl, dye beats the eye, and a species substitute beats a nose that has not learned the range.

**1. Oil loading.** The wood is boiled in or injected with oil under pressure. Weight rises, colour darkens, and the piece may sink. The tell is often in the cut: a dark shell around a pale core, or resin pooled in cracks rather than following the grain. Heating can also expose it — a sharp, perfumed note that collapses quickly is not the same as resin developing over minutes.

**2. Dyeing.** Non-resinous or low-resin wood is stained to look dense. The tell is colour that sits on the surface, fills pores uniformly, or runs when the piece is wiped with a damp cloth. A genuine piece usually shows variation: darker resin around wounds, lighter wood elsewhere.

**3. Species substitution.** A different resinous wood, sometimes from another family, is sold as agarwood. The tell is botanical: grain, pore structure, and the way the piece behaves when cut. A nose trained only on one region may miss it, which is why reference material matters.

A fourth category — **compression and lamination** — is less about chemistry and more about construction. Pieces can be pressed from dust and resin, or built from smaller fragments. A cut edge usually settles it. The [incense authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) collects the material-level checks in one place.

## A six-step inspection sequence

**Direct answer:** Inspect in the order that costs the least and destroys the least: paperwork, photograph, surface, water, heat, cut. Stop at the first failed claim.

1. **Paperwork.** Species, region, year, and permit number if the material crosses a border. A seller who cannot name the species has already failed the first step.
2. **Photograph.** Neutral light, both faces, a scale, and a close-up of the end grain. A photograph is not proof, but it is a record of what was claimed.
3. **Surface.** Resin should follow the grain and cluster around wounds. Uniform blackness, paint-like edges, and resin pooled in cracks are warning signs.
4. **Water.** Record sink, half-sink, or float. Compare with the seller's claim. A failed claim is a failed claim even if the piece is genuine in some other respect.
5. **Heat.** Warm a corner gently. Listen for complexity and development. A single sharp note, or a smell that vanishes, is not a good sign.
6. **Cut.** Cut a small sample or ask for a cut face. The interior should match the exterior story. This is the expensive step; it is also the one that ends arguments.

The sequence is not a laboratory method. It is a way to make claims testable before money changes hands.

## Heat: three waves

**Direct answer:** A good piece often releases aroma in stages: a top note at low heat, a fuller middle as the resin warms, and a base note that lingers. A fake frequently collapses after the first wave.

On a heater, start low. The first wave is the most diagnostic because it shows what the material does without being pushed. A genuine high-resin piece may be quiet at first, then open. An oil-loaded piece often announces itself immediately and then flattens.

Increase the heat gradually. The middle wave should add body rather than change the subject. The base note should smell like wood, resin, or a sweet-balsamic trace — not like a candle or a perfume counter.

Charcoal and direct flame are less informative because combustion adds its own chemistry. If you must burn, use the smallest sliver and treat the result as one data point, not a verdict.

## The cut face

**Direct answer:** The cut face is the closest thing to an internal audit. Resin penetration should be continuous and should follow the wood's structure, not sit in a thin surface layer.

Look for three things:

- **Depth.** Resin should extend into the piece, not stop at a stained surface.
- **Pattern.** Bands, pockets, and wound-associated concentration are normal. A uniform, paint-like layer is not.
- **Colour change.** A pale core inside a dark shell is the classic oil-loading signature. A natural piece can have pale areas, but the transition should follow grain and anatomy.

A clean cut also releases aroma. Smell the fresh surface before and after warming. The difference is often more informative than the exterior.

## Matching grade to purpose

**Direct answer:** The right piece depends on what you will do with it. Heating, burning, carving, and blending reward different properties.

| Use | What matters most | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Heating (electric or charcoal) | Low-temperature aroma, resin maturity, clean burn | Oil-loaded pieces that smell sharp or collapse |
| Incense blending | Consistent resin load, predictable aroma, powder yield | Mixed lots with unknown species |
| Carving and beads | Density, colour, workability, soundness | Hidden cracks, soft pockets, laminated material |
| Collection | Provenance, rarity, condition, documented chain | A "wild" story with no paperwork |

A high-resin piece is not automatically the best carving piece: dense, brittle material can chip. A beautifully figured piece is not automatically the best burner: figure says nothing about resin chemistry. Grade words are a starting point; use decides the rest. If you are buying powder rather than chips, read [powder vs chips](/blog/agarwood-powder-vs-chips/) first — grinding removes the evidence this page relies on.

## Certificates and what they can say

**Direct answer:** A CITES permit can show that a shipment was declared and cleared. It cannot prove the species on the label, the resin load, or the age of the wood.

This matters because "certified" is often used as a quality claim. The document is a trade-control instrument. It answers a legal question, not an aromatic one.

A useful document check has three parts:

- **Species** on the permit matches the species on the invoice.
- **Form** matches what is being sold: chips, powder, oil, or finished product.
- **Quantity and date** are consistent with the batch.

If any of the three is missing, the certificate is not doing the work the buyer thinks it is.

## When to walk away

**Direct answer:** Walk away when the story is more specific than the evidence, when a test result is dismissed rather than explained, or when the seller cannot separate the four components of price.

The red flags are consistent:

- A dramatic wild-forest story with no harvest year or region.
- A refusal to provide a cut face or a sample.
- A price that is far below the market for the claimed grade.
- A claim that a single test — usually the water test — settles everything.
- A certificate offered as a substitute for species identification.

None of these proves fraud. Together they describe a transaction where the buyer is carrying all the information risk.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** I would rather buy a documented, mid-grade plantation piece than an undocumented "wild 奇楠" whose only evidence is a dark photograph and a confident sentence.

**Reasoning:** First, the grade words are cheap to print and expensive to verify; the documents and the cut face are the opposite. Second, the market's most common failure is not a rare sophisticated fake — it is ordinary oil loading and dyeing sold at a premium, and both are exposed by basic inspection. Third, a mid-grade piece with a clear chain teaches you more: you can heat it, compare it, and build a reference library without betting the price of a used car on a story.

**Disclosure:** This is an editorial judgment about buying strategy, not a laboratory finding or a legal opinion. Experienced collectors may reasonably pay for provenance and rarity in ways this page does not attempt to price.

## FAQ

**Is a sinking piece always better?**
No. Sinking indicates high resin load. It does not prove natural resin, mature aroma, or the right species. Oil-loaded pieces can sink.

**Is 奇楠 a grade above 沉水?**
It is a different condition. 沉水 describes resin load; 奇楠 describes a transformed, oil-wrapping-wood state. Some 奇楠 is half-sinking rather than sinking.

**How settled is the five-colour scheme?**
It is traditional, widely repeated, and not fully settled. The middle colours are treated by some sources as distinct grades and by others as aging stages. Use the names descriptively.

**Does cultivated wood ever become 奇楠?**
The category is associated with wild, aged material, and plantation 奇楠 is disputed. Treat any cultivated "奇楠" claim as a claim requiring unusually strong evidence.

**What does 沉香 actually mean?**
"Sinking fragrance." The name refers to the old water test for resin density, not to a botanical species or a legal grade.

## Summary

Grading is two measurements, not one ladder. The bowl sorts resin load; the hand, the heat, and the cut reveal fibre state. 奇楠 sits outside the staircase because it describes a different material condition. Learn the formation words, ask for species and provenance, and test in the cheapest order. A grade word is a claim. The inspection is the evidence.

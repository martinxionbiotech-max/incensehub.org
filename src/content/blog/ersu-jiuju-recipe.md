**Direct answer:** 二苏旧局 is a contemporary 合香 by 陈云君 (Chen Yunjun) from his *燕居香语* — not an ancient 香谱 text — built on a wood core of agarwood and sandalwood, lifted by frankincense, grounded by amber, and finished with a honey binder and a jasmine coat.

## Key facts

苏轼 and 苏辙, the Song brothers known as the "two Su" literati, supply the name — an evocative label rather than a historical record. What the formula teaches best is its 佐 (assistant) seat, filled by two materials that pull in opposite directions.

## The Recipe, and the Boundary It Sets

**Direct answer:** 二苏旧局 is a revival written in the 合香 idiom, so the honest first move is to state the boundary: a modern composition wearing a classical structure, not a 香谱 original.

The ingredient line reads 沉香、檀香、乳香、琥珀、蜂蜜、茉莉花 — agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, amber, honey, jasmine. Making it runs in six moves: cube the agarwood and sandalwood; powder the frankincense and amber; work all four together with honey (jujube-flower, by tradition); roll the mass into pills; dust the outside with dried jasmine; leave them in open air to dry.

| Role | Material | Job in this blend |
|---|---|---|
| 君 (chief) | 沉香 agarwood | Deep, quiet spine — the study's "old desk" |
| 臣 (minister) | 檀香 sandalwood | Takes the chill off the agarwood, keeps it soft |
| 佐 (assistant) | 乳香 frankincense + 琥珀 [amber](/ingredients/amber/) | One opens and lifts; the other settles and deepens |
| 使 (guide) | 蜂蜜 honey + 茉莉花 jasmine | Honey holds the pill together; the coat writes the opening line |

## Why the Split Assistant Is the Real Lesson

**Direct answer:** The 佐 (assistant) role here is split across two materials pulling opposite ways — frankincense lifts while amber settles — which shows "assistant" is a function, not a count.

In a one-assistant formula, that seat goes to a single material with a single task. Here it is two, and they are not even aligned. Frankincense is a bright, volatile resin that evaporates fast and opens the top of the scent; amber is dense and slow, grounding the base and holding it down. Putting both in the same role shows the role system describes *what a material does in the structure*, not how many materials fill the slot.

The jasmine coat is the second half of the same lesson, and it works by geometry: the coat sits on the pill's surface → heat reaches it first → it volatilizes before the interior warms through → the first impression is a bright floral → only as the ember sinks does the deep agarwood body emerge. One pill, two stages, from layers that burn in sequence rather than at once.

There is a second chain in the wood-plus-resin core. The diced woods are held together by honey, so the pill burns as a slowly glowing solid rather than a flash of powder → the honey caramelizes and releases the resins gradually → frankincense's quick lift and amber's slow settle unfold in the order their volatility dictates → the blend reads as a sequence, not a chord. The binder paces the release; it is not just glue.

## The Dicing Decision: Why Cubes, Not Powder

**Direct answer:** The recipe dices the woods rather than grinding them, and that choice is a burn-rate dial — cubes smolder slower and longer, so the deep wood core outlasts the resins around it.

Follow the geometry from cutting board to ember. A cube and a powder made of the same wood burn on different clocks: powder packs tight and exposes every grain to heat at once → the mass warms and releases quickly → the note flashes and fades. Cubes expose less surface relative to their mass → heat works inward slowly → the wood smolders across the full burn → the deep core holds while the powdered resins around it spend themselves faster.

The dicing keeps agarwood and sandalwood as the long, quiet foundation instead of letting them burn off in the first rush. It is the same logic that makes a chip outlast a fine grind: surface area sets release rate, and this recipe chose its surface area on purpose.

## The Honey-Binder Chain

**Direct answer:** Honey is the recipe's structural load — it turns a loose mix of cubes and powders into a solid pill that can smolder as one object — and its slow caramelization paces the whole release.

Honey does two jobs at two different times. During the making → it coats every particle and, as it dries, welds the mass into a firm pill → the pill holds its shape through rolling and drying. During the burning → heat caramelizes the honey gradually → the sugars darken and melt slowly → the resins and woods release in the order their volatility dictates → the honey's slow melt is the metronome that sets the sequence.

A binder that flared fast would spend the whole pill in one burst; honey's slow caramelization stretches the blend into a staged experience. The ingredient does structural work in the hand and pacing work in the fire, and both halves matter to why the recipe reads as a sequence rather than a chord.

## The Air-Dry Finish

**Direct answer:** Air-drying is not a pause — it sets the jasmine coat and firms the honey, and its evenness decides whether the pill burns clean or crumbles.

After the pills are rolled and coated, they rest in open air, and two things settle at once. The jasmine coat dries onto the surface → it adheres and holds its bright top note → a coat that dries unevenly flakes off → the two-stage experience is lost before the first light. The honey binder loses its residual moisture → the pill firms into a solid → a pill dried too fast cracks, a pill dried too slow stays tacky → either failure ruins the smolder.

This is the same even-drying logic as a coil or a stick: the surface sets first, the core last, and the tension between them is where defects open. A well-dried pill is the quiet proof that every earlier step was done right.

## The Amber Question

**Direct answer:** 琥珀 here is the fossil resin — dense, slow, grounding — and its job is the opposite of frankincense's, which is exactly why the two share the assistant seat.

Amber is easy to misread as a wood or a gem. In the classical incense context it is the hardened resin of ancient trees, and it behaves in a specific way: amber is dense and slow → it does not evaporate readily → it grounds the blend and holds the low register → it balances frankincense's quick, bright lift → the two together make the assistant seat do two jobs at once. A reader who understands why amber sits beside frankincense has understood the role system itself. The material has its own entry: [amber](/ingredients/amber/).

## Reading the Formula as a Template

**Direct answer:** Strip the names away and 二苏旧局 is a reusable pattern — a slow wood body, a two-material assistant, a binder that also paces, and a surface layer that opens — and the pattern, not the ingredient list, is what a maker can carry to another blend.

The test is what can move and what cannot. The woods can change: another dense, slow-burning wood can hold the 君 seat if it releases over the length of the burn. The assistant pair can change too, provided one material lifts and the other settles — the split is the point, not the specific pair. What cannot move without breaking the design is the order: a coat must sit outside the body, and the binder must outlast the resins it carries.

Failures follow from breaking that order. A binder that hardens before the core → the coat cracks away from the pill → the opening note is gone before the first light. One soft resin asked to do both assistant jobs → the top and the base arrive together → the two-stage read collapses into a single flat note. The template tells you where to look when a blend disappoints.

## FAQ

### Q: Is 二苏旧局 an ancient formula or a modern revival?
Modern, and the distinction is the most important thing to get right. 陈云君's *燕居香语* is a recent manual, and it writes in the old 合香 register. Its name and its shape are old in style; its text is not a Song or Ming 香谱 entry. Retellings often slip and present it as a period formula. "Modern revival" is the accurate frame, and it is why no historical weights are assigned here.

### Q: Where does the "two Su" name come from?
苏轼 (Su Shi) and 苏辙 (Su Zhe), the Song-dynasty brothers who rank among the era's celebrated writers. "二苏" gestures at them, but the gesture is poetic rather than documentary: nothing connects either brother to this blend. The recipe uses their names to summon a scholar's study — quiet, bookish, deep-wood — and the reader should treat that as a mood-setting label, not a record.

### Q: What job does the jasmine coat perform?
Jasmine forms the shell of the pill, so it speaks first: a clean, bright floral ahead of the agarwood. Because the shell meets heat before the interior, the experience arrives in two stages — a light top yielding to the quiet, resinous body. Within the role system it belongs, with the honey, to the 使 (guide) seat: it governs presentation, not the core.

### Q: Why dice the woods instead of grinding everything?
Because surface area sets the release rate. Powdered wood warms through at once and spends its note in a quick flash; a cube heats inward slowly and keeps releasing across the whole burn. Grinding everything would flatten the layering the recipe is built on, collapsing the slow wood core into the same fast timeline as the resins. The dice is the cheapest way to keep the body of the blend behind the opening.

### Q: Why honey rather than another binder?
It is the binder and the pace-setter, not a flavoring. Honey holds the diced woods and ground resins together as a solid pill, and as the pill smolders it caramelizes slowly, releasing the resins in the order their volatility dictates rather than all at once. That is why the blend reads as a sequence — frankincense first, amber later — instead of a single chord. It turns a pile of powder into a timed release.

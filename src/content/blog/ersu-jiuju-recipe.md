**Direct answer:** 二苏旧局 is a contemporary 合香 by 陈云君 (Chen Yunjun) from his *燕居香语* — not an ancient 香谱 text — built on a wood core of agarwood and sandalwood, lifted by frankincense, grounded by amber, and finished with a honey binder and a jasmine coat.

**Key facts:** The name points to the Song brothers 苏轼 and 苏辙 (the "two Su" literati), but only as an evocative label, not a historical record. The formula's most teachable feature is its 佐 (assistant) role split across two ingredients pulling in opposite directions.

## What the Recipe Is — and What It Is Not

**Direct answer:** 二苏旧局 is a revival written in the 合香 idiom, so the honest first move is to state the boundary: it is a modern composition wearing a classical structure, not a 香谱 original.

The ingredient line reads 沉香、檀香、乳香、琥珀、蜂蜜、茉莉花 — agarwood, sandalwood, frankincense, amber, honey, and jasmine. The method dices the two woods into cubes, grinds the frankincense and amber to powder, binds all four in honey (traditionally jujube-flower honey), rolls the mass into pills, coats the outside with dried jasmine flowers, and air-dries the result.

That sequence matters more than it first appears, because it separates the blend into layers that burn at different moments. The wood core smolders longest, the resins open in the middle, and the jasmine coat — sitting on the surface — is the first thing to meet the heat.

| Role | Ingredient | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 君 (chief) | 沉香 agarwood | Supplies the deep, quiet skeleton — the "old desk" of the study |
| 臣 (minister) | 檀香 sandalwood | Warms the agarwood so it reads as soft, not cold |
| 佐 (assistant) | 乳香 frankincense + 琥珀 [amber](/ingredients/amber/) | Frankincense lifts and opens; amber grounds and deepens |
| 使 (guide) | 蜂蜜 honey + 茉莉花 jasmine | Honey binds the pills; the jasmine coat opens a bright floral top |

## Why the Split Assistant Is the Lesson

**Direct answer:** The 佐 (assistant) role here is split across two ingredients pulling in opposite directions — frankincense lifts while amber settles — which proves "assistant" is a function, not a count.

This is the cleanest teaching point in the whole cluster. In a simpler recipe the assistant is one ingredient doing one job. Here it is two, and they are not even aligned. Frankincense is a bright, volatile resin that evaporates fast and opens the top of the scent; amber is a dense, slow material that grounds the base and holds it down. Placing both in the same role shows that the role system describes *what a material does in the structure*, not how many materials occupy the slot.

The jasmine coat is the second half of the same lesson, and it works by a simple physical chain: the coat sits on the pill's surface → heat reaches it first → it volatilizes before the interior warms through → the first impression is a bright floral → only as the ember sinks does the deep agarwood body emerge. A single pill therefore delivers a two-stage experience — top note, then body — from layers that burn in sequence, not all at once.

There is a second chain hiding in the wood-plus-resin core. The agarwood and sandalwood are diced and held together by honey, which means the pill burns as a slowly glowing solid rather than a flash of powder → the honey caramelizes and releases the resins gradually → frankincense's quick lift and amber's slow settle unfold in the order their volatility dictates → the blend reads as a sequence rather than a single chord. The binder is not just glue; it paces the release.

## The Layering Mechanism: Why the Coat Burns First

**Direct answer:** The jasmine coat sits on the pill's surface, so heat meets it before the interior — and that simple geometry is what turns one pill into a two-stage experience.

Trace the sequence a single pill runs through as it burns. The layers are not mixed together; they are stacked, and the stacking sets the order of release:

the dried jasmine coat wraps the outer surface → the ember touches the coat first → the bright floral volatilizes before the core warms → the honey and wood inside heat next → the frankincense opens in the middle → the deep agarwood body emerges last → one pill, read as a top note and then a body.

The geometry is the whole trick. If the jasmine were ground into the core instead of dusted on the surface, it would release with the resins and the top would never stand apart. The coat-and-core construction is a deliberate staging device, not a cosmetic flourish — it is how the recipe writes a beginning and a middle into a single object.

## The Dicing Decision: Why Cubes, Not Powder

**Direct answer:** The recipe specifies dicing the woods into cubes rather than grinding them to powder, and that choice is a burn-rate dial — cubes smolder slower and longer, so the deep wood core outlasts the resins around it.

Follow the geometry from cutting board to ember. A cube and a powder made of the same wood burn on different clocks:

powder packs tight and exposes every grain to heat at once → the whole mass warms and releases quickly → the note flashes and fades.

cubes expose less surface relative to their mass → heat works inward slowly → the wood smolders and releases across the full burn → the deep core holds while the powdered resins around it spend themselves faster.

The dicing, then, is what keeps the agarwood and sandalwood as the long, quiet foundation instead of letting them burn off in the first rush. It is the same logic that makes a chip burn slower than a fine grind — surface area sets the release rate, and the recipe chose its surface area on purpose. The cube is a pacing decision, not a texture quirk.

## The Honey-Binder Chain: How the Pill Stays Whole

**Direct answer:** Honey is the recipe's structural load — it is what turns a loose mix of cubes and powders into a solid pill that can smolder as one object — and its slow caramelization paces the whole release.

Trace honey's job through the making and the burning. It does two separate things at two separate times:

during the making → honey coats every particle and, as it dries, welds the mass into a firm pill → the pill holds its shape through rolling and drying.

during the burning → heat caramelizes the honey gradually → the sugars darken and melt slowly → the resins and woods release in the order their volatility dictates → the honey's slow melt is the metronome that sets the sequence.

A binder that flared fast would spend the whole pill in one burst; honey's slow caramelization is what stretches the blend into a staged experience. The ingredient is doing structural work in the hand and pacing work in the fire, and both halves matter to why the recipe reads as a sequence rather than a chord.

## The Air-Dry Finish: What Happens After the Coat

**Direct answer:** The final air-drying is not a pause — it is the step that sets the jasmine coat and firms the honey, and its evenness decides whether the pill burns clean or crumbles.

Trace the closing step as a process. After the pills are rolled and coated, they rest in open air, and two things settle at once:

the jasmine coat dries onto the surface → it adheres and holds its bright top note → a coat that dries unevenly flakes off → the two-stage experience is lost before the first light.

the honey binder loses its residual moisture → the pill firms into a solid → a pill dried too fast cracks, a pill dried too slow stays tacky → either failure ruins the smolder.

The air-dry is where the recipe's layered design either survives or falls apart. It is the same even-drying logic as a coil or a stick — the surface sets first, the core last, and the tension between them is where defects open. A well-dried pill is the quiet proof that every earlier step was done right.

## The Amber Question, Handled Directly

**Direct answer:** 琥珀 in this recipe is the fossil resin — a dense, slow, grounding material — and its job here is the opposite of frankincense's, which is exactly why the two share the assistant seat.

Amber is easy to misread as a wood or a gem, so it is worth pinning down. In the classical incense context it is the hardened resin of ancient trees, and it behaves in a specific way:

amber is dense and slow → it does not evaporate readily → it grounds the blend and holds the low register → it balances frankincense's quick, bright lift → the two together make the assistant seat do two jobs at once.

The pairing is the recipe's clearest teaching point, restated: the assistant is not one ingredient but a *function*, and here that function is split across a lifter and a settler. A reader who understands why amber sits beside frankincense has understood the role system itself. See the [amber ingredient](/ingredients/amber/) entry for the material's own page.

## The Step-by-Step, in One Pass

**Direct answer:** The full making sequence folds into six moves — dice, grind, bind, roll, coat, dry — and holding them in order is the fastest way to see the recipe's layered design.

The six moves, stated plainly:

1. Dice the agarwood and sandalwood into small cubes — the slow-burning core.
2. Grind the frankincense and amber to powder — the fast and the grounding pair.
3. Bind everything in honey, traditionally jujube-flower honey — the structural weld.
4. Roll the mass into pills — the unit the blend will burn as.
5. Coat the outside with dried jasmine flowers — the bright top layer.
6. Air-dry until firm and even — the step that sets it all.

Six moves, each mapping to a role in the finished pill: the cubes are the body, the powders the assistant, the honey the guide, the coat the opening, and the drying the finish. The sequence is the recipe — read in order, it is the whole design without a single measurement.

## A Note on the Honey's Role in Drying

**Direct answer:** The honey does more than bind — its moisture content governs how the pill dries, so the honey's consistency is part of the curing, not a separate detail.

Honey's water is the drying dial. Too much and the pill stays tacky; too little and it cracks. The mechanism:

runny honey → excess moisture → the pill dries slowly and may slump or stay soft → the coat cannot set cleanly.

stiff honey → little free moisture → the pill dries fast → the surface hardens before the core → cracks open at the weak points.

a honey worked to the batch's dryness → the pill dries evenly, coat and core together → the finished pill is firm and stable.

The honey, then, is the recipe's humidity control, folded into the binder. A maker who ignores its consistency is leaving the drying to chance — and drying, as the air-dry section showed, is where the layered design either holds or fails.

## FAQ

### Q: Is this an ancient recipe or a modern one?
Modern, and the distinction is the single most important thing to get right. The formula comes from 陈云君's *燕居香语*, a contemporary manual that happens to be written in the classical 合香 idiom. The name and the shape of the blend are old in style, but the recipe itself is not drawn from the Song or Ming 香谱. Retellings frequently slip and present it as a period formula; the accurate frame is "modern revival." That is also why the page marks its proportions unverified rather than assigning historical weights.

### Q: Who are the "two Su" in the name?
They are 苏轼 (Su Shi) and 苏辙 (Su Zhe), the Song-dynasty brothers who rank among the era's most celebrated writers. The name "二苏" ("two Su") gestures at them, but the gesture is poetic rather than documentary: nothing connects either brother to this particular blend. The recipe uses their names to summon a scholar's study — a quiet, bookish, deep-wood atmosphere — and the reader should treat that as a mood-setting label, not a record of what either man burned.

### Q: What does the jasmine do in the blend?
It is the pill's outer coat, and it delivers the first impression: a bright, clean floral that opens the scent before the deeper agarwood body reveals itself. Since the coat sits on the surface and meets the heat before anything else, it creates a two-stage experience — a light floral top that then yields to the quiet, resinous wood underneath. In the role system it sits with the honey in the 使 (guide) position, controlling how the blend presents itself rather than forming its core.

### Q: What makes this recipe different from the others in the cluster?
Two things. First, it is a modern revival rather than a 香谱 original: the source is 陈云君's contemporary *燕居香语*, and the proportions are loose rather than fixed in 两 and 钱. Second, its assistant (佐) role is split across two ingredients doing opposite jobs — frankincense opens and lifts, while amber settles and deepens. That split is a cleaner teaching example of the assistant as a function than any single-assistant recipe in the cluster, which is why this page treats it as the best entry point.

### Q: What is the honey actually for?
It is the binder and the pace-setter, not a flavoring. Honey holds the diced woods and ground resins together as a solid pill, and as the pill smolders it caramelizes slowly, releasing the resins in the order their volatility dictates rather than all at once. That is why the blend reads as a sequence — frankincense first, amber later — instead of a single chord. The honey is structural: it turns a pile of powder into a timed release.

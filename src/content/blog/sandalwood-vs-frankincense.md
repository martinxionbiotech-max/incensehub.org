**Direct answer:** Sandalwood and frankincense share a shelf in most incense shops, yet they are not the same kind of material. 檀香 names the scented core wood of *Santalum*, a solid in which aroma sits locked inside the fibre. 乳香 is what *Boswellia* bleeds after its bark is cut: a milky sap that sets into brittle lumps. Scent held inside wood, versus scent that is the material — hold that contrast and the rest of the comparison falls into place.

## Key facts

One family is Santalaceae, the other Burseraceae; apart from smelling good, the two plants have little in common. The tree grows slowly, steals part of its living from host roots, and is cut for its core. The resin is a wound product, collected by hand from scored bark. In blend terms the wood holds the bottom and the resin opens the top. Substitution risk is lopsided between them. Gentle heat suits both, though the physics behind that advice diverges.

---

## Key Takeaways

- One material is scented wood cut from a trunk; the other is dried tree exudate collected from a wound. Physical state separates them before scent does.
- The wood anchors a blend at the base; the resin opens it at the top. They are complements rather than alternatives.
- Copying is not evenly distributed. Solid wood attracts species swaps, oil passed off as timber, and perfume-soaked sticks; exudate is more often the real thing, with grade and species left to argue about.
- Heat rule: the wood smoulders, the resin melts. Getting that backwards wastes both materials.
- Nothing here is a health claim for either material.

## Two Physical States, and Why They Govern the Rest

**Direct answer:** Start with what the stuff is, not with how it smells. A solid and an exudate answer heat, damp and blending in different ways, and the hand can tell them apart before the nose joins in.

Sandalwood is a structural material. The scent is distributed through heartwood tissue that has grown for decades; a chip is dense, hard and holds its shape. Frankincense is an exudate. It leaves the tree as a milky fluid, sets into a brittle or waxy lump, and softens again when warmed. Nothing about a frankincense tear is wood; nothing about a sandalwood chip is sap.

What follows:

heat must travel into the fibre before scent can escape → release is gradual → the material works as a floor and rewards a slow session.

resin lump → heat softens the mass and drives volatiles off the surface → release is fast and bright → the material suits an opening role and punishes overheating.

That is a mechanical difference, not a matter of taste. A maker who treats one material like the other gets a poor result for a predictable reason.

| Property | Sandalwood (檀香) | Frankincense (乳香) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Heartwood | Gum-resin |
| Plant part used | Trunk heartwood | Bark exudate |
| Forms sold | Chips, powder, sticks, oil | Tears, powder, oil |
| On heat | Smoulders, releases slowly | Melts, releases quickly |
| Blend role | Base | Top |
| Storage enemy | Damp, odour pickup | Heat, light, oxidation |
| Substitution risk | High | Low |

## Where Each Material Comes From

**Direct answer:** *Santalum* supplies the wood; its benchmark species is *S. album*, a Santalaceae tree. *Boswellia* supplies the resin; *B. sacra*, *B. carterii* and *B. serrata* account for most of what is traded.

One genus, several traded species. *S. album* (Indian, or white) is the aromatic yardstick. Australian *S. spicatum* is what most legal trade actually moves today. New Caledonia (*S. austrocaledonicum*) and Hawaii (*S. paniculatum*) add modest volumes. Each is a hemiparasite: it makes its own sugars but steals water and minerals from a neighbour's roots, so a plantation takes years to pay back.

The resin's botany runs from the Horn of Africa across Arabia to India. *B. sacra* is the Omani reference; *B. carterii* dominates Horn of Africa shipments; *B. serrata*, the Indian species, reads earthier. Nothing is felled — the tree is wounded and the exudate collected.

Both Chinese names are descriptions. 檀香 (tánxiāng) says what the wood smells of; 乳香 (rǔxiāng) says what the fresh sap looks like — milk. Sound links them; scent does not. 紫檀 is *Pterocarpus santalinus*, cut from another family, and it yields a dye rather than a perfume — nothing sweet, nothing buttery. Every 檀 is not the same tree, and a label that leans on the character is describing another plant. Profiles: [sandalwood](/ingredients/sandalwood/), [frankincense](/ingredients/frankincense/), and the lookalike at [red sandalwood](/ingredients/red-sandalwood/).

## What the Nose Meets

**Direct answer:** The wood gives warm cream and soft timber; the resin gives lemon, pine and a warm earthy bed.

Sandalwood's aroma is often called buttery. The word tries to capture a smooth, rounded quality with little sharpness — a scent that sits close to the heater rather than leaping off it. Good material holds that smoothness for a long session at low heat. Poor material turns dry, dusty or faintly sour, and the cause is usually species, sapwood content or age rather than technique.

The resin has the opposite shape: a bright citrus lift first, then a resinous body, then a warm balsamic tail. The citrus is not an additive; it comes from the light volatile fraction. Heat too high and that fraction scorches, which is where a bitter, acrid note comes from. The same tear can read clean or burnt depending on the plate temperature.

Both vocabularies borrow the word "creamy," which causes endless confusion. In sandalwood, creamy describes a fatty, rounded wood note. In frankincense, it describes the soft resinous base under the lemon. Same adjective, different source, and the overlap says almost nothing about substitution.

| Descriptor | Sandalwood (檀香) | Frankincense (乳香) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Low, warm, slow to rise | Lemon and pine, immediate |
| Heart | Cream and sweet timber | Resin and earth |
| Base | Buttery, stays put | Balsamic, softens |
| Fade | Even and long | Quick at the top, slower below |
| Common flaw | Dusty, sour, thin | Bitter, acrid, sooty |

## Heat: Why One Instruction Means Opposite Things

**Direct answer:** Neither material is meant to sit in a flame, yet the reasons diverge: wood needs steady warmth to give up its aroma slowly, while resin needs enough heat to melt but not enough to scorch the volatile fraction.

Wood, heated:

warmth soaks into the core → oils move toward the surface → the santalol fraction leaves slowly → scent gathers over minutes and stays.

Resin, heated:

warmth softens the lump → it melts before it burns → the lightest compounds leave first → lemon arrives within seconds → the heavier fraction trails behind.

What follows is practical. Being wood, it will smoulder on a coal or inside a stick. The resin does not want flame at all: fire chars the surface, and charred resin smells of burnt sugar rather than lemon. A charcoal disc works when the coal is buried under ash or separated by a mica plate. An electric heater gives finer control and less smoke, which matters in a small room.

Smoke is the shared caveat. Any smouldering or melting operation produces particulate matter, and indoor use needs ventilation. Resin on a hot coal can produce more visible smoke than a low electric warmer. That is a room-management issue, not a reason to rank one material above the other.

## A Supply Picture With Two Different Pressures

**Direct answer:** The wood is limited by biology and law — decades to a fragrant core, and a CITES listing for wild *S. album*. The resin is limited by harvest discipline, with reports of over-tapping in several parts of its range.

Sandalwood takes decades to build fragrant heartwood, which is why plantation supply and Australian *S. spicatum* carry so much of the market. Wild *Santalum album* is listed under CITES, so legal trade in wild Indian material runs through permits rather than open sale. A seller who claims wild *S. album* in bulk without paperwork is making a claim the permit system makes hard to sustain.

Frankincense trees are tapped by cutting bark and collecting the exudate. The tree needs recovery time between cuts. Where demand outruns oversight, trees are cut harder and more often; the reports describe weaker resin and a stand under strain. Buying graded tears does not by itself guarantee good practice; asking about harvest cycle and origin is the practical check.

| Pressure | Sandalwood | Frankincense |
|---|---|---|
| Growth time | Decades to fragrant heartwood | Years to a tappable tree |
| Legal constraint | CITES listing for wild *S. album* | Origin and harvest-practice dependent |
| Main supply today | Plantation *S. album*, Australian *S. spicatum* | Wild and semi-managed stands |
| Failure mode | Illegal or mislabelled wild wood | Over-tapping, short rest cycles |
| Buyer check | Species, origin, permit basis | Species, origin, harvest cycle |

## Forms: The Same Material in Different Bodies

**Direct answer:** A chip, a spoon of powder, a stick and a vial of oil are four different products even when they start from the same wood. Surface area, dilution and the route heat takes into the aroma all change with form.

A sandalwood chip exposes a small surface and releases slowly. Powder exposes far more surface per gram, so it gives a stronger initial release and burns faster when it is loose. A stick adds a binder and a burn path, which is why stick performance depends as much on the binder and the charcoal base as on the wood. An oil is a distillate: it carries part of the aroma profile and leaves the wood's heavier, resinous fraction behind.

Frankincense follows a parallel logic. A whole tear melts from the outside inward, so size changes the timing. Powder melts almost at once and can scorch at the edges before the centre warms. An oil captures the volatile top notes and loses much of the balsamic base.

The practical rule is to judge a material in the form you will actually use. The same wood, chipped or milled, can read two ways on one heater, and neither reading is mistaken.

| Form | What changes | Best use | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood chips | Slow, uneven release | Long sessions on a coal bed | Scorched edge, faint centre |
| Sandalwood powder | Fast, strong release | Blending, stick making | Dust, uneven burn |
| Sandalwood stick | Binder plus a burn path | Everyday use | Binder smell, synthetic oil |
| Sandalwood oil | Concentrated top, no wood body | Perfume, stick scenting | Unknown carrier and dilution |
| Frankincense tears | Graded, slow melt | Warming, ritual use | Soot at high heat |
| Frankincense powder | Fast melt, mixed grade | Blending | Hides the grade of the input |
| Frankincense oil | Bright top, thin base | Fragrance work | Volatile loss in storage |

## From Material to Stick

**Direct answer:** A stick is an engineered delivery system, not a material in a new shape. The fragrant component is one of three parts, and the other two decide how it burns.

Incense sticks combine a fragrant load, a binder, and a base that keeps the coal alive. Workshops use plant-derived binders — bark or gum powders — or a prepared charcoal base, and recipes vary widely. What matters to a buyer is that the smell of a stick is the sum of all three parts, not a window onto the raw material.

Sandalwood in a stick behaves as a base load. Its powder is dense, burns slowly, and needs a binder that will not fight its creaminess. A stick carrying a high proportion of the wood tends to burn cool and long, and it can struggle to stay lit when the binder is weak or the stick is thick.

Frankincense in a stick behaves as a top note and a burn modifier. Resin powder melts before it burns, so it can slow the coal front, soften the smoke and release its citrus early. A stick with too much resin may stall or burn unevenly, because melted resin seals the surface it sits on.

The two chains, side by side:

wood powder → dense, slow-burning load → a cool and even coal front → a long session with a creamy tail.

resin powder → melts ahead of the coal front → volatiles leave early in the burn → a bright opening and a weaker finish → a stick that may stall if the load is high.

That is why a maker testing a formula changes one variable at a time: binder share, powder mesh, stick diameter, drying time. A blend that smells right in a bowl can fail in a stick, because a stick's burn path is nothing like an open pile.

Drying matters as much as the recipe. A stick dried too fast cracks; one dried too slowly can grow mould or pick up a stale note. The workshop's climate, rather than the fragrance, often decides whether a batch comes out consistent.

A stick also tells you about itself while it burns. Ash that stays pale and holds its shape points to clean combustion and a modest charcoal load; ash that crumbles dark points to a hotter base or a wet stick. A curl to one side usually means uneven drying rather than a fault in the fragrance. A stick that needs relighting has too little glow-retaining base or too much resin at the surface. None of this identifies the material, and all of it narrows the field before the nose is consulted.

For a buyer, the useful inference is that "sandalwood stick" or "frankincense stick" describes a formula, not a purity claim. A stick can contain genuine material and still smell poor because the binder, the charcoal or the drying was wrong. That is a quality problem separate from substitution, and it is common enough to check.

## A Sample Protocol for a Workshop

**Direct answer:** A sample is only useful when it is labelled, coded and tested the same way twice. Otherwise the result is an impression, not a comparison.

A workable sequence:

1. **Ask for a labelled sample.** Species, origin, form, harvest or production date, and the seller's own grade term. A sample without a label cannot be compared with anything later.
2. **Code the samples before testing.** Remove the seller's name from the table, number the lots, and record the code separately. Expectation is the largest single source of error in sensory work.
3. **Test the same dose and the same heat.** Use a scale for solids and a consistent piece count for resin. Run each sample at the temperature that suits its material.
4. **Score in three passes.** Opening, middle and tail, each on a simple scale: presence, character, off-notes. Write the score at the time; do not reconstruct it afterwards.
5. **Keep half of each sample.** The retained portion lets you re-test a surprising result, and it is the only defence against a batch that changes between sample and delivery.
6. **Repeat the winner.** A single good session can be luck. Two sessions on different days, ideally with a different nose in the room, turn a result into a pattern.

What this protocol cannot do is decide for you. It produces a record that makes a decision defensible, which is a different and more useful thing than a verdict.

## Reading Ash and Residue

**Direct answer:** What a burn leaves behind is a cheap cross-check on what went into the stick or onto the plate.

Wood ash is fine and pale when the material is clean and the burn is complete. Gritty or dark residue suggests mineral filler, incomplete combustion or a coal that ran too hot. Resin leaves a sticky film on the plate and little ash; a residue that is dry and crusty usually means the plate was overheated. None of these signs identifies a species or a grade on its own. They are filters that tell you when to look closer, which is the same job the water test does for agarwood: a first pass, never a verdict.

## Substitution: Two Very Different Risk Profiles

**Direct answer:** Few incense materials are copied as often as the wood, and few as rarely as the resin. That asymmetry decides what a buyer should check first.

Sandalwood fails in three ways. Species substitution swaps a cheaper *Santalum* for *S. album* and relies on the buyer not knowing the difference. Oil-for-wood substitution sells a scented carrier or an essential oil preparation as if it were wood. Synthetic fragrance appears in cheap sticks that contain no sandalwood at all. The first two are plausible at mid price; the third dominates the bottom of the market.

The resin side fails differently. Faking it outright rarely pays, since the raw material is not costly enough to make synthesis worthwhile. What remains open is species, grade and form: Arabia's *B. sacra* or an ordinary commercial species, whole clean tears or dusty fragments, powder from decent input or from sweepings.

| Failure mode | Sandalwood | Frankincense |
|---|---|---|
| Species swap | Common — other *Santalum* sold as *S. album* | Possible, usually disclosed by origin |
| Oil sold as solid | Common | Rare |
| Synthetic fragrance | Dominant at the cheap end | Uncommon |
| Grade inflation | "Premium" without species | Dust sold as tears; mixed grades |
| What to verify | Species, wood vs oil, origin | Species, tear quality, form |

The shared lesson is the one the [comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) makes across all incense materials: substitution risk belongs in the buying decision, not in the footnotes. A material with high substitution risk needs a named species and a traceable seller. A material with low substitution risk needs a grade check and a look at the actual lot.

## A Fair Comparison Method

**Direct answer:** A comparison is only as good as its controls — same dose, same room, same nose, and enough of a gap between samples that the first one does not colour the second.

Most comparisons fail before the first sniff. A fat lump versus a thin chip; a fierce coal versus a gentle warmer; air moving versus air still. The verdict that follows usually credits the material, not the arrangement. The fix is unglamorous procedure.

1. **Match the dose.** Compare by mass or by a consistent piece size. Resin is denser than wood, so equal volumes are not equal doses.
2. **Heat each material the way it wants.** Forcing both onto one plate temperature judges one of them at a setting built for the other. Run each at its recommended heat, then compare the experiences.
3. **Hold the room constant.** Ventilation, humidity and background smells change perception more than most buyers expect.
4. **Smell in stages.** Record the opening, the middle and the tail separately. A material that opens well and dies at the tail is a different product from one that builds slowly and holds.
5. **Reset between samples.** Ten to fifteen minutes of fresh air, water, and a neutral smell such as the back of your own hand. Coffee beans are the usual shorthand, though the effect is partly a change of context rather than a true reset.
6. **Write it down.** Memory for scent is short and strongly influenced by expectation, especially when a label has already told you what to smell.

What this method cannot do is rank the two materials. It can establish how each behaves under conditions that suit it, which is the only comparison that means anything. The rest is preference, and preference is allowed to be arbitrary.

## Storage and Drift

**Direct answer:** Frankincense changes faster than sandalwood because its bright top notes are the most volatile part of the resin. Sandalwood is comparatively stable, but it picks up odours and suffers from damp.

Storage changes resin along a predictable path:

exposure to light and warmth → the lightest terpenes oxidise first → the lemon edge goes → what remains is the balsamic body → a stored tear smells rounder and duller, never brighter.

That drift is not spoilage. The resin still works; it has simply lost the fraction that made it vivid. A sealed jar in a cool, dark place slows the process. A warm windowsill accelerates it.

Sandalwood's oils are heavier and evaporate slowly, so the wood keeps its character for a long time. Its enemies are different: damp that encourages mould, storage next to solvents or spices that the wood absorbs, and surface dust that burns into a sour note. Ground material ages faster than solid: more surface per gram, more contact with air. A long-opened jar of dust reads stale beside a batch milled that week.

| Material | Main enemy | Early sign | What helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood chips | Damp, odour pickup | Musty note, dull surface | Sealed jar, dry, dark |
| Sandalwood powder | Surface exposure, dust | Flat smell, clumping | Small jars, grind in small batches |
| Frankincense tears | Heat and light | Weak citrus, dull surface | Sealed, cool, dark |
| Frankincense powder | Volatile loss | Faint smell, clumping | Buy whole, grind to order |

## Troubleshooting the First Ten Minutes

**Direct answer:** Most disappointing sessions are temperature or dose errors rather than bad material, and the symptom usually names the cause.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Resin tastes bitter or acrid | Plate too hot; the surface is charring | Lower the setting, add a mica or foil layer |
| Nothing smells at all | Too little material, too little heat, or a stale lot | Add a piece, warm gradually, try fresh stock |
| Wood smells sharp or sour | Sapwood, or the wrong species | Change lot; test the species claim |
| Smoke without scent | Poor combustion and weak airflow | Improve airflow; use a better coal or warmer |
| Resin is gone in minutes | A small tear spends its volatiles fast | Use a larger piece, accept a short session |
| Melted resin fuses to the plate | It has run through | Line the plate; warm lower |
| Stick smells of ash or cardboard | Binder and charcoal base dominate | Try another format or maker |
| Room feels stuffy, eyes sting | Smoke load in a closed space | Ventilate, cut the dose, stop if irritation persists |

The last row is a safety note, not a medical one. Incense smoke is particulate matter, and a small closed room concentrates it quickly. Anyone who reacts badly should stop the session and air the room rather than push through.

## Where Each Sits in a Blend

**Direct answer:** Sandalwood supplies the floor and frankincense the ceiling; most blend problems are solved by deciding which end is missing rather than by adding more of everything.

The structural chain:

a composition needs a base to hold its shape → sandalwood's heavy volatiles release late → the blend keeps a floor under the brighter notes → frankincense's light volatiles release early → the opening has lift → the two together read as a sequence rather than a single flat note.

Dosage is asymmetric. A small amount of resin can dominate the opening, while sandalwood needs mass before it registers as a base. That is why blends built on the pair tend to look lopsided on paper: the resin is a seasoning and the wood is a body. The imbalance is functional, not an error.

In the traditional 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) reading of 合香, materials are assigned roles rather than quantities. Read that way, the soft wood supports a chief ingredient; the bright resin does the guide's work at the opening. That is a traditional framework for thinking about structure, not a rule that fixes ratios, and different schools read the roles differently. Used carefully, it explains why a blend with a strong chief material still needs a floor and an opening.

So ask which end of the formula is thin, rather than which material wins. A heavy, flat composition lacks lift at the top; a thin, restless one lacks weight underneath. Adding the wrong end makes both problems worse.

## Inspecting the Lot: Tear and Chip

**Direct answer:** Inspection is a sequence of cheap checks that narrows a seller's claim before you spend anything on a warm test — and the checks differ because one material has a grain and the other has a fracture.

Start with the sandalwood chip. Turn it in the light and look for a cut face: heartwood shows resin colour running through the grain, while sapwood stays uniformly pale and reads weak. Weight in the hand is a rough density signal, though it is easily confused by moisture. Smelling the wood cold tells you little, since it stays quiet until warmed. A chip that shouts at room temperature is either exceptional or treated. Warm a sliver on a low plate and judge the opening, the middle and the tail.

A frankincense tear gives more away cold. Whole tears with clean surfaces and some translucency rank above dusty fragments and dark lumps. Stickiness suggests heat or damp during storage rather than a defect in the resin itself. Lay a fragment on a warm plate and watch. Good material slumps gradually, spreads rather than spits, and gives lemon before the heavier balsamic note. A tear that smokes immediately or smells of burnt sugar is being overheated, not necessarily cheated.

| Check | Sandalwood chip | Frankincense tear | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface | Uniform colour, faint sheen | Pale, clean, translucent | Dust, dye, surface contamination |
| Cut face | Resin lines through grain | Glassy fracture, no grit | Sapwood sold as heartwood |
| Weight | Dense for its size | Consistent with size and grade | Wet-weight inflation |
| Cold scent | Quiet to faint | Soft resinous, slightly citrus | Perfumed or solvent-soaked stock |
| Warm scent | Creamy, holds | Citrus opening, balsamic tail | Charred or spent material |
| Residue | Fine pale ash | Sticky melt, little grit | Mineral filler or adulterant |

One piece cannot grade a lot. Take several from across the bag, because a top layer of good tears over a base of dust is a common way to sell an average batch as a good one.

## The Cost of a Session

**Direct answer:** Price per gram is a poor comparison across forms, because the number of sessions a unit delivers depends on dose, density and waste — three things the price tag does not show.

The chain is simple once it is written out:

price per gram → dose used per session → sessions per unit → cost per session.

A material that looks cheap per gram can lose on the second and third links. Loose powder spills and burns faster than chips. A stick carries binder and charcoal that you are paying for by weight. An oil is often diluted, so the aromatic fraction per gram is lower than the bottle implies. With resin, a bag of broken fragments yields fewer clean sessions than the same weight in whole lumps: the dust chars before it can melt.

There is a second cost that never appears on a price list: the cost of finding out. A buyer who purchases a large amount of an unverified lot to save on unit price is taking a risk that a smaller sample would have retired cheaply. Sample first, then commit. That is not caution for its own sake; it is the only way to price the uncertainty.

Sandalwood's high substitution risk makes this arithmetic sharper. A perfumed stick can undercut every real one by weight and still deliver nothing; the true cost carries the wasted purchase as well as the price. The resin is copied less, which softens the sums. Grade does not vanish, though: sweepings sold as whole lumps exist, and they move the cost of a session.

## Reading a Lot Before You Buy

**Direct answer:** Ask the wood which species, whether the lot is wood or oil, and where it grew. Ask the resin which species, how clean the tears are, and what form it takes. A label that will not name the plant has answered none of these.

Paperwork is the last check, not the first. Species on the label, form, origin, and — for wild *S. album* — a permit basis. The physical sequence above does the rest, and it is the part a seller cannot write for you.

## Traditional Standing, and the Limit of That Language

**Direct answer:** Each has centuries of ritual and medical writing behind it, in China and elsewhere. That writing is cultural history, not evidence of effect.

Chinese workshops put the wood to work in sticks, 合香 blends and temple offerings; its sweetness makes it a natural base or partner. Conventionally it is named beside agarwood as one of the two reference woods. In churches, temples and fumigation rites the resin usually appears beside 没药 (myrrh).

Both appear in classical materia medica. That record is a historical claim about how they were used, not a modern finding about what they do. Where this page mentions traditional use, it means traditional belief or historical practice, and nothing more. No therapeutic or medical claim is made for either material, and readers should treat any modern sales copy that makes such claims as outside the evidence.

Notice the pairing: one wood is read against agarwood, one resin against myrrh. That pairing is a sanity check — solids against solids, exudates against exudates. A sales story that mixes the two carelessly is worth a second look.

## Two Judgments Worth Keeping Apart

**Direct answer:** Aroma preference is a taste judgment; material quality is a set of measurable and inspectable properties. Mixing them is how buyers end up paying for a word.

The first correction is categorical. Sandalwood is not a resin, and frankincense is not a wood, no matter how many product pages use "aromatic resin" as a catch-all. With the categories settled, the questions sort themselves. For a solid: which species, how much scented core, how much oil. For an exudate: which species, how clean the lumps, how good the batch. Mix the categories and you get the odd advice that one can stand in for the other by weight — advice that fails twice, on heat and on structure.

The second judgment is about price. Sandalwood costs more than frankincense in most markets, and the gap is usually read as a quality ranking. It is not. The gap tracks growth time, heartwood yield, plantation economics and substitution pressure. A well-handled resin can beat a badly handled chip on every count, and still cost less. Price is a supply signal first and a quality signal second.

My editorial position: buy the behaviour you need. If a blend wants a slow, creamy floor, the wood is doing a job the resin cannot do, and paying for a good piece is rational. If a blend wants a bright opening, the resin is the right tool and a costly chip will not substitute. "Better" has no fixed answer here, since the two never compete for one position.

*These are editorial judgments, not verified facts.*

## Two Worked Examples

**Direct answer:** A comparison only sharpens when it is tied to a decision. Two invented cases, clearly hypothetical, show how the checks fall into order.

**Case one: a stick maker with a flat blend.** A formula built on a creamy wood reads heavy and lifeless, with no lift at the opening. The instinct is to add more of the expensive wood. The diagnosis says otherwise: the base is already full, and what is missing is an opening. A small load of resin, warmed low and blended in, gives the first minute a lift without changing the body. The point: weight is the wrong unit of exchange. Role is the right one, and even then only within the role.

**Case two: a buyer with two resin lots.** Lot A is pale, whole and smells of lemon at room temperature. Lot B is darker, dustier and smells faintly of stored cardboard. The price difference is small. The correct comparison is not A versus B in the abstract but A versus B on the buyer's own heater, at the same dose, on two different days. Suppose one lot starts clean and dies young, while the other starts flat and ends warm. What separates them is need: a beginning or an ending. Neither lot earns the word "better" on its own.

Both cases land on one rule — name the job, then choose. That order is the whole method, and it is the opposite of starting from a grade word.

## Packaging, Repacking and the Loss In Between

**Direct answer:** Most material is damaged after purchase rather than before: repacking, moisture and label transfer cost more quality than a mediocre lot does.

A sealed original pack tells you what you bought. Decant into a jar with no label and three losses happen together: you forget which lot it was, the contents start trading moisture with the air, and more surface is exposed.

For wood, the risk is moisture and odour. Chips are dense, but end grain takes up water; a jar opened in a humid kitchen and closed again traps that moisture inside. The first sign is a musty note that was not there at purchase. A second sign is a pale bloom on the surface, which is a storage problem rather than a material one.

For resin, the risk is heat and light. A tear left in a warm room softens, sticks to its neighbours and loses the light fraction that made it bright. Repacking into small, full jars cuts the air space; the same logic as keeping coffee in a small tin rather than a large one.

Label transfer matters more than it sounds. Write species, origin, supplier, date and form on the new container before the material goes in. A blend drawn from three unlabelled jars cannot be reproduced, not even by the person who made it.

None of this is expensive. It is the difference between a lot that is still comparable six months later and one that has quietly become anonymous.

## The Second Purchase

**Direct answer:** The first purchase teaches you what the material does; the second should test whether the seller can repeat it.

A short list for the return visit:

1. **Bring the reference.** Keep a labelled portion of the first lot and smell it beside the new one. Memory alone will flatter the new purchase.
2. **Ask what changed.** Harvest, species, grind — any of the three accounts for a shift. A seller who cannot name which one is guessing.
3. **Check the form you actually use.** A superb chip lot is no help if the workshop runs powder.
4. **Re-test the failing edge.** If the first lot scorched too easily, the second should be tested at the same heat, not a gentler one.
5. **Decide before you open the bag.** Once the material is in the jar, the label is gone and the comparison is over.

The second purchase is where a supplier relationship is either confirmed or quietly ends. That is a commercial judgment, not a sensory one, and it belongs in the same notebook as the tasting notes.

## FAQ

### Q: Is 檀香 the same material as 乳香?

No. 檀香 is wood cut from a *Santalum* trunk (Santalaceae); 乳香 is hardened exudate from *Boswellia* bark (Burseraceae). One is solid, the other is dried sap. They share a shelf because they fill opposite ends of a scent, not because they are related.

### Q: Why does the timber cost more?

Usually. Price follows species, origin, how much of the piece is fragrant core, and grade. *S. album* is expensive; a cheap "sandalwood" stick is normally perfume on a carrier. Resin is abundant by comparison, though *B. sacra* sits above ordinary commercial lots.

### Q: Can the two be heated the same way?

No. Wood smoulders on a coal or in a stick. Resin must melt; flame chars it and turns the citrus note bitter. Use an electric warmer or a coal under ash, and ventilate — both produce smoke.

### Q: Which material attracts more counterfeits?

The wood. At the cheap end: another species sold as *S. album*, oil sold as solid wood, perfume with no wood at all. Resin is seldom faked outright; species, tear cleanliness and powder quality are the real questions. See the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

### Q: How do the two pairings work — woods with woods, resins with resins?

Chinese practice reads the wood beside 沉香: one soft and anchoring, the other darker and layered. Its partner is 没药 (myrrh). Both come from Burseraceae, though myrrh is a *Commiphora* product — bitter where the other is sharp and citric. See [myrrh](/ingredients/myrrh/).

## Summary

Sandalwood is a wood; frankincense is a resin. Three consequences follow: a different approach to heat, a different seat in the formula, and supply troubles that share almost nothing. The wood's hazards are swaps, oil sold as timber, and perfume sticks; the resin's are grade, species and how the trees were cut. Species and form for the solid; species and lump quality for the exudate. Those four checks cover the decisions that change what arrives in the box. The rest — which one someone prefers — is taste, and taste is not a grade.

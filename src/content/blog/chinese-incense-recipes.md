**Direct answer:** A 香方 (xiāngfāng) is a working classical Chinese incense formula — not a scent blurb — written into the canonical manuals (香谱), naming the ingredients, fixing their proportions, and spelling out the grinding, steaming, and aging that turns them into a burnable blend.

**Key facts:** This cluster renders a selection of classical and revival formulas into English, almost none of which has ever been rendered into English anywhere. The manuals are the deepest and least-translated layer of Chinese incense culture, and that gap is exactly what these pages set out to narrow.

## What a 香方 Actually Is

**Direct answer:** A 香方 is a recipe in the kitchen sense — a bill of materials plus a procedure — and it is the basic unit the entire classical canon is organized around.

The term breaks down plainly: 香 (xiāng) is fragrance, 方 (fāng) is a formula or prescription. Put together, a 香方 names what goes in, how much of each, and what to do with it — grind, steam, soak, age, form, burn. The three canonical collections (陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱, 香乘) are, at bottom, recipe books: hundreds of entries, each a working formula rather than a description of how something smells.

That framing is the thing the English-language web almost completely overlooks. A search for "Chinese incense recipe" surfaces modern DIY blends and essential-oil projects, not the classical canon. These pages are a small, careful bridge across that gap.

**Why the recipe form matters:** a formula is *reproducible knowledge* — you can hold a 香方 in your hand and attempt it — whereas a scent description is just an opinion about a finished product. The manuals preserved the tradition precisely because they wrote it down as procedures, not impressions. That is the difference between a craft that survives a thousand years and one that dies with its practitioners.

## How to Read a Recipe Honestly

**Direct answer:** Three rules govern every page in this cluster, and skipping any of them turns a careful translation into a romanticized retelling.

1. **The famous name is almost always legend.** "苏轼," "寿阳公主," "花蕊夫人" act as cultural markers — recipe *titles*, not documented authors. Read the famous name as part of the formula's identity, not as a byline.
2. **The quantities shift by dynasty.** The historical weights (两, 钱) carried different gram values across periods, and the same formula often survives in several disagreeing versions. The ratio between items is the durable content, not the grams.
3. **A few entries are modern, not ancient.** 二苏旧局 is a contemporary composition in the classical idiom, not a 香谱 original. It belongs in the cluster, but it must be labeled as what it is.

The honest-reader test is simple: **if a page hands you a famous name and an exact gram count without flagging either as uncertain, treat it with suspicion.** The real record is messier than that, and the mess is the point.

## Weights, Measures, and Why the Ratio Is the Only Safe Reading

**Direct answer:** Classical formulas weigh their materials in historical Chinese units, and the only reliable reading is the ratio between ingredients, never a converted gram figure.

| Unit | Relation | Rough Song–Ming mass |
|---|---|---|
| 两 (liǎng) | the base unit | on the order of 37 g |
| 钱 (qián) | one-tenth of a 两 | about 3.7 g |
| 分 (fēn) | one-tenth of a 钱 (one-hundredth of a 两) | about 0.37 g |

Beyond these three, recipes reach for two other ways of counting. Some tally individual pieces (枚), the way one entry writes "一百枚白梅" (a hundred white plums); others decline to count at all, using the open-ended "不计多少" (in whatever amount), which is how 柏子香 phrases it. And at least one cluster entry — 二苏旧局 — is contemporary and fixes no ratio whatsoever.

The reason this caveat recurs on every page is that it is exactly where home reproduction stalls: the 两 was never a stable gram weight. Depending on the era it sat anywhere near 31–41 g, and it moved with region and use, so a gram figure is always a rough guess. The trustworthy reading is therefore proportional — "一两沉香, 一钱檀香" means a 10:1 split between the two woods, and that split, rather than any converted mass, is what the formula actually says. Each recipe page here records its quantities (or flags them unverified) under this same rule.

## The 君臣佐使 Structure, in Four Questions

**Direct answer:** Every 合香 formula can be unpacked into four roles — chief, minister, assistant, guide — by asking four questions in order.

- **Which material dominates?** (the chief — almost always a wood, usually agarwood or sandalwood)
- **Which material rounds it out?** (the minister — a smaller wood or resin that supports the chief)
- **Which material gives it its name?** (the assistant — the signature note the blend is known for)
- **Which material holds it together?** (the guide — often a binder or a process, like honey or steaming)

Treat the four questions as a diagnostic, not a form to fill from a chart. A formula can leave the minister seat empty (柏子香), or hand the assistant role to two ingredients working in opposite directions (二苏旧局's frankincense and amber). The test is whether the *structure* stands — a dominant carrier, a support, a signature, and a finish — regardless of how many items sit in each seat. The [合香 blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) lays out the full framework.

## Why the Grind and the Age Matter

**Direct answer:** Two processing verbs — grinding and aging — show up across almost every formula because they change how the material burns, not just how it looks.

**Chain one — fine grinding raises the surface area, so the burn evens out.** A resin or wood chip burns from its exposed face. Grinding it into powder multiplies that face many times over → each grain heats and releases at once → the fragrance unfolds evenly instead of flaring in patches → a steady, predictable scent rather than a sputtering one. The same logic governs why recipes specify how fine to grind: a coarse grind smolders slowly and unevenly, a fine grind burns fast and uniformly, and the formula's chosen texture is part of the design, not an afterthought.

**Chain two — aging lets the volatiles settle into each other, so the sharp edges soften.** Fresh-ground materials carry bright, aggressive top notes. Sealed away for days or weeks (the 窨 step), the mixture's volatiles intermingle → the harshest notes dull → the blend reads as rounder and more coherent than the sum of its parts. This is why so many formulas close with "age (窨), then burn" — the resting period is a chemical step, not a scheduling nicety. *The reasoning is a modern reconstruction of the classical steps, stated as traditional practice rather than a measured result.*

## Classical 香方 vs Modern DIY Recipe: A Field Guide

**Direct answer:** A classical 香方 and a modern DIY incense recipe look similar on paper but differ in what they assume you already know.

| Dimension | Classical 香方 | Modern DIY recipe |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 两/钱/分, pieces, or "in whatever amount" | Grams or teaspoons |
| Author | Often legendary, rarely signed | Named, dated, photographed |
| Precision | Ratios, frequently variable by version | Exact quantities for reproducibility |
| Intent | Record the formula for posterity | Let a beginner replicate it now |
| Materials | Agarwood, musk, resins, woods | Often oils, premixed bases, extracts |

The mismatch is instructive: a classical formula was written by a practitioner *for* practitioners, and it assumes you can fill the gaps a beginner cannot. A modern recipe reverses that — it spells everything out precisely because its reader is a novice. Reading a 香方 as if it were a modern recipe is the single most common misstep in this whole domain.

## Reading a Formula: Where to Start

**Direct answer:** The fastest honest entry point is to ignore the famous name and look straight at the ratio line — what dominates, and what barely registers — then ask what role each item plays.

A beginner facing a 香方 for the first time usually does two things that get in the way: they fixate on the legendary author, and they try to convert everything to grams. Neither leads anywhere useful. The productive approach is three moves, in order:

1. **Find the largest quantity.** That item is almost always the chief — the wood that carries the blend. If agarwood is six times the sandalwood, you know where the body sits.
2. **Find the smallest quantity.** The trace item measured in 钱 or 分 is usually the signature — the note the blend is named for, like camphor in 雪中春信 or borneol in a court mix.
3. **Spot the binder or process.** Honey, or a steaming step, or an ash — the thing that turns a powder into a burnable form and shapes how it releases.

Those three moves give you the structure of any formula in about a minute, without a single gram conversion. After that, the role table on each recipe page is just the same reading written out in full.

## The Milling Chain: Why the Grind Grade Sits Inside the Formula

**Direct answer:** The grind size a 香方 specifies is not a convenience — it is a dial on the burn, because the fineness of the powder sets how fast heat moves through the mass and how evenly the fragrance releases.

Follow one mechanism from instruction to smoke. A formula that asks for a fine grind is asking for a specific fire behavior:

coarse grains leave gaps between particles → air pockets slow the ember → the burn smolders unevenly, flaring where the resin is exposed → the scent reads patchy and sputtering.

fine, uniform powder packs tight → heat moves grain to grain → the whole surface warms at once → the fragrance unfolds as a steady, even wave → the blend reads smooth rather than stuttering.

The same logic runs backward in recipes that deliberately keep a material coarse — a chip left whole burns slow and low, which is why 江南李主帐中香 threads the agarwood rather than grinding it. The grind is a chosen texture, and the texture is part of the design.

## The Misconception Worth Correcting: "Authentic" Does Not Mean "Fixed"

**Direct answer:** The most common error a newcomer makes is to treat a 香方 as a single, settled recipe — when the honest record shows the same formula surviving in several disagreeing versions, with quantities drifting by dynasty.

The word "authentic" invites a false picture. It suggests one correct text, frozen in time, that a faithful page should reproduce exactly. The real canon is messier:

## The Aging Step, Traced End to End

**Direct answer:** The 窨 (xūn) aging step that closes so many formulas is a slow equalization, not a rest — the sealed mixture's volatiles migrate until the sharp edges blur and the blend reads as one thing rather than a pile of parts.

Trace the step as a physical process. A freshly mixed formula is still a collection of separate materials, each with its own smell. Sealing them together sets a migration in motion:

fresh-ground materials carry bright, aggressive top notes → sealed together, the volatiles intermingle across the whole mass → the harshest notes react and dull → the mixture's scent evens out → the blend reads rounder and more coherent than the sum of its parts.

That is why the instruction recurs as "age (窨), then burn" — the resting period is doing chemical work, not waiting out a clock. A formula burned the day it is mixed tastes like its ingredients; the same formula burned after its aging reads like a single, settled scent. The difference between those two experiences is the entire point of the step. *This reconstruction of the aging mechanism is modern inference applied to the classical instruction, stated as traditional practice rather than a measured result.*

## Reading a 香方's Cost From Its Materials Alone

**Direct answer:** A formula's social register is legible from its ingredient list before a single proportion is read — the presence of musk, two grades of agarwood, or a humble seed tells you which room the incense was made for.

The material list is a class signature. Scan the chief and the accents and the register resolves:

musk and multiple agarwood grades → courtly, opulent → the blend was made to display wealth.

a single common wood and a signature note → scholar, refined → the blend was made for taste on a budget.

a roasted seed, "in whatever amount" → folk, humble → the blend was made for practice over luxury.

No proportion is needed to place these; the ingredients alone carry the register. That is a useful skill for reading any classical formula at a glance — before the ratios, before the processing, the cost of the materials has already told you what kind of incense this was. *The three-band reading is an editorial interpretation, not a claim in the source texts.*

## The Four Verbs That Do the Real Work

**Direct answer:** Beyond the ingredient list, a 香方's processing verbs — grind, steam, soak, age — carry most of the craft, because they decide the texture, the release, and the final smoothness of the burn.

The verbs are the formula's working half, and each changes something measurable:

grinding sets the particle size → it controls how evenly the ember travels → a coarse grind smolders slow, a fine grind burns even.

steaming opens the materials and drives off harsh edges → it softens the woods and binds the blend's scent → the result reads rounder than an unsteamed mix.

soaking in wine, honey, or water carries a flavor or a binder into the mass → it adjusts the burn and the finish → the liquid is an ingredient, not a solvent.

aging seals the mixed powder and lets it settle → it equalizes the volatiles → the sharp notes dull and the blend coheres.

The four verbs are the difference between a pile of aromatic dust and a burnable, balanced incense. Read them as instructions on the *physics and chemistry of burning*, not as ritual decoration — the formula's author wrote them down because they change what reaches the nose. ## The Copyist's Drift: How Formulas Change in Transmission

**Direct answer:** A 香方 does not move through the centuries untouched — each copy introduces small changes, and the drift itself is part of the record a careful reader learns to watch for.

The canon was copied by hand for generations, and copying is never frictionless. Trace what happens as a formula passes from one manuscript to the next:

a scribe misreads a character or a numeral → the next copy carries the error forward → another scribe "corrects" a quantity to his own era's units → the formula forks into two versions → the same name now heads two slightly different recipes.

That drift is why the honest page reads quantities as ratios and flags attribution as legend. The variants are not noise to be discarded; they are evidence of how the formula lived. A reader who demands one clean, fixed text is asking the tradition to be tidier than it ever was — and the tidiness is the fiction, not the drift. *The transmission account is standard textual scholarship, not a documented claim about any specific manuscript.*

## FAQ

### Q: What is a 香方?
Think of it as a classical working formula, the incense equivalent of a written recipe. A 香方 names the raw materials, fixes how much of each goes in, and lays out the processing — how fine to grind, whether to steam or soak, how long to age, what shape to form — so the mixture can actually be burned. Beneath the ingredients, the best entries carry a 君臣佐使 skeleton (chief, minister, assistant, guide), the same four-role logic a Chinese herbal prescription uses. The manuals that hold these formulas — 陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱, and 《香乘》 — are, in effect, cookbooks running hundreds of entries, and virtually none of the content has ever appeared in English.

### Q: Are these formulas real, or invented?
The formulas themselves are real — they sit in the classical manuals, copied down for centuries. What is usually invented is the famous name pinned to them. "苏轼," "寿阳公主," and "花蕊夫人" function as titles and cultural markers, not bylines: there is seldom any dependable evidence that the named figure actually wrote the formula. The proportions carry their own uncertainty too, because one recipe often survives in several disagreeing versions and the historical weights shifted gram-value by dynasty. So the honest position is to accept the name as a title, trust the ratios as the durable content, and flag the attribution as traditional rather than verified.

### Q: Could I actually make these at home?
In principle, yes, with genuine caveats attached. The historical weights are not fixed grams — the 两 drifted by dynasty — so you follow the ratio between ingredients, not a converted gram figure. Some materials are scarce or controlled: genuine musk and 奇楠-grade agarwood are rare, expensive, and sometimes trade-restricted, and a few entries are modern revivals rather than ancient originals. Because of all that, these pages treat the formulas primarily as historical and cultural study — a window into how a blend was structured — rather than as a precise, turnkey DIY specification.

### Q: Why is there so little English coverage?
Because the source material is long, dense, and commercially useless to most English sites. The manuals run hundreds of entries across 陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱, and 《香乘》, composed in a compact classical idiom full of specialized vocabulary — 合香, 奇楠, 沉水 — that has no settled English equivalent. Product-focused sites have no incentive to do the work: they sell scents and attach wellness claims, not translate a technical canon. The practical result is that an English search for "Chinese incense recipe" mostly surfaces modern blends instead of the classical record, and that missing record is precisely the gap this cluster is trying to fill.

### Q: Why do some recipes measure in pieces instead of weights?
Because a piece count sidesteps the gram problem entirely. Where a weight in 两 or 钱 can only be read as a ratio — the 两 itself shifted mass across dynasties — a count of items fixes a quantity that needs no conversion. One entry writes "一百枚白梅," a hundred white plums, and the number means the same thing in any era. A piece count is the formula's way of being precise without pretending the units were stable. It tells you to count, not to weigh, and that instruction survives the centuries intact.

### Q: What should I actually look for when I open a 香方?
Three things, in order: the largest quantity, which names the chief; the smallest, which usually names the signature; and the processing verbs — grind, steam, soak, age — which tell you how the blend is meant to burn. Ignore the famous name and skip the gram conversion until those three are clear. The structure you find that way is the formula's real content, and everything else on the page is commentary on it.

### Q: How do I tell an ancient formula from a modern one?
Look at the source it cites. A formula traced to 香乘 or the Song 香谱 is a classical entry (attribution flagged, proportions in 两/钱). A formula traced to 陈云君's contemporary 燕居香语 — like 二苏旧局 — is a modern composition wearing the old idiom, and its proportions are typically loose rather than fixed. The marker is the manual, not the style: a modern author can write convincingly "in the classical manner," which is exactly why the page labels these entries rather than letting the idiom speak for itself.

**Direct answer:** A 香方 (xiāngfāng) is the Chinese equivalent of a working recipe: materials, the share each one takes, and the steps that make them burnable. It was composed to be executed rather than admired, and the handbooks (香谱) hold hundreds of examples.

**Key facts:** This hub carries seven of those formulas, from a court blend needing two agarwood grades to a scholar's roasted seed. The manuals they come from remain almost untouched in English, and that neglect is what the cluster answers.

## What a 香方 Actually Is

**Direct answer:** A 香方 is a recipe in the kitchen sense — materials, quantities, method — and that unit, repeated hundreds of times, is what the classical canon consists of.

The word divides cleanly: 香 (xiāng) is fragrance, 方 (fāng) is a formula or prescription. Put the two together and you get an instruction set — what goes in, how much of each, and what to do with it. Grind, steam, soak, age, form, burn: the verbs belong to the formula, not to commentary around it.

The three canonical collections — 陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱, and 香乘 — are recipe books first. Hundreds of entries, each one a working formula. They kept the craft alive precisely by recording procedure instead of an impression of how something smells.

Three words get conflated in English writing on the subject, and separating them prevents most confusion. 香谱 names the book — a catalogue of formulas, not one recipe. 香方 names a single formula inside it. 合香 names the practice of building a compound incense from several materials. A manual holds formulas; a formula describes a blend; the blend is the craft. Keeping the three apart makes the rest of this page easier to follow.

What the English-language web tends to miss is the genre itself. Ask a search engine for a Chinese incense recipe and the results are modern blends and essential-oil projects; the canon does not surface. These pages are a narrow, careful bridge across that gap.

**Why the form matters:** a formula is reproducible knowledge — you can hold it in your hand and attempt it — while a scent description is merely an opinion about a finished product. A craft survives a thousand years when its procedures are written down, and dies with its practitioners when only impressions are left.

## Reading One Honestly: Name, Ratio, Era

**Direct answer:** Three rules govern every page in this cluster. Skip any one of them and a careful translation turns into a romanticized retelling.

1. **The famous name is almost always legend.** 苏轼, 寿阳公主, and 花蕊夫人 serve as cultural markers and as titles for a formula, not as documented authors. Treat the name as part of the identity.
2. **Quantities shift with the dynasty.** The old weights (两, 钱) held different gram values in different periods, and one formula often survives in disagreeing versions. What lasts is the proportion between items.
3. **A few entries are recent.** 二苏旧局 was composed in the twentieth century in the classical idiom; it is not a 香谱 original and should be labeled as what it is.

The test a reader can apply to any page: **if it hands you a famous name and an exact gram count without flagging either as uncertain, be suspicious.** The record is messier than that, and the mess is the point.

| Signal | What an honest page does | What should worry you |
|---|---|---|
| Attribution | Names the legend as a legend or a title | Asserts documented authorship |
| Quantity | Gives proportions and notes the dynastic drift | Prints grams as settled fact |
| Missing data | Says unverified where the source is silent | Fills the hole with a number |
| Health claims | Sticks to culture and history | Promises a therapeutic outcome |
| Citation | Points to a manual and admits its limits | Points nowhere, or points to a blog |

The logic compresses to one line: a page that smooths over uncertainty is telling you a story, not translating a record. The page with the caveats is the honest one.

## Weights, Measures, and the Ratio Rule

**Direct answer:** Classical formulas weigh materials in historical Chinese units, and the only reliable reading is the proportion between ingredients, never a converted gram figure.

| Unit | Fraction of a 两 | Indicative Song–Ming mass |
|---|---|---|
| 两 (liǎng) | 1 | roughly 37 g |
| 钱 (qián) | 1/10 | roughly 3.7 g |
| 分 (fēn) | 1/100 | roughly 0.37 g |

Two other counting habits appear beyond those three units. Some entries tally individual pieces (枚) — one writes 一百枚白梅, a hundred white plums — which sidesteps the weight problem entirely. Others decline to count at all: 不计多少, "in whatever amount", is how 柏子香 puts it. And 二苏旧局, being contemporary, fixes no ratio worth the name.

Reproduction usually stalls right here. A 两 was not a fixed mass; over the centuries its gram equivalent wandered between roughly 31 and 41, and regional practice moved it further. Converting to grams therefore buys false precision. What survives is the proportion: when a formula sets 一两沉香 against 一钱檀香, the useful statement is a ten-to-one split between the woods, and that relation — not any number in grams — is the content. Each recipe page here follows the same rule, recording quantities as given or marking them unverified where the text is silent.

## The 君臣佐使 Skeleton, in Four Questions

**Direct answer:** Any 合香 formula can be unpacked into four roles — chief, minister, assistant, guide — by asking four questions in order.

- **What carries the blend?** The chief — nearly always a wood, agarwood or sandalwood in most of these formulas.
- **What rounds it off?** The minister — a lesser wood or a resin standing behind the chief.
- **What gives it its name?** The assistant — the note the blend is known by.
- **What holds it together?** The guide — a binder or a step, honey or steaming being the usual suspects.

Treat the four questions as a diagnostic, not a form to fill in from a chart. A formula can leave the minister's seat empty (柏子香) or hand the assistant role to two ingredients pulling in opposite directions (二苏旧局 and its frankincense–amber pairing). The test is whether the structure stands — a dominant carrier, a support, a signature, a finish — regardless of how many items occupy each seat. The [合香 blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) sets out the full framework.

Seats are relational, not intrinsic. A wood that carries one blend can merely support the next, and a resin that lifts one formula can settle another. Reading a 香方 therefore means asking what each item does *here*, not what it is in the abstract. *That relational reading is an editorial interpretation of the classical roles, not a rule stated in the manuals.*

A beginner can find the skeleton in about a minute without converting a single gram: locate the largest quantity (the chief), locate the smallest (usually the signature), then locate the binder or the process step. Those three moves give the structure of almost any formula in the collection.

柏子香 makes the method concrete, and it is the awkward case worth working through. One material, roasted, measured 不计多少: the seed holds the chief's seat by default, the roasting step does the guide's work by fixing how the material releases, and the minister and assistant seats stay empty. The formula still passes the test — a carrier, a finish, nothing else — which is why a reader who can read 柏子香 can read the court blends too. Structure does not require four ingredients; it requires that a structure exist.

## Grind, Steam, Soak, Age: The Verbs That Do the Work

**Direct answer:** Beyond the ingredient list, a 香方's processing verbs carry most of the craft, because they set the texture, the release rate, and the final smoothness of the burn.

**Grinding sets the particle size, and particle size sets how the ember travels.** A resin or wood chip burns from its exposed face; powder multiplies that face many times over:

coarse grains leave air pockets → the ember slows at the gaps → the burn flares where resin is exposed → the scent reads patchy → a fine, uniform powder packs tight → heat moves grain to grain → the whole mass warms at once → the fragrance unfolds as a steady wave.

That is why recipes specify a grind grade: it is a dial on fire behavior, not a convenience. Texture is chosen, and the choice is part of the design — which is also why 江南李主帐中香 threads the agarwood rather than reducing it to powder. A chip left whole burns slow and low on purpose.

**Aging is a chemical step, not a scheduling nicety.** A freshly mixed formula is still a collection of separate materials, each smelling of itself. Sealed together (the 窨 step), migration begins:

fresh-ground materials carry bright top notes → sealed in one mass, the volatiles intermingle → the harshest notes dull → the mixture evens out → the blend reads rounder than the sum of its parts.

Burn the formula the day it is mixed and it tastes like its ingredients. Burn it after the aging and it reads as one settled scent. The gap between those two experiences is the whole point of the instruction.

Steaming and soaking work the same way, by different means. Steam opens the woods and drives off harsh edges, softening the blend. A soak in wine, honey, or water carries a flavor or a binder into the mass, so the liquid is an ingredient rather than a solvent. Read the four verbs as notes on the physics of burning; the author wrote them down because they change what reaches the nose. ## Classical 香方 vs Modern DIY: What Each Assumes

**Direct answer:** A classical 香方 and a modern DIY recipe look alike on paper but assume opposite readers — one writes for a practitioner, the other for a novice.

| Dimension | Classical 香方 | Modern DIY recipe |
|---|---|---|
| Units | 两, 钱, 分, pieces, or "in whatever amount" | Grams or teaspoons |
| Author | Often legendary, rarely signed | Named, dated, photographed |
| Precision | Ratios, often variable between versions | Exact quantities for replication |
| Intent | Record the formula for posterity | Let a beginner succeed today |
| Materials | Agarwood, musk, resins, woods | Oils, premixed bases, extracts |

The mismatch explains most misreadings. A classical formula was written by a practitioner for practitioners and leaves gaps a beginner cannot fill; a modern recipe spells everything out precisely because its reader is new. Treating a 香方 as if it were a modern recipe is the single most common error in this domain.

## The Register: What the Ingredient List Already Says

**Direct answer:** You can place almost any classical incense on the tradition's social ladder by looking at one thing — how costly its chief material is — before reading a single proportion.

| Band | Representative | Chief material | What the register signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Court / elite | 花蕊夫人衙香, 江南李主帐中香 | Agarwood in two grades, musk | Wealth and status on display |
| Scholar / literati | 雪中春信, 寿阳公主梅花香 | Sandalwood, camphor, plum | Refined taste on a budget |
| Folk / humble | 柏子香 | A roasted tree seed | Practice over luxury |

The scan is quick because the materials are a class signature. Musk and multiple agarwood grades announce a courtly blend made to display wealth. A single common wood with one signature note points to a scholar's blend made for taste on a budget. A roasted seed measured "in whatever amount" signals practice over luxury, and no ratio is needed to see it.

The register is not a quality ranking. 柏子香 is not a worse incense than 花蕊夫人衙香; it is a different social act. A court blend advertises its cost, a seed incense advertises its maker's lack of pretense. Reading the register tells you *why* a formula was written, not just what it contains. *The three-band grouping is an editorial reading, not a claim found in the source texts.*

## The Copyist's Drift: Why "Authentic" Cannot Mean "Fixed"

**Direct answer:** A 香方 did not travel through the centuries untouched. Each copy introduced small changes, and the drift is part of the record a careful reader learns to watch.

The canon was copied by hand for generations, and copying is never frictionless:

a scribe misreads a character or a numeral → the next copy carries the error forward → a later scribe "corrects" a quantity to his own era's units → the formula forks into two versions → one name now heads two slightly different recipes.

This is why the honest page reads quantities as ratios and flags attribution as legend. The variants are not noise to be discarded; they are evidence of how the formula lived. A reader who demands one clean, fixed text is asking the tradition to be tidier than it ever was, and the tidiness is the fiction rather than the drift.

Put two versions side by side and the durable content separates from the noise. Whatever survives unchanged across copies — the chief material, the ratio between items — is the backbone. Whatever shifts — quantities, minor accents — is the transmission's fingerprint. Whatever the name alone carries is legend, not recipe. Comparing turns a single text from a closed answer into an open question, which is the honest core of the whole canon.

## Why Study a Formula You Cannot Make

**Direct answer:** Even when a 香方 cannot be reproduced — the musk unavailable, the agarwood grade unaffordable, the units uncertain — the structure it teaches transfers to any blend you can actually make.

A skeptic may ask what a recipe is worth when the materials are out of reach. The answer is that the formula's lesson is the skeleton, not the finished incense:

read the chief → you learn what a dominant carrier looks like → read the assistant → you learn how a signature note is dosed → read the guide → you learn what binds a blend and paces its release → the structure transfers to whatever materials you do have.

The 君臣佐使 logic, the ratio discipline, and the four processing verbs are portable skills. A modern maker who cannot source the original materials can still borrow the architecture and the dosing habits, which is worth more than any single formula's gram count. The recipe teaches blending; the incense itself is the example, not the point.

The boundary deserves stating plainly, because the wider web does not keep it. Search for Chinese incense and wellness language arrives within a screen or two. The 香谱 canon is a technical and cultural record — materials, proportions, processing — with no promised outcome attached. These pages stay on that side of the line: the formula is craft and history, attribution is flagged as legend, quantities are read as proportions, and nothing here asserts a health effect. That restraint separates a translation from a retelling with an agenda.

## FAQ

### Q: What is a 香方?

A formula from the classical handbooks for a compound incense (合香). It gives the materials, their shares, and the processing that follows — grinding, steaming, soaking, aging, forming. The stronger entries also show a 君臣佐使 skeleton: chief, minister, assistant, guide, the four roles a Chinese herbal prescription uses. The books that hold them — 陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱 and 香乘 — run to hundreds of entries, and almost none of that has appeared in English.

### Q: How much of a 香方 is genuine?

The formulas are real, copied down over centuries. The famous name is the part usually added later. 苏轼, 寿阳公主 and 花蕊夫人 function as titles and cultural markers, not as bylines, and dependable evidence that the named figure composed a given formula is rare. Proportions carry their own uncertainty: a single formula often survives in versions that disagree, and the old weights changed value with the dynasty. Take the name as a title, the proportion as the durable content, and the attribution as traditional rather than verified.

### Q: Can a modern maker reproduce them?

Yes in principle, though not as a shopping list. Because the 两 drifted, the workable instruction is the proportion between materials rather than a weight in grams. Sourcing is the harder obstacle: true musk and 奇楠-grade agarwood are scarce, costly, and in some places restricted, and several entries here are twentieth-century compositions rather than recovered originals. Read the pages as a way to learn structure; treat making as a separate project with its own sourcing problems.

### Q: Why has nobody translated these?

The labour is large and the market is small. The entries sit across 陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱 and 香乘, written in a terse classical register and full of terms — 合香, 奇楠, 沉水 — whose English equivalents were never standardized. Translating that takes a specialist's time and earns none of the traffic a product page earns, so commercial sites skip it. What an English reader finds instead is the modern layer: oils, blanks, and blends invented last year.

### Q: Why do some recipes count pieces instead of weighing?

Because a piece count sidesteps the gram problem. A weight in 两 or 钱 can only be read as a ratio, since the 两 itself shifted mass across dynasties; a count of items fixes a quantity that needs no conversion. One entry writes 一百枚白梅, a hundred white plums, and the number means the same thing in any era. Counting is the formula's way of being precise without pretending the units were stable.

### Q: How do I tell an ancient formula from a modern one?

Look at the manual it cites. An entry traced to 香乘 or a Song 香谱 is classical, with attribution flagged and proportions in 两 or 钱. An entry traced to 陈云君's contemporary 燕居香语 — 二苏旧局 is the example here — is a modern composition wearing the old idiom, and its proportions tend to be loose rather than fixed. The marker is the manual, not the style, because a modern author can write convincingly in the classical manner.

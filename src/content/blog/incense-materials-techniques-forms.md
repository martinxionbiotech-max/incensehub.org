**Direct answer:** A finished piece of incense can be described completely by three answers: the substance it is built from, the method that shaped it, and the geometry it takes on the burner. Those answers are the materials, techniques, and forms taxonomies — 15, 12, and 12 records, each published as open data.

## Key facts

The counts are 15 materials, 12 techniques, and 12 forms, served as JSON from `data.incensehub.org` under CC BY-SA 4.0. The JSON is authoritative; the prose below is a reading aid.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Materials** name the substance: binders, a plant core, aromatic timber and gums, and the cheap fillers that give a factory stick away.
- **Techniques** cover making and attention alike: warming, shaping, composing, scenting, and the disciplined study of a finished piece.
- **Forms** name the geometry: rods, spirals, pyramids, loose meal, pellets, tablets, beads, and prepared low-smoke lines.
- Each product fills one slot per layer, and **material → technique → form** is the order in which the work actually happens.
- The datasets are open and deliberately lossy: they index terms without grading them.

## Three Questions That Separate One Craft

**Direct answer:** A single flat list of incense words mixes three different kinds of answer, and separating them is what makes the vocabulary usable.

Ask a shop assistant "what is this?" and the reply may be "sandalwood", "hand-rolled", or "coil" — three answers to three different questions. Sandalwood names a substance. Hand-rolled names a process. Coil names a shape. None of the three substitutes for the others, and a description that supplies only one leaves the other two blank.

The blank slots are where buying mistakes happen. "Sandalwood" on a box does not say whether the contents are chips for a heater, powder for a seal, or a formed stick; those are three different products with three different burn behaviours and price logics. "Hand-rolled" does not say what was rolled. "Coil" does not say what the coil is made from. Each word is accurate, and each is incomplete on its own.

Read as a sequence, the layers also describe production. A material is worked by a technique into a form, and nothing on a burner skipped a step. That is why the shorthand **material → technique → form** runs in one direction only — reverse it and you are describing an object that cannot exist.

The separation earns its keep in conversation. Two people comparing "incense" may be comparing a raw chip with a finished stick; two people comparing "sandalwood" may be comparing a wood species with a fragrance oil that borrowed the name. Naming the layer forces the comparison onto common ground. That is a small move with a large effect: it turns a taste argument into a set of checkable claims.

## What Burns: The 15 Materials

**Direct answer:** The ingredient list holds what actually burns or warms — plant binders, a bamboo core, aromatic woods and gums, and the fillers and oils that mark a manufactured stick.

The list has four functional groups rather than four shelves. Two entries make a paste workable: makko (抹香), which holds it together and keeps it burning, and honey (蜂蜜), which binds and fixes. Guar gum, gum arabic, and gum tragacanth act as minor plant glues, with the synthetic binder as their industrial substitute. Bamboo core, charcoal, and sawdust supply structure or cheap bulk. The aromatic group — wood powder or chips, frankincense and myrrh (乳香, 没药), cypress seed (柏子), white plum (白梅), clove powder (丁香) — exists to smell, with the timber warmed rather than burned. Fragrance oil stands alone as a scent made in a factory. Makko is the only entry belonging to two groups, since it both holds the paste and feeds the flame.

The factory route is easier to spot by what it lacks: no named botanical at the base, no plant binder, and a scent compounded somewhere else. The distinction is functional, not moral — one route carries the plant's own volatiles, the other carries a formula. Two sticks can share a silhouette and behave nothing alike on a burner.

The fifteen entries index roles, not volumes: a material central to one tradition can be marginal to another. The binder question gets its own treatment in [makko as a natural binder](/blog/makko-natural-binders/), and the wider contrast is unpacked in [natural vs synthetic incense](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/).

## How It Is Worked: The 12 Techniques

**Direct answer:** The twelve techniques split into preparation — heating, forming, blending, scenting — and appreciation, which is how a finished material is studied rather than made.

The twelve split by intent. Eight change the material: warming it above coal (隔火焚香), stamping powder into a seal track (篆香), composing by role (合香), rolling pills (香丸), filling sachets (香囊), burning openly (熏香), scenting with flowers (花熏香), and heating without flame (空熏). Four change the observer: study of one wood (品香), the formal ceremony (香道), single-material judgment (单方), and the full compound formula (复方).

Each method is easiest to grasp through what it refuses. 隔火焚香 refuses flame: coal sits under ash, a plate goes over it, and the material warms without combusting, so the smoke thins and the wood's top notes survive. The [gehuo fenxiang setup](/blog/gehuo-fenxiang-setup/) walks through the arrangement. 篆香 refuses the free heap: powder is packed into a carved channel and lit at one end, so the burn follows a fixed path and the pattern doubles as a rough clock. 空熏 refuses combustion entirely and hands the heat to an element.

The blending side is a role system, not a recipe list. 合香 assigns each material a job as chief, minister, assistant, or guide — 君臣佐使 — so the first question is what a material does, not how much of it goes in. Two formulas with identical ingredient lists can differ in effect because the roles were assigned differently. The [hexiang blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) takes the roles apart in detail.

A lineage runs through the list. Indirect-fire heating is the direct ancestor of Japanese kōdō, and the seal method is the ancestor of the timed powder pattern. Trace the arrow and the geography lines up: **buried-charcoal heating → no-flame appreciation → the formal ceremony**. The list is a family tree as much as a how-to index, and [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) follows the making side from raw material to finished bundle.

## What Ends Up on the Burner: The 12 Forms

**Direct answer:** The forms are the twelve shapes a finished product can take — the stick family with and without a bamboo spine, spirals, pyramids, backflow cones, loose meal, pellets, cakes, tablets, beads, and the prepared low-smoke and smokeless classes.

Twelve shapes complete the set. The stick family covers plain rods (线香) and bamboo-cored sticks (竹签香). The compact family covers coils (盘香), cones (塔香), backflow cones (倒流香), pills (香丸), cakes (香饼), and plaques (香牌). Loose material appears as powder (香粉). Worn material appears as beads (香珠). Two prepared classes, low-smoke (微烟香) and smokeless (无烟香), sit at the end.

Geometry is the hidden variable. A stick exposes a thin edge and draws air along its length; a coil lays the same paste flat and keeps it alight for hours; a cone burns from the tip and supports itself as it shortens; powder has no fixed geometry until it is tamped into a channel or heaped. Change the shape and surface area, airflow, and duration change with it, while the recipe stays untouched.

The stick family alone carries a real split. A coreless rod is paste throughout and burns as one body. A bamboo-core stick (竹签香) wraps paste on a splint, firmer in the hand and slower to burn, with the core itself taking part in the burn. Beads (香珠) leave combustion out: they are worn, and oil held in a porous body supplies the scent.

Cakes, plaques, low-smoke, and smokeless carry "details unverified" markers that mirror the datasets' own flags. That is a documentation gap, not a verdict — the terms are indexed because a reader will meet them, and the flag invites independent checking. For the shape decision, see [which incense format](/blog/which-incense-format/); the stick-versus-cone trade-off is in [incense stick vs cone](/blog/incense-stick-vs-cone/).

## One Stick, End to End

**Direct answer:** Trace a natural sandalwood stick from log to shelf and the three layers appear in order, which is the clearest evidence that they describe one process rather than three topics.

The walk, step by step: a sandalwood log is milled into powder (material) → the powder is blended with makko and a little water into paste (technique) → the paste is extruded into a straight rod and dried (technique again) → the rod is trimmed and bundled as a coreless stick (form). The object never leaves the taxonomy. It changes slot, and every slot is something a buyer could inspect.

That trace doubles as a checklist. "Is this a natural stick" decomposes into three checks: was the base wood real (material), was it bound with a plant binder (technique), does the object match the label (form). A product can be honest at one layer and misleading at another. Real sandalwood powder bound with a chemical adhesive passes the first check and fails the second; a fragrance-oil stick on a sawdust base fails the first no matter how carefully it was formed.

The classical case follows the same three slots: cypress seed for 柏子香 is the substance, honey-binding and shaping are the methods, and the finished pellet is the shape. Background sits on the [cypress seed entity](/ingredients/cypress-seed/) and in the [baizi recipe](/blog/baizi-incense-recipe/). One trace is enough to learn the template.

## Placing a Word in the Right Slot

**Direct answer:** When a word's layer is ambiguous, run it through three yes/no slots; most terms land in exactly one, and the slot tells you which question the word answers.

| The term you see | Material? | Technique? | Form? | Read it as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sandalwood | Yes | No | No | A substance that can become many forms |
| Powder | No | No | Yes | A shape that can hold many materials |
| 合香 blending | No | Yes | No | A method applied to a set of materials |
| 隔火焚香 | No | Yes | No | A method, not a finished object |
| Coil | No | No | Yes | A shape, independent of what it is made from |
| Makko | Yes | No | No | A material that also enables other materials to take form |

The lesson is directional. "Sandalwood powder" is not a contradiction — it is a material inside a form — but the two words answer different questions, and treating them as synonyms is the root of much label confusion. A seller who lists "powder" has told you the shape and nothing about the contents; a seller who lists "sandalwood" has told you the contents and nothing about the shape. Two words, two slots, one product.

The table also catches a common category error: reading a process word as if it were a product. 隔火焚香 is not a kind of incense you can buy in a box; it is a way of warming something you already have. Likewise 合香 is not an ingredient but an arrangement. Once the slot is clear, the shopping question changes from "which one is this" to "which layer am I still missing".

## Binder Logic, Natural and Otherwise

**Direct answer:** The binder is the fastest natural-versus-synthetic signal in the taxonomy, because it sits at the join between the material layer and the technique layer.

| Signal | Natural route | Synthetic route |
|---|---|---|
| Binder | Makko, honey, plant gums | Charcoal, sawdust, chemical adhesive |
| Scent source | The aromatic material itself | A manufactured fragrance oil |
| Burn character | Tends toward material-led, cleaner | Can be strong and uniform |
| Price logic | Tied to the raw botanical | Tied to production efficiency |
| What to check | Ingredient list, named wood | Fragrance disclosure, "natural" claims |

The mechanism is causal, not cosmetic. A plant binder burns alongside real wood and resin → the smoke carries the material's own volatiles → the scent tracks the raw botanical. A synthetic filler reverses the chain: a cheap base carries a pre-built scent → the smoke delivers whatever the oil holds → the botanical identity stops mattering. Two sticks that look alike can therefore be entirely different products, and the binder column is the first place a careful buyer looks.

One boundary is worth stating plainly. A plant binder does not certify the grade of the wood it carries. Makko on an ingredient list tells you how the stick was built, not that the sandalwood is old, well sourced, or even the species named. That distinction matters whenever a seller uses "natural binder" as a proxy for quality — the binder answers a construction question, and the wood answers a different one. *Editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## Reading a Burn Before You Light It

**Direct answer:** Each layer carries a physical consequence that the next one inherits, so the taxonomy forecasts behaviour rather than merely naming it.

- **Same material, different form** → sandalwood as powder warms gently and briefly; as a stick it burns hotter and longer, because the shape changes how much surface meets air.
- **Same form, different technique** → a hand-rolled and a machine-extruded rod of identical dimensions burn differently, because density and binder distribution differ inside the same mould.
- **Same technique, different material** → two coils made the same way can smell nothing alike, because the volatiles come from the material, not the method.

The first bullet explains a common disappointment. A reader who loves sandalwood chips on a heater may buy sandalwood sticks expecting the same profile and find something sharper and smokier; the material did not change, the form did. The second explains why craft claims matter even when the shape is identical: two rods can share a silhouette and differ in how they draw. The third explains why cheap and expensive coils can look the same and smell unrelated.

That is why a broken link in a product description is diagnostic. A missing technique often hides a synthetic base, and a form that contradicts the description often signals a material substitution upstream. Read a product as a chain and the omissions show up as gaps in the chain rather than as vague suspicion.

## The Edge of What a List Can Say

**Direct answer:** The lists say what a thing is, how it was worked, and what shape it takes; they do not say whether the material is good, the work careful, or the result pleasant to burn.

Two gaps mislead beginners. Makko as a material does not tell you that the wood it binds is sandalwood rather than a cheaper lookalike — the layer names a category, not a grade. "Hand-rolled" as a technique does not certify care; it names a method, and a method can be rushed. The taxonomy separates concepts cleanly and then deliberately stops short of judgment.

There is a third gap that matters more to collectors than to shoppers: the records are lossy by design. A termCode, a name, a Chinese reading, a definition, and a handful of typed fields cannot hold how a particular sandalwood smells on a given evening, whether a maker's coil burns evenly, or what an aged agarwood chip does on a heater. The lists are a skeleton, not a body. A reader who treats them as the whole story underuses them; a reader who treats them as a map to set out from uses them correctly.

My view: use the three layers as a grammar for sharper questions, not as a set of answers. A description that fills all three slots is legible; one that refuses all three is a warning in itself. *Editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## Chinese Labels as Layer Clues

**Direct answer:** Chinese incense vocabulary clusters by layer, and the characters in a compound usually tell you which slot you are looking at before you know the individual word.

Form words share 香: 线香 (stick), 盘香 (coil), 塔香 (cone). Material words carry the substance itself: 檀 (sandalwood), 沉 (agarwood), 乳 (frankincense), 没 (myrrh). Technique words name an action: 焚 (burn), 熏 (fumigate), 合 (blend), 品 (appreciate). A string such as 檀香线香 fills the material and form slots at once, and the missing technique is exactly the question to put to the seller.

This is a reading aid, not a translation key. Characters combine and shift between contexts, and a term you cannot parse is a term to check rather than guess at. The value of the layer markers is that they let you place an unfamiliar word in the system — is this a thing, a method, or a shape — even when the dictionary is silent. *Editorial note, not a linguistic authority.*

## The Open Data Behind the Lists

Counts live in three files: fifteen materials, twelve techniques, twelve forms. Each is typed as a Schema.org Dataset and DefinedTerm record, hosted at `data.incensehub.org/datasets/` and mirrored on the main site, so a raw entry can be pulled and checked rather than taken on trust. ## A Blender Works the Layers in Reverse

**Direct answer:** A reader moves from material toward form; a blender starts with the shape the product must take and works back through technique to the materials that can survive it.

The reversal is practical, not rhetorical. Someone planning a cone that holds for twenty minutes begins with geometry and burn time, chooses a forming method that keeps the cone from slumping as it dries, then selects materials whose volatiles survive that method's heat. The same three slots, travelled the other way.

Each form imposes its own constraint, and the constraint pushes back up the chain. A stick needs paste with enough tensile strength to hold a straight profile while it dries. A pill needs a dough that can be rolled without cracking at the seam. A seal needs powder fine enough to flow into a carved channel and consistent enough to burn at an even rate. Those requirements eliminate techniques before any material is chosen, and they eliminate materials before any scent is judged.

This is why the taxonomy is useful in a workshop and not only in a shop. A blender who cannot name the constraint is guessing at the recipe; a blender who can say "this form will not tolerate that technique" has narrowed the field before the first test batch. The lists do not design anything, but they make the order of decisions explicit.

One boundary applies here too. Working backward does not guarantee a good result. It narrows the field, and narrowing is all a framework can do before the actual burning, smelling, and revising begins. *Editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## Form and Format

**Direct answer:** "Form" is the dataset's word for the finished shape; "format" is everyday language for the same idea, and the difference is register rather than substance.

A shopper searching for "which incense format" and a reader browsing the forms table are asking about the same column. The page keeps "form" because the records use it, and because a stable vocabulary matters more than elegant variation. When a product page says "stick format", translate to "stick form" and the slot appears. There is no hidden distinction to decode, only a consistency choice.

The practical value of settling the word is small but real: it stops a reader from treating "format" as a separate category with separate rules. One fewer false distinction is one fewer place to get lost. *Editorial note.*

## What a Glossary Cannot Do

**Direct answer:** A glossary defines a word in isolation; the taxonomy places it, and placement is what lets a reader reason about an unfamiliar product.

A glossary entry for makko tells you it is a binder. The taxonomy adds what follows from that: a binder sits at the join between material and technique, which is exactly where the natural-versus-synthetic question gets decided. The definition is a fact; the placement is a tool.

A glossary also cannot show sequence. Entries sit alphabetically, side by side, with no arrow between them. The taxonomy's arrow carries the content — material worked by technique into form — and removing it leaves a word list rather than a model. That is the difference between looking up a term and understanding where it sits.

## FAQ

### Q: What is the difference between a material, a technique, and a form?
Use three verbs as a test: hold, do, see. You can hold a material (a chip of sandalwood, a spoon of makko). You can do a technique (grind it, warm it over coal, compose a blend). You can see a form (a rod, a pyramid, a spiral, a heap of meal). A product description that only supports one verb is a partial description.

### Q: How many records are in the three datasets?
Fifteen materials, twelve techniques, twelve forms. Each file is Schema.org Dataset / DefinedTerm JSON under `data.incensehub.org/datasets/`, mirrored on the main site, so the raw records can be pulled and checked rather than taken on trust. The knowledge map indexes the full eight-dataset set.

### Q: Which ingredient marks a stick as naturally bound?
Makko (抹香) — flour from the inner bark of *Machilus thunbergii*, working as both glue and combustion aid. A paste built on it needs no chemical adhesive and burns at an even rate, which makes its presence the simplest single signal of plant-based construction. The makko guide goes further into sourcing and behaviour.

### Q: What suggests a stick is factory-scented?
A base of charcoal or sawdust plus fragrance oil is the pattern: cheap bulk that burns, carrying a smell compounded elsewhere. Add a synthetic binder and nothing in the stick comes from a named botanical. The natural-versus-synthetic guide sets out the full contrast.

### Q: Why does one sandalwood smell different as powder and as a stick?
The shape sets the burn. A heap of meal warms over a broad, shallow face and releases lighter notes early; a rod burns along a thin edge at a higher temperature and drives heavier compounds into the smoke. Same wood, different geometry, different nose.

### Q: What is indirect-fire incense (隔火焚香)?
A coal is buried in ash, a mica or silver-leaf plate rests above it, and the material lies on the plate — warmed, never ignited. Smoke stays low and the finer notes survive. The method is the direct ancestor of Japanese kōdō; the setup is described in the gehuo fenxiang guide.

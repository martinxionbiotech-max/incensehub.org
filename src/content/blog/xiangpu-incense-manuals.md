> **Direct answer:** 香谱 (xiāng pǔ) is the Chinese name for a genre of incense manuals — not one book but a line of compilations that record formulas, materials, and the culture of burning. The line runs from Song works such as 陈氏香谱 and 洪芻香谱, through 新纂香谱, to the Ming encyclopedia 《香乘》 compiled by Zhou Jiazhou. The corpus is often described as the most complete ancient incense literature in existence. It is also, in English, largely unread: systematic translation has never been done.

| Point | Detail |

| Type | A genre of compilations, not a single title |

| Capstone | 香乘, Ming dynasty, compiled by Zhou Jiazhou |

| Attribution | Several compilers across Song and Ming; no single author |

## A Genre, Not a Title

**Direct answer:** The 香谱 are a shelf, not a spine — several manuals by different compilers, each building on what came before.

The character 谱 (pǔ) is the tell. It means a register or tabulated record, the kind of book that sets things in order so they can be consulted. A 香谱 therefore does not read like an essay on fragrance. It reads like a working reference: materials grouped, recipes named, notes attached. That form reveals what the compilers thought worth preserving — not impressions, but repeatable arrangements of substance.

The genre also explains a confusion that catches new readers. Asking "who wrote the 香谱?" assumes a single author. No such person exists. Each manual has its own compiler, and each compiler worked on top of earlier books. The lineage is the author.

## Four Titles That Carry the Lineage

**Direct answer:** Four names anchor most discussion of the canon, and each occupies a different place in the chain rather than a rank in a league table.

| Manual | Pinyin | Place in the line |
|---|---|---|
| 陈氏香谱 | Chén shì xiāng pǔ | A foundational Song-era collection |
| 洪芻香谱 | Hóng Chú xiāng pǔ | A Song compiler's own curated set |
| 新纂香谱 | Xīn zuǎn xiāng pǔ | A "new compilation" that reorders and adds |
| 香乘 | Xiāng chéng | The Ming capstone, broadest of the group |

《香乘》 is the title most often cited in English, partly because it is the largest and partly because its name invites translation: 乘 can be read as "vehicle," so the title becomes "Incense Vehicle" or, more usefully, "Incense Compendium." Zhou Jiazhou assembled it in the Ming period by gathering the earlier manuals into one encyclopedic reference.

Reading the table as a hierarchy is the first mistake. 香乘 is later and larger, which makes it the best catalogue of what the tradition contained. It is not the oldest witness, and it is not automatically the most faithful to any single recipe.

## How One Manual Absorbs Another

**Direct answer:** A later manual grows by swallowing earlier ones, and that growth is exactly why the same recipe appears in several forms.

The chain runs like this: a compiler records the formulas he knows → a later compiler copies those formulas into a bigger book and adds his own → in copying, a character shifts or a proportion is reordered → the new reading circulates under the same recipe name → 香乘 gathers both readings without declaring a winner → a modern reader meets variant A and variant B and no original to settle between them.

That transmission path produces a strange trade-off. Completeness and originality pull in opposite directions. The most comprehensive manual preserves the most material, yet it has also smoothed over the differences that would let you date a formula or trace it to a region. A smaller, earlier text may be less useful as an encyclopedia and more useful as evidence.

For anyone rebuilding these blends, the practical consequence is concrete: the canon is a body of variant records, not a single canonical recipe file. A responsible reconstruction flags the divergence instead of quietly choosing the tidier version. That is the editorial rule this site applies whenever a named blend appears in more than one manual.

## The Translation Problem, Stated as a Chain

**Direct answer:** The manuals are untranslated for structural reasons, not because no one noticed them.

Follow the incentives: the texts are long and written in classical Chinese → rendering them requires both language training and craft knowledge → the work is slow and specialist → a seller of incense has no commercial reason to fund it → the gap stays open → English writing on Chinese incense improvises its vocabulary instead of inheriting one.

Three consequences follow. The formulas stay closed to readers without classical Chinese. The terminology has no settled English form, so 香道, 合香, 奇楠, and 沉水 get borrowed, glossed, or mistranslated depending on the writer. The depth of the tradition — that this is a formula art with a written canon — remains largely invisible, which reduces a complex practice to "a pleasant room scent."

The consequence for readers is not abstract. A term like 奇楠 carries a grade distinction and a set of expectations; an English page that renders it as "eaglewood" without comment has dropped information the source assumed. Translation is not decoration here. It is the difference between a name and a specification.

## Complete Source, Inaccessible Shelf

**Direct answer:** "The most complete ancient incense literature" and "almost entirely untranslated" are both true, and they describe different objects.

| Signal | The manuals themselves | English availability |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | Hundreds of named formulas | Scattered excerpts and summaries |
| Completeness | The fullest surviving corpus of its kind | No systematic rendering |
| Language | Classical Chinese | Modern English, piecemeal |
| Reader access | Requires the source language | Depends on secondary work |

The trap is conflating the columns. A complete source does not mean an accessible source, and an inaccessible source does not mean an empty one. Two errors follow from the mix-up: assuming nothing worthwhile is recorded, or assuming a reader can simply pick up "the manual" in translation. Neither holds.

For a collector or researcher, the honest position is the boundary itself. The material exists, it is substantial, and it is locked behind language and edition. Any serious English engagement eventually has to touch that boundary — through a translation, a study edition, or the source text.

## The Qualifier "Known"

**Direct answer:** The canon is the most complete *known* incense literature, and the word "known" is doing real work.

Early texts were written by hand in small numbers. Each copy was exposed to fire, damp, war, and neglect. A work that was never re-copied or printed simply disappeared. What remains is what later compilers chose to preserve, gathered most fully in 香乘. The surviving canon is therefore a record of survivors, with the losses baked in.

That is why careful descriptions say "most complete known" rather than "complete." The qualifier is not hedging. It marks the difference between a corpus and the whole shelf that once existed. A reader who forgets it will over-read the evidence — treating absence from the canon as absence from the practice.

## Reading a Recipe as a Design

**Direct answer:** A 合香 formula is a structure with assigned roles, and the manuals record the structure, not just the shopping list.

The organizing principle is 君臣佐使 — chief, minister, assistant, guide. The chief material carries the blend's main character. The minister supports and reinforces it. The assistant rounds out or corrects the composition. The guide harmonizes the whole and directs the scent. Proportions decide how loudly each role speaks.

Once you see the roles, a formula stops looking like a random pile. The same materials can build a dozen different blends because the roles and their weights change. The manuals preserve hundreds of such arrangements, which is why reading one means reading a design rather than a list. The wider logic is set out in [the blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/).

## Rebuilding a Blend Today

**Direct answer:** Turning a manual formula into something burnable is a reconstruction, not a recipe follow.

The chain: a formula lists classical material names → the reader identifies what each name refers to today → proportions are read and scaled → processing steps are interpreted (grind, moisten, rest, form) → a test blend is made → the result is adjusted toward the intended character.

Every arrow is a place where the modern version can drift. A name may have shifted meaning, a measure may be lost, a material may be restricted today. The manuals are historical records, not validated instructions, and none of their formulas should be treated as guidance for consumption or therapy. Start with a simple, well-documented blend from a secondary source that has already done the modern mapping, then treat the classical text as the origin you are approximating.

## Where the Canon Sits Beside Other Chinese Books

**Direct answer:** The 香谱 are one canon among several, and placing them beside the others shows what only they contain.

| Canon type | Example | What it records |
|---|---|---|
| Incense manuals | 香乘 | Formulas, proportions, processing |
| Materia medica | 本草纲目 | Material properties and traditional uses |
| Aesthetic memoir | 梦粱录 | Culture, occasions, the 四般闲事 frame |
| Poetry and miscellany | Song verse | Mood, status, and occasion |

The materia medica names materials; the memoir describes the culture around burning; poetry supplies atmosphere. Only the manuals give the recipes — the proportions and processing that turn a material list into a blend. That is the hard-to-replace content of the 香谱, and it is why a translation gap here matters more than a gap in the essays.

## An Editorial Position: Scarcity Is the Opening

**Direct answer:** The untranslated shelf is not a dead end. It is unclaimed ground, and the scarcity is the opportunity.

My view, stated as an editorial judgment rather than a fact: the most valuable untapped resource in English incense writing is not a new scent or a new origin. It is a thousand-year-old bookshelf that almost no English speaker has read. The 香谱 are the operating system of the tradition — the place where its formulas, roles, and vocabulary are stored. Whoever renders and organizes them first will supply the terms everyone else borrows.

The misconception to correct is that "untranslated" means "minor" or "unavailable." It means neither. These are the most complete known manuals of their kind, and their obscurity in English follows from commercial incentives, not from the texts' worth. The boundary to hold is simple: this page is an orientation to the tradition, not a translation of it, and not a claim that the work is finished.

## A Reader's Route In

**Direct answer:** There are three practical ways into the manuals, and they differ in effort rather than in legitimacy.

The lightest is to read secondary summaries and the recipe pages on this site, which surface named formulas and their roles without pretending to be the source. The middle route is a parallel text: work through a classical passage with a dictionary, treating material names and proportions as the parts that matter most, since they are the most parseable. The deepest route is to learn enough terminology to read a formula directly, at which point the manual becomes source material rather than secondhand description.

None of these is fast. That is the honest state of the field. The manuals reward effort, and the effort is precisely what has not been done in English. A reader who wants the formula tradition without the language work should use the secondary route and know what it is; a reader who wants to check a claim should go to the text.

## FAQ

### Q: Is 香乘 the same thing as 香谱?

No. 香谱 is the genre — the class of incense manuals. 香乘 is one member of that genre, the Ming compendium compiled by Zhou Jiazhou, and it is the largest and most comprehensive of the group. Calling 香乘 "the 香谱" is like calling one encyclopedia "the reference shelf": it is the biggest book on the shelf, not the shelf itself.

### Q: Who wrote the 香谱?

Several compilers, across several centuries. 陈氏香谱 and 洪芻香谱 are associated with Song-era compilers; 新纂香谱 is a later compilation that reorders and adds; 香乘 is Zhou Jiazhou's Ming assemblage. The canon has no single author because it grew by absorption, and each title carries the fingerprints of the hands that transmitted it.

### Q: Are the manuals translated into English?

Almost not at all. Beyond scattered excerpts and secondary summaries, the major titles have not been systematically rendered. The formulas, the material vocabulary, and the overall architecture of the tradition remain closed to readers without classical Chinese. That absence is a fact about the current state of English resources, not a claim that this site has translated the canon.

### Q: Can I treat a manual's recipe as a set of instructions?

No. A recipe records what a compiler recorded, with centuries of copying between the event and the page. Names may map only approximately onto modern materials, measures may be lost, and some classical materials carry conservation or trade restrictions. The formulas are historical and cultural records, not validated modern instructions, and none should be used for consumption or therapeutic purposes.

### Q: How many formulas do the manuals contain?

No settled count can be given, because the answer depends on how variants are counted. The same named recipe reappears in several manuals in slightly different forms, and counting each version separately inflates the total. "Hundreds of named formulas" is the safe description, consistent with the canon's reputation; a precise figure is a scholarly question rather than a published constant.

## Summary

- 香谱 is a genre of Chinese incense manuals, not a single book.
- The lineage runs from Song compilations to the Ming 香乘 of Zhou Jiazhou.
- The canon preserves 合香 formulas as structures of roles and proportions.
- Its completeness is real; its English accessibility is close to zero.
- Transmission produced variants, so the manuals record versions rather than one authoritative text.
- The translation gap is structural, and it is the opening this kind of reference work exists to address.

## Evidence status

What is recorded here is bibliographic tradition: titles, compilers, dynastic placement, and the description of 香乘 as the broadest compilation. Those attributions come from the manuals and later Chinese bibliographic writing, and this page has not collated editions or checked a text against a surviving copy. The claim that the corpus is the most complete known incense literature is a reputation repeated in secondary writing, not a measured comparison. No translation or recipe here has been tested, and no formula is offered as instructions. The page is an orientation to a body of texts, and its evidence is only as strong as the bibliography behind it.

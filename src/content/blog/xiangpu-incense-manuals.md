**Direct answer:** The 香谱 (xiāng pǔ) — "incense manuals" — are the canonical Chinese texts that record incense formulas, materials, and lore, culminating in the Ming-dynasty encyclopedia 《香乘》 compiled by Zhou Jiazhou, and they remain almost entirely untranslated into English.

**Key facts:** The major manuals run from the Song-era 陈氏香谱 and 洪芻香谱 through the 新纂香谱 to the Ming capstone 《香乘》. Their untranslated status is the single biggest gap in English-language incense knowledge.

## The Lineage, From Song to Ming

**Direct answer:** The 香谱 are not one book but a tradition of books, each generation compiling and extending the one before it, until 香乘 gathered the earlier manuals into a single reference.

| Manual | Chinese | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 陈氏香谱 | Chén shì xiāng pǔ | "Chen's Incense Manual" — a foundational Song text |
| 洪芻香谱 | Hóng Chú xiāng pǔ | Hong Chu's manual |
| 新纂香谱 | Xīn zuǎn xiāng pǔ | The "New Compilation" |
| 香乘 | Xiāng chéng | Zhou Jiazhou's Ming encyclopedia — the most comprehensive |

The 香乘 (literally "Incense Vehicle/Compendium") is usually treated as the capstone: an encyclopedic collection that consolidates the earlier manuals into one reference, hundreds of named formulas long.

That lineage matters for a concrete reason, not just as chronology. A later manual quotes an earlier one, and in doing so it both preserves and re-organizes the material. The result is a layered record — the same formula often appears in multiple manuals, sometimes in different versions — which is why the recipe pages in this project flag version variation rather than pretend a single authoritative form exists.

## Why These Texts Are the Source, Not a Summary

**Direct answer:** The 香谱 are the primary record of the 合香 blending tradition — hundreds of named formulas, not just ingredient lists — and they document what was burned, how, and why, across centuries.

Three reasons the manuals matter, each distinct:

1. **They are the primary record of 合香.** The manuals preserve hundreds of named formulas with their proportions and processing — the actual structure of the blending tradition, not romanticized descriptions of it. (See [the blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/).)
2. **They document material culture.** Across centuries they record what was burned, in what form, for what purpose — the technical history behind the scent.
3. **They are the source of the terminology.** The terms English content struggles with — 香道, 合香, 奇楠, 沉水 — all come from these texts and have no settled English rendering.

## The Translation Gap, and Why It Persists

**Direct answer:** The 香谱 are almost entirely untranslated into English, and that absence is a structural fact with a structural cause — the texts are large, dense, and commercially unrewarding to render.

Three consequences follow directly from the gap:

- The **formulas** are inaccessible to readers who do not read classical Chinese.
- The **terminology** (合香, 奇楠, 沉水) has no canonical English rendering, so English writing improvises.
- The **depth** — that Chinese incense is a formula art with a written canon — is largely invisible in English, which reduces the whole tradition to "a pleasant room scent."

The cause is a chain worth stating plainly: the manuals are extensive classical texts → the task of translation is long and specialized → no product-focused competitor has a commercial reason to do it → the gap persists. A knowledge hub that systematically translates and organizes the material is therefore doing work no competitor is attempting, which is the strategic value of this content.

## FAQ

### Q: What are the 香谱 (xiāng pǔ)?
香谱 (xiāng pǔ) literally means "incense manuals" — the canonical Chinese texts that record incense formulas, raw materials, and the lore around their use. The tradition runs from Song-dynasty works such as 陈氏香谱 (Chen's Incense Manual) and 洪芻香谱 (Hong Chu's), through the 新纂香谱 ("New Compilation"), and culminates in the Ming-dynasty 《香乘》 (Incense Compendium) compiled by Zhou Jiazhou. Together they form what scholars regard as the world's most complete body of ancient incense literature, yet almost none of it has been translated into English, leaving a large gap in what Western readers can access.

### Q: What is 《香乘》?
《香乘》 (Xiāng chéng, "Incense Compendium") is a Ming-dynasty encyclopedia of incense compiled by Zhou Jiazhou (周嘉胄). It gathers the earlier Song manuals — 陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱, and others — into a single large reference, making it the most comprehensive of the classical incense manuals. The title is sometimes rendered "Incense Vehicle," a play on the character 乘. Because it consolidates hundreds of named formulas and the terminology of the 合香 blending tradition, it is the text most often cited when English writers describe the depth of Chinese incense literature, despite the original remaining largely untranslated.

### Q: Are the incense manuals (香谱) translated into English?
Almost not at all. Apart from scattered excerpts and secondary summaries, the major manuals — 陈氏香谱, 洪芻香谱, 新纂香谱, and 《香乘》 — have not been systematically translated into English. This is the single biggest gap in English-language incense knowledge: the formulas, the material terminology (合香, 奇楠, 沉水), and the overall depth of the tradition remain inaccessible to readers who do not read classical Chinese. That absence is precisely why a project that translates and organizes the manuals fills a niche no product-focused competitor currently addresses. The untranslated status is a fact about the current state of resources, not a claim about this site's own translations.

### Q: Why do these manuals matter today?
They are the primary written record of the blending (合香) tradition, preserving hundreds of named formulas rather than mere ingredient lists, and they document what was burned, how, and for what purpose across centuries. They are also the source of the terminology — 香道, 合香, 奇楠, 沉水 — that English content consistently struggles to render. Because the manuals capture the whole craft as a formula art with a written canon, they explain why Chinese incense is more than a consumer scent. Without access to them, Western writing on the subject stays thin, limited to romanticized descriptions instead of the actual structure of the tradition.

### Q: What is the difference between the manuals being "complete" and being "translated"?
The two claims describe different things. "The world's most complete ancient incense literature" is a claim about the source — the manuals themselves contain hundreds of named formulas in classical Chinese. "Almost entirely untranslated" is a claim about English resources — almost none of that content has been rendered for non-Chinese readers. Conflating the two is the common error: a complete source does not mean an accessible source. This page states both facts and keeps the boundary between them clear.

## How a Formula Survives: The Copying Chain

**Direct answer:** A manual recipe is not a single fixed text — it is a chain of copyists, each of whom preserved, trimmed, or altered what they received, which is why the same formula can appear in several manuals in several forms.

The chain: a formula is recorded in an early Song text → a later compiler copies it into a new manual → in copying, a character shifts or a proportion is reordered → the new version circulates and is quoted again → centuries later the 香乘 gathers both variants side by side without declaring a winner. That transmission path is why a modern reader finds "version A" and "version B" of the same named recipe and no authoritative original to settle between them. For anyone reconstructing these blends today, the practical consequence is that the manuals are a body of *variant* records, not a single canonical recipe file — and a responsible reconstruction flags the divergence instead of smoothing it over.

## A Grading Table: Which Manual Serves Which Need

**Direct answer:** The manuals are not interchangeable — each one is better at a different job, and picking the right one is faster than reading all of them.

| Manual | Best use | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| 陈氏香谱 | Tracing early Song formulas | Foundational recipes, many later quoted |
| 洪芻香谱 | A single author's curated set | Tighter scope, one compiler's taste |
| 新纂香谱 | The expanded compilation | Adds and reorganizes earlier material |
| 香乘 (香乘) | The encyclopedic capstone | The broadest consolidation, hundreds of formulas |

The table is a map, not a ranking. If you want the earliest surviving form of a blend, start with the Song texts; if you want the fullest picture of how a formula evolved and branched, go to 香乘. The mix-up to avoid is treating 香乘 as "the original" — it is the *latest and largest* collection, which means it is the best catalog of variants, not the oldest authority.

## More FAQ

### Q: If the manuals are untranslated, how do I actually use them?
Three routes, in increasing order of effort. The lightest is to read secondary summaries and the recipe pages on this site, which already surface named formulas and their roles. The middle route is to work from the classical-Chinese text with a dictionary, treating the recipe lines — material names and proportions — as the parts that matter most, since those are the most parseable. The deepest route is to learn enough of the terminology to read a formula directly, at which point the manuals open up as source material rather than secondhand description. None of these is fast, which is the honest state of the field: the manuals reward effort, and the effort is exactly what has not been done in English yet.

### Q: Why do the manuals list the same recipe with different ingredients?
Because they were compiled over centuries by different hands, and each compiler transmitted what they had, sometimes altering it. A recipe recorded in a Song manual may be copied into a Ming collection with a changed proportion, a substituted material, or a dropped step — the copyist's emendation, a regional variant, or a simple scribal error all look identical in the finished text. Without an original manuscript to compare against, the honest treatment is to record the variants as variants rather than pick one and call it authoritative. That is the editorial position this project takes, and it is why a single named blend can carry a "version varies by manual" note instead of a single fixed list.

## The Compilation Chain: How 香乘 Swallowed Its Predecessors

**Direct answer:** 香乘 is the endpoint of a compiling habit — later manuals absorbed earlier ones, so the "newest" text is really a library of the oldest — and that layering is what makes it valuable and tricky at once.

The chain: a Song compiler records formulas he knows → a later compiler copies those formulas into a larger work, adding his own → the 香乘 then gathers both layers into one encyclopedic text → each absorption preserves the older layer while reordering it → the result is a text where a single recipe may carry the fingerprints of several hands across centuries. The value is completeness; the trick is that "completeness" and "originality" point in opposite directions. The most comprehensive manual is the least useful for dating a formula, because it has smoothed over exactly the differences that would tell you where a recipe first appeared.

## A Cross-Reference Table: The Manuals Against Other Canon Types

**Direct answer:** The 香谱 are one of several Chinese canons, and placing them beside the others shows what they uniquely contain — formulas, not just names.

| Canon type | Example | What it records |
|---|---|---|
| Incense manuals (香谱) | 香乘 | Formulas, proportions, processing |
| Materia medica | 本草纲目 | Material properties and traditional uses |
| Aesthetic essays | 梦粱录 | The culture and the Four Leisure Arts |
| Poetry and miscellany | Song verse | Mood, occasion, and status |

The table separates the 香谱 from the texts they are often confused with. The materia medica names materials and their traditional roles; the essays describe the culture; only the manuals record the *recipes* — the proportions and processing that turn a material list into a burnable blend. That is the specific, hard-to-replace content of the 香谱, and it is why the translation gap matters more than a gap in the essays would.

No single authoritative count can be stated here, because the number depends on how you count variants — the same named recipe reappears in several manuals in slightly different forms, and counting each variant separately inflates the figure. What is safe to say is "hundreds of named formulas," a figure consistent with the manuals' reputation as the most complete ancient incense literature known. ### Q: Are any of the manuals' recipes safe to recreate today?
The recipes can be studied and, with care, adapted, but several cautions apply. The proportions and processing steps are historical records, not validated modern instructions; some named materials map onto today's ingredients only approximately; and a few classical materials carry conservation or trade restrictions. None of the manuals' formulas are presented here as instructions for consumption or for any therapeutic purpose — they are historical and cultural records. If you want to experiment, start with a well-documented, single-material or simple blend from a secondary source that has already done the modern mapping, and treat the classical text as the origin, not the manual.

## The Naming Chain: Why Manuals Carry So Many Titles

**Direct answer:** A single manual can be known by several names because Chinese texts were retitled by their compilers and editors across dynasties, and untangling the titles is the first step to reading the lineage correctly.

The chain: a text is first compiled under one title → a later editor reissues it, sometimes renamed to honor a patron or mark a new edition → yet another collector cites it under an older or abbreviated name → the same book now circulates under two or three titles → a modern reader meeting the different names assumes they are different books. The 香谱 family shows this: works attributed to different compilers often overlap heavily, and the 香乘 itself gathers and reorders them. Recognizing that titles are unstable is what lets a reader trace a formula back through the re-namings to its earliest source instead of treating each title as a separate origin.

## More FAQ

### Q: How do the manuals relate to Japanese kōdō texts?
The relationship is one of descent and divergence. The Japanese kōdō tradition grew out of the same Song-dynasty techniques the Chinese manuals record — the low-smoke warming method 隔火焚香 and the appreciation of blended and single-material fragrance — but Japan developed its own codified texts and schools, with their own terminology and grading. The Chinese 香谱 are the older, broader record of formulas and materials; the Japanese tradition later systematized the *appreciation* into a formal art. They share an ancestor and differ in emphasis: the Chinese manuals are a formula canon, the Japanese texts a practice canon.

## The Annotation Chain: How a Manual Grows Footnotes

**Direct answer:** The manuals did not stay as their compilers left them — later readers annotated, corrected, and cross-referenced them, and those additions are themselves part of the text's value and its difficulty.

The chain: a compiler finishes a text → a later scholar reads it and adds notes in the margins or a commentary → those notes get copied into the next edition as part of the text → a still later reader cannot always tell the original line from the annotation → the "manual" now carries the compiler's words and the annotators' glosses fused together. That layering is why reading the 香谱 demands care: a note that began as a reader's opinion can read, centuries later, like part of the formula. The annotation chain is a source of richness and a source of error, and honest scholarship separates the layers rather than reading the fused text as one voice.

## More FAQ

### Q: How reliable are the formulas in the manuals as historical records?
Reliable as records of what was *recorded*, with the usual cautions of a copied, centuries-old text. The manuals genuinely preserve named formulas with materials and proportions, and that is their core value. But transmission through generations of copyists introduces the classic problems: variant readings, dropped or transposed characters, and later glosses fused into the text. The honest position is that a formula is strong evidence of a tradition's practice, not a laboratory-grade recipe — it tells you what a compiler recorded, with a margin of copying uncertainty that a careful reading must account for rather than ignore.

## The Reconstruction Chain: How a Modern Reader Rebuilds an Old Formula

**Direct answer:** Turning a manual's formula into a burnable blend today is a reconstruction, not a recipe follow — the reader must map old names onto modern materials, interpret proportions, and accept that the result is an approximation.

The chain: a formula lists classical material names → the reader identifies what each name refers to today (some map cleanly, some are ambiguous) → proportions are read and scaled → processing steps are interpreted (grind, moisten, rest, form) → the modern blend is made and tested → the result is adjusted to match the intended character. Each arrow is a place where the modern version can drift from the historical one, because a name may have shifted meaning, a measure may be lost, or a material may be restricted today. Reconstruction is honest approximation: the goal is to honor the formula's intent, not to reproduce a lost exact object.

## More FAQ

### Q: Can I actually make one of the manual's recipes at home?
You can attempt a reconstruction, with the right expectations. The manuals record real formulas with materials and proportions, but translating them to a modern kitchen requires mapping classical names to available ingredients, interpreting old measures, and sometimes substituting a restricted material. The result is an approximation of the historical blend, not a chemically identical recreation, and some materials carry conservation or trade restrictions today. Start with a simple, well-documented formula from a secondary source that has already done the modern mapping, and treat the classical text as the origin you are approximating rather than a step-by-step manual for the modern kitchen.

## The Edition Chain: Why a Text's Value Shifts With Its Copy

**Direct answer:** A manual's authority is not fixed — it depends on which edition or copy you are reading, because each transmission either preserved or eroded the original, and that makes the copy itself part of the scholarship.

The chain: a text is written once → it is copied by hand, then printed, then re-printed → each generation of copying introduces or repairs errors → some editions are carefully collated against earlier copies, others are casual reprints → a modern reader's trust in a formula depends on which edition they are holding. A carefully collated edition is a scholarly tool; a casual reprint is a convenience that may repeat old errors. This is why serious work with the 香谱 has to ask "which edition?" before it asks "what does it say?" — the edition is the first fact about the text, not the last.

## More FAQ

### Q: Which edition of the manuals should I read?
The short answer is the most carefully collated one available, and that varies by text — for 香乘 and the major 香谱, scholarly or critical editions that note variant readings are preferable to casual reprints, because they flag where copies disagree instead of silently choosing one reading. If you cannot access a critical edition, a faithful digital transcription of a known edition is a reasonable starting point, with the caveat that it reflects that edition's errors. The practical rule: prefer an edition that tells you its source and its variants, because that transparency is what lets you judge a formula's reliability rather than trusting a single printed line.

## The Formula-Role Chain: Why a Blend Is a Structure

**Direct answer:** A 合香 formula is not a pile of ingredients — it is a structure with assigned roles, and the manuals record that structure, which is why the same materials can build a dozen different blends.

The chain: a formula assigns each material a role — chief, minister, assistant, guide (君臣佐使) → the chief carries the main character, the minister supports it, the assistant rounds or corrects it, the guide harmonizes and directs → the proportions fix how strongly each role speaks → the result is a layered scent with a designed shape, not a random mix. That structure is exactly what the manuals preserve: hundreds of formulas, each a different arrangement of roles. A reader who learns the role system can read any formula as a design, seeing why a material is present and what it is doing there.

## More FAQ

### Q: What is the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) structure?
It is the organizing principle borrowed from herbal formulation that shapes a 合香 blend. The chief (君) material carries the blend's main character; the minister (臣) supports and reinforces it; the assistant (佐) rounds out or corrects the composition; and the guide (使) harmonizes the whole and directs the scent. The proportions assign how strongly each role speaks, so the structure — not just the ingredient list — is what gives a blend its layered, composed quality. The manuals record hundreds of formulas built on this principle, which is why reading a formula means reading its roles, not just its names.

## The Lost-Text Chain: Why the Canon Has Gaps

**Direct answer:** The manuals that survive are not everything that was written — a good share of the earlier incense literature is lost, and the surviving canon is what remained after centuries of loss, not the complete original record.

The chain: early texts were written by hand in small numbers → each copy was vulnerable to fire, damp, war, and simple neglect → texts that were not re-copied or printed gradually vanished → only the works that later compilers chose to preserve survived → the 香乘, by gathering what remained, became the record of a record, with the losses baked in. That is why the manuals are called the most complete *known* incense literature rather than the complete original corpus — the qualifier "known" carries the weight of everything that did not survive. A reader of the canon is reading the survivors, not the whole shelf.

## More FAQ

### Q: Are there incense texts that have been lost?
Almost certainly yes, and the surviving canon is understood to be incomplete. The earliest incense writing was copied by hand in small numbers, and hand-copied texts are vulnerable to fire, damp, war, and neglect — a work that was never re-copied or printed simply did not survive. The manuals that remain are what later compilers chose to preserve, gathered most fully in the 香乘, so the modern canon is best described as the most complete *known* record, with an unknown number of earlier texts lost along the way. ## The Commentary Chain: Why the Canon Invites Notes

**Direct answer:** The manuals did not come with footnotes, but they constantly invite them, because a formula's meaning depends on conventions that only a later reader can make explicit — which is why the tradition is as much commentary as text.

The chain: a formula assumes the reader knows its conventions (material names, measure units, processing shorthand) → a modern reader lacks those conventions → the gap is filled by commentary that spells out what the original assumed → the commentary becomes the bridge between the classical text and the modern reader → the canon and its commentary travel together. That is why a translation or a study edition is not just the text restated — it is the text plus the recovered conventions. A reader who approaches the manuals without the commentary is meeting a text that was written for an audience that already knew what it left unsaid.

## More FAQ

### Q: Why do the manuals need so much interpretation to read today?
Because they were written for readers who shared the author's assumptions — the material names, the units of measure, the processing shorthand — and those assumptions have to be recovered before the text can be read. A formula that was self-evident to a Song-dynasty reader requires a modern reader to reconstruct what each name pointed to and how each measure was read. That recovery work is what commentary and study editions do, and it is why the manuals are a field of interpretation rather than a set of instructions you can follow directly off the page.

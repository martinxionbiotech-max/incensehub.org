**Direct answer:** The 宣德炉 (Xuande censer, *xuān délú*) is the best-known — and most frequently forged — bronze incense censer in the Chinese tradition, produced on imperial order during the Ming Xuande reign (宣德, 1426–1435) from copper brought in from abroad. Collectors prize it for a deliberately quiet silhouette and, above all, for the surface patina (皮壳) that decades of age and handling build up, rather than for any carved decoration.

**Key facts:** The trait that defines a 宣德炉 is its patina, not its ornament. Period examples survive in tiny numbers and are mostly held in museums; nearly everything sold under the name is a later copy — some Qing, some modern — and a slice of those copies are themselves old enough to interest collectors.

## Why the Form Turned Heads

**Direct answer:** The Xuande censer moved Chinese incense hardware away from sculptural, theatrical burners toward a restrained ideal built on proportion, metal quality, and surface — the yardstick every later censer was measured against.

Set the 宣德炉 beside the earlier 博山炉 (Boshan censer) and the contrast is immediate. The Boshan piece is a mountain-shaped sculpture crowded with peaks and mythical beasts; the Xuande censer is a calm, rounded body, usually fitted with small loop or animal handles (耳, such as 蚰龙耳), and seated on short feet. There is no carving to distract. The restraint is deliberate — the design lets the metal and the aging surface do the talking, an approach later collectors adopted as the benchmark for good censer work.

## What "Patina" (皮壳) Actually Means

**Direct answer:** The 皮壳 is the bronze's aged outer skin — a layered record of oxidation, handling wear, and accumulated surface chemistry — and it is the single most informative thing a collector reads on any censer.

皮壳 translates loosely as "skin" or "shell," and the image is exact. Fresh bronze is bright and metallic; given years of air, handling, and occasionally incense smoke, the surface darkens and drifts through a range of named colors. Collectors read that skin the way a reader reads a page: it records where the piece has lived and how it has been kept. Two censers cast from the same design become distinct objects the moment their patinas take different paths.

## How a Patina Builds Up

**Direct answer:** A patina is grown, not painted on — bare bronze meets air and moisture → a thin, patchy oxidation layer appears → handling and smoke add further change → the color deepens unevenly across the piece → that layered unevenness becomes the signature of real age.

The mechanism is what forgers attack, so it repays close study. On a genuinely old piece, the tone varies: recesses read darker than raised surfaces, the inside of the bowl differs from the outer wall, the feet differ from the body. That is what decades of irregular exposure produce. A surface that reads the same flat color everywhere has almost certainly been treated in a single pass — dipped, painted, or waxed — to fake an age it never earned.

## The Named Colors: A Reader's Field Guide

**Direct answer:** Collectors give the patina shades traditional names — each name describes how a surface has aged, and none of them is a rank in any grading scale.

| Name | Character | Hue it points to |
|---|---|---|
| 棠梨色 | crabapple-pear | a warm reddish-brown |
| 猪肝色 | pig's-liver | a deep liver-brown |
| 蟹壳青 | crab-shell green | a greenish-bronze tone |

These are descriptive labels, not a ranking: no single color is worth more than another on its own. The names do, though, feed the forgery trade, since a forger aims squarely at producing a textbook 棠梨色 or 蟹壳青. A 宣德炉 wearing a flawless, uniform version of any named color is a caution sign, not a guarantee — the grading table below explains why.

## Alloy and Casting: What the Metal Tells You

**Direct answer:** The material side of a 宣德炉 is imported copper, and the way the metal was melted, poured, and finished leaves a fingerprint in the casting quality that a forger can rarely hide.

The imperial commission began with copper brought in from outside China, and the metal itself is part of the object's story. A well-poured piece shows crisp, confident edges where body, handles, and feet meet, and an even wall thickness that rings cleanly when tapped. A hurried copy — especially a modern sand-cast one — betrays itself in blurred junctions, an off-balance stance, and a dull or muddy ring. Metal and patina work together: a sound alloy ages into a rich skin, while a cheap alloy turns a flat, dead color no matter how long it sits.

## Why "Xuande" Now Names a Style, Not a Date

**Direct answer:** For anyone buying today, "Xuande" operates as a style label and a reputation, because period censers are museum rarities and the market is almost entirely later copies.

The shift in meaning is the real lesson of this object. "Xuande" started as a reign date and an imperial commission. Across five centuries it hardened into a brand — a recognizable shape plus a prized patina, detached from any realistic chance that the piece in your hands was poured in 1426–1435. The working rule follows: assume it is a copy unless expert provenance says otherwise, judge it as a copy, and let the price reflect "later bronze" rather than "Xuande original."

## Copy vs Original: A Grading Table

**Direct answer:** Nearly every "宣德炉" falls into a handful of grades set by age, casting quality, and patina honesty — and the question that matters is which grade you are holding, not whether the label says Xuande.

| Grade | What it is | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| Xuande-period original | poured 1426–1435 on imperial order | museum-grade; near-unobtainable without provenance |
| Old Qing or Ming copy | a later bronze, itself antique | collectible for its own age and honest patina |
| Well-made modern bronze | honest recent casting, natural patina forming | a sound censer; pay for the bronze, not the name |
| Mass-produced fake | artificial uniform patina, rushed casting | red flag; price it as decorative metal |

The table makes one point: value is not a two-way switch between "real" and "fake." A genuine middle band of old, well-made copies exists, and those are legitimate censers in their own right. The error people make is handing over the top grade's money for the bottom grade's object because the label read 宣德炉.

To get a uniform fake patina, a forger works in one pass: chemical bath or wax applied → the coating sits on the surface without biting in → raised and recessed areas read the same flat tone → no layered depth develops → the sameness gives the fake away rather than hiding it.

## Reading Wear, Not Just Color

**Direct answer:** The distribution of wear is as revealing as the patina's hue — genuine handling rubs the places a hand actually touches, and that selective polish is something a chemical bath cannot reproduce.

A forger can fake a color with a single dip, but faking the pattern of human contact is a different problem. Real use rubs the rim where fingers lift the lid, the handles where the piece is carried, and the feet where it rests on a surface. Those contact points read slightly brighter than the surrounding metal, while the sheltered recesses stay darker. A piece whose high points and low points read identical — no brighter rims, no darker pockets — has been surfaced, not used. This is the same logic of reading a censer's history that runs through [the authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/), and it is why a uniform patina is a warning even when the color itself looks textbook.

## Display and Use: Treating It as a Censer, Not a Trophy

**Direct answer:** A 宣德炉 is a working censer, and it earns its patina through use — incense ash and gentle handling deepen the skin, while sealing it away behind glass freezes the very surface collectors value.

The paradox of this object is that its most prized feature, the patina, is built by ordinary use. Burning incense in it — with a proper ash bed and ventilation — and handling it naturally over years adds exactly the kind of uneven surface change that collectors read as honest age. A piece locked in a display case and polished to a shine is, in a real sense, being prevented from becoming what it is supposed to be. Treat a genuine old censer with care, but treat it as a censer. This is a judgment call about stewardship, not a proven provenance fact.

## The Author's View

**Direct answer:** The Xuande censer teaches the same discipline as 奇楠 agarwood — once an object becomes a legendary collectible, the market fills with copies, and the honest move is to judge what is in front of you rather than the name.

Two editorial judgments follow. First, a well-made later bronze with an honest, uneven patina is a perfectly good censer; the mistake is paying Xuande-original prices for one, not the purchase itself. Second, the "perfect patina" is overrated as a signal — uniformity is the one thing a forger can reliably manufacture, and the one thing real age does not produce. For a collector, the practical skill is reading unevenness, depth, and wear, not memorizing color names. *This is the author's editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## Summary

- The 宣德炉 is a deliberately plain bronze censer poured on imperial order in the Ming Xuande reign (1426–1435) from imported copper.
- Its defining feature is the patina (皮壳), a grown surface skin whose named shades — 棠梨色, 猪肝色, 蟹壳青 — describe aging, not rank.
- Patina builds through slow, uneven oxidation and handling; a flat, uniform tone signals a forgery.
- "Xuande" now names a style and a reputation, since period pieces are museum rarities and the market is nearly all later copies.
- Buyers should grade what is in front of them — original, old copy, honest modern bronze, or fake — and pay accordingly.

## FAQ

### Q: What exactly is a Xuande censer?
**Direct answer:** A bronze incense burner poured in the Ming dynasty on an imperial commission that ran from 1426 to 1435, using imported copper, and now prized more for its aged surface than for its shape.

### Q: How do I tell an old one from a modern copy?
**Direct answer:** Start by assuming period pieces are museum rarities, then read the surface rather than the label — real age leaves uneven depth and wear, while a single flat color across every face is what a forger produces.

### Q: What do the patina color names mean?
**Direct answer:** 棠梨色 (crabapple-pear), 猪肝色 (pig's-liver), and 蟹壳青 (crab-shell green) describe how the bronze has weathered — a vocabulary of aging, not a ranking, and no single shade is worth more than another.

### Q: Should I buy a "宣德炉"?
**Direct answer:** Only when you price it as the later copy it almost certainly is — a well-cast bronze with an honest, uneven patina at a fair price, not a Xuande original at original money.

### Q: Is a later copy ever worth collecting?
**Direct answer:** Yes — Qing and Ming copies are collectible in their own right for their age, casting, and honest patina; the mistake is paying original prices for one, not buying one at all.

### Q: Does using the censer for incense help or hurt its value?
**Direct answer:** Gentle use deepens the patina that collectors prize, so normal burning — with an ash bed and ventilation — supports the surface's natural aging; careless handling or polishing is what damages it.

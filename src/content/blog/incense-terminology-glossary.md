**Direct answer:** Incense is sold in a handful of physical formats — stick (线香), coil (盘香), cone (塔香), resin (香脂 or 香片), and loose powder (香粉) — plus prepared variants such as coreless sticks and backflow cones. The format decides how the piece burns, how long it lasts, which holder it needs, and how much smoke it makes.

**Key facts:** This page covers **format and practice vocabulary**. Ingredient words such as sandalwood, frankincense, and makko belong to the [ingredients glossary](/blog/incense-ingredients-glossary/); the larger Chinese–English word list lives in the [terminology reference](/blog/incense-terminology-reference/).

## Five shapes, one sorting principle

> **Direct answer:** The formats split by what supplies the heat and what carries the structure: stick, coil, and cone are self-burning pieces, while resin and powder are loose material that needs an external heater or a bed of charcoal.

| Format | Chinese | Physical description | What it needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stick | 线香 | A slim rod, either cored with bamboo or pressed solid | A tray or a simple holder |
| Coil | 盘香 | A flat spiral with a long, folded burn path | A stand or a hanging clip |
| Cone | 塔香 | A freestanding pyramid burned from the tip down | A small dish or a cone burner |
| Resin | 香脂 / 香片 | Dried gum, wood chips, or resin lumps | Charcoal or an electric heater |
| Powder | 香粉 | Loose ground material | Charcoal, a heater, or a seal template |
| Backflow cone | 倒流香 | A hollow cone with a central smoke channel | A burner with a matching hole |

The functional split is more useful than a list of names. A stick, a coil, and a cone carry their own fuel and finish without help. Resin and powder do not; they need a heat source, and that source changes the experience. A beginner who buys loose powder expecting to light it like a stick will be disappointed. A beginner who buys a coil without a stand will improvise badly.

The second sorting principle is geometry. A stick is a line; a coil is a line folded into a spiral; a cone is a line that narrows as it burns; a seal is a line pressed into a pattern. The line is the burn path, and the path determines the session length. Once you see the format as a path, the vocabulary stops being arbitrary.

## What each shape does to the burn

> **Direct answer:** Shape controls the burn in three ways — the cross-section sets how much material is exposed, the path sets how long the ember travels, and the airflow around the piece sets how fast it draws.

The chain is easy to hold in mind:

**cross-section → surface area exposed to air → ember temperature and speed → burn time and smoke volume.**

A thin stick has a small cross-section, so it burns hot and fast per unit of length but delivers a modest total amount of material. A thick coil has a larger cross-section, so it burns cooler and slower, and the long path adds hours. A cone tapers, so the wide base burns more slowly than the narrow tip, which is why a cone can appear to slow down near the end. A seal is a thin line of powder, so it burns along the pattern at a fairly steady rate, which is what makes it usable as a timer.

The material matters as much as the shape. A stick made with a high proportion of aromatic wood and a low proportion of binder may struggle to stay lit; a stick with more binder burns steadily but smells of the binder. A coil needs enough structural strength to hold its spiral without sagging. A cone needs a balance between density and air passage, or it will tunnel or go out. These are formulation problems, not vocabulary problems, but they explain why two sticks of the same length can behave differently.

Smoke volume follows the same logic. A smoldering piece with a large exposed surface produces more smoke than a small, tight piece. A cone produces a concentrated plume at the tip; a coil produces a long, thin trail. A stick produces a steady column that rises with the room's air current. If smoke volume is the concern, the format is the first variable to change, before the ingredient.

> **Direct answer:** The shape is a burn engine: cross-section, path length, and airflow set the pace, and the ingredient only decides what the smoke smells like.

## The five practice words

> **Direct answer:** Five terms recur in manuals and product pages — 香道, 合香, 隔火焚香, 篆香, and 四般闲事 — and each names a method or a cultural frame rather than a physical object.

| Term | Pinyin | What it names |
|---|---|---|
| 香道 | xiāngdào | "The Way of Incense" — the practice of preparing and appreciating incense as a discipline |
| 合香 | hé xiāng | Compound incense, built on a formula rather than a single material |
| 隔火焚香 | gé huǒ fén xiāng | Indirect-fire warming: material placed over charcoal buried in ash, or over a heater |
| 篆香 | zhuàn xiāng | Seal incense: powder pressed into a pattern and burned along its path |
| 四般闲事 | sì bān xián shì | The "four leisure arts" — incense, tea, hanging scrolls, and flower arrangement |

The two terms worth learning first are 隔火焚香 and 篆香. The first explains the ash bed, the mica plate, and the whole low-smoke approach to warming wood. The second explains the metal or ceramic template, the little channel of powder, and the idea of a scent that is also a clock. The other three are cultural context: 香道 names the practice, 合香 names the compositional method, and 四般闲事 places incense among the literati arts.

The phrase 四般闲事 comes from 吴自牧's 梦粱录, a Song-dynasty record of urban life. The list — burning incense, brewing tea, hanging paintings, arranging flowers — is a compact statement of what a cultivated household was expected to enjoy. It is also a reminder that incense was not always a solitary sensory hobby. It was part of a social and aesthetic setting, alongside tea and visual art.

## The swaps that cause confusion

> **Direct answer:** Most beginner confusion comes from four near-pairs: stick versus coreless stick, coil versus spiral stick, cone versus backflow cone, and resin versus powder.

| Pair | What separates them | Why the confusion matters |
|---|---|---|
| Stick / coreless stick | A bamboo core runs through the center of one; the other is pressed entirely from fragrant material | Coreless sticks are usually thinner, burn shorter, and produce less smoke, with a purer scent |
| Coil / spiral stick | A coil is a flat spiral meant to burn for hours; a spiral stick is a decorative shape with a shorter burn | Buying the wrong one changes the session length, not just the look |
| Cone / backflow cone | A standard cone burns upward; a backflow cone has a hollow channel that sends smoke downward | The backflow effect is visual; the scent and burn time are otherwise similar |
| Resin / powder | Resin is a solid gum or chip; powder is ground material | Resin can be warmed as a piece; powder needs a heater, charcoal, or a template |

The stick/coreless distinction is the one with the biggest practical consequence. A bamboo core adds a woody, neutral note to the smoke and keeps the stick straight; a coreless stick is more fragile but gives a cleaner perfume. Neither is better in the abstract. A coreless stick suits a short, precise session; a cored stick suits a longer, sturdier burn.

The coil/spiral distinction is mostly a retail problem. A coil is engineered for duration; a spiral-shaped stick is engineered for appearance. If a product page shows a spiral and does not state the burn time, the buyer cannot tell which one is being sold. The same applies to cones: a backflow cone is a cone with a hole, and the hole changes the airflow, not the ingredient.

Resin and powder are often sold together because they come from the same materials. A resin is a solid that softens or melts under heat; a powder is a collection of small particles. The powder may be pressed into a cone or a seal, in which case it becomes a shaped product, but the underlying material is still powder. The format name describes the shape, not the source.

## Matching format to session

> **Direct answer:** Choose by session length and attention first, then by scent; a format that does not fit the occasion will be used badly no matter how good the material is.

| Session | Best fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A short meditation or reading block | Stick or coreless stick | A predictable 20–60 minute burn, easy to extinguish or let finish |
| A long evening or a room scent | Coil | Hours of continuous burn with little attention |
| A brief offering or a single note | Cone | Burns down completely in a compact session |
| Careful appraisal of a material | Resin chip on a heater | Gentle heat reveals the top, body, and dry-down |
| A timed, decorative burn | Seal powder | The pattern itself measures the session |
| A visual centerpiece | Backflow cone | The downward smoke is the point; scent is secondary |

The table is a starting point, not a rule. A stick can be broken to shorten a session; a coil can be extinguished and relit; a cone can be placed in a smaller room. The real question is how much attention the session allows. A stick asks for almost none; a resin chip on a heater asks for attention because the scent changes and the heat needs managing. The format should match the attention you have, not the image you want to project.

Cost per session is a separate axis. A long coil may look expensive, but if it burns for six hours, the cost per hour can be lower than a premium stick that lasts twenty minutes. A small chip of resin can be warmed twice if it is dense enough. Comparing formats by unit price rather than by session cost is one of the most common buying mistakes.

## From dough to stick: the making chain

> **Direct answer:** A stick is a composite of aromatic powder, binder, and water, extruded or rolled into shape and dried; the binder is what lets the shape hold together and burn steadily.

The manufacturing chain runs like this:

**aromatic powder + binder + water → kneaded dough → extrusion or hand-rolling → cutting to length → controlled drying → bundling and storage.**

The powder provides the scent. The binder provides cohesion and, in many recipes, part of the burn. Water makes the mixture workable. The proportions are formula-specific: too much binder and the stick smells of the binder; too little and the stick crumbles or goes out. The drying step is where many batches fail, because a stick that dries too fast cracks, and a stick that dries too slowly can mold.

A bamboo-core stick adds a fifth element: the core itself. The dough is applied around a thin bamboo splint, which gives the stick strength and a straight axis. A coreless stick is pressed from the dough alone, which is why it is usually thinner and more fragile. The choice is a manufacturing choice with sensory consequences, not a quality hierarchy.

The same dough can become a cone or a coil. A cone is pressed in a mold; a coil is extruded and wound into a spiral; a stick is extruded straight. The shared step is the dough, and the shared variable is consistency. A factory that can hold the moisture and particle size steady can produce all three formats from one blend. A factory that cannot will produce sticks that crack, cones that tunnel, and coils that sag.

## Why a coil can run for hours

> **Direct answer:** A coil is a long burn path folded into a compact shape; the ember travels the spiral at a steady rate, so the duration comes from path length rather than from a special ingredient.

The geometry is the whole trick. A straight stick of the same material might burn for half an hour; the same length of material wound into a spiral can burn for hours because the ember has farther to travel. The spiral also self-supports, so the burning section stays above the unburned section and the ash falls away. A stand or a hanging clip keeps the coil flat and prevents the burning edge from touching the surface.

Airflow matters. A coil in a still room burns slowly; a coil near a draft burns faster and may become uneven. The outer edge of the spiral is more exposed than the inner edge, so a coil can develop a faster side. A well-made coil has a consistent cross-section and a binder that holds the shape without clogging the burn. A poorly made coil may go out at a bend or burn through a support.

The long burn has a practical consequence: the scent accumulates. A stick delivers a short, defined note; a coil fills a room over hours, and the fragrance settles into fabrics and walls. That is desirable in some settings and oppressive in others. A coil in a small, closed room can become too much; a coil on a shaded terrace can be exactly right. The format is a tool for a space as much as for a time.

## Why a seal burns like a clock

> **Direct answer:** A seal is a continuous line of powder pressed into a pattern; because the line has a known length and a steady burn rate, the pattern can measure a session.

The practice, 篆香, uses a template — often a metal or ceramic plate with a cut-out design — placed on a bed of ash. Powder is sifted into the cut-out, the template is lifted, and the line of powder remains. The line is lit at one end, and the ember travels along the pattern. The design can be a character, a geometric form, or a stylized symbol; the point is that the line is continuous and the burn is predictable.

The clock analogy is not perfect, because humidity, powder density, and airflow all change the rate. A seal in a damp room may burn slowly or go out; a seal in a draft may race. The template controls the path, not the speed. An experienced practitioner adjusts the powder and the environment, and uses the seal as a rough measure rather than a precision timer.

The seal is also a visual practice. The pattern is part of the experience, and the slow consumption of the line is part of the attention. A stick can be ignored; a seal invites watching. That is why the format appears in tea rooms and meditation settings where the burn is meant to be seen as well as smelled.

## What the smoke does

> **Direct answer:** Smoke rises because it is warmer and less dense than the surrounding air; a backflow cone reverses the visible direction by cooling the smoke and channeling it through a hollow center.

The physics is straightforward:

**combustion → hot smoke rises → the cone's hollow channel and the burner's hole create a low-pressure path → cooled smoke becomes denser than the room air → the plume falls and pools at the base.**

In a standard cone, the smoke simply rises. In a backflow cone, the hollow center and the hole in the burner give the smoke a downward path, and the cooling smoke loses buoyancy. The result looks like a waterfall of smoke. The effect depends on the burner, the cone's internal channel, and still air; a draft will break it.

The visual effect is the reason the format exists. The scent is the same as a standard cone of the same material, and the burn time is similar. A buyer who wants the waterfall should choose a backflow cone and a compatible burner; a buyer who wants a long, even scent should choose a standard cone or a coil. The format is a performance, and the performance is worth paying for only if you want it.

Smoke direction also matters for placement. A rising plume carries scent upward, so a low table may scent the ceiling rather than the seating area. A downward plume pools near the burner, so the scent is concentrated at the base. Neither is better; both are placement problems that the format solves or creates.

## Reading the characters

> **Direct answer:** The Chinese names are descriptive: 线 means thread, 盘 means coil or plate, 塔 means pagoda, 香 means incense, and the compounds tell you the shape before you see it.

The character 香 (xiāng) is the constant. It appears in every format name and in the general word for incense. 线 (xiàn) means thread or line, so 线香 is the thread incense — the stick. 盘 (pán) means a plate, a tray, or a coil, so 盘香 is the coiled incense. 塔 (tǎ) means a pagoda or tower, so 塔香 is the cone that stands like a small tower. 片 (piàn) means a slice or flake, which is why 香片 can name a flat piece of aromatic material.

The practice words are equally literal. 隔 (gé) means to separate or interpose, 火 (huǒ) means fire, and 焚 (fén) means to burn, so 隔火焚香 is "burning incense with fire separated" — the indirect method. 篆 (zhuàn) names a seal script or a carved pattern, so 篆香 is the patterned powder. 合 (hé) means to join or combine, so 合香 is joined incense — a blend.

This is one of the small pleasures of the vocabulary: the names are not arbitrary. A learner who reads the characters can often guess the format, and a buyer who knows that 盘 involves a coil will not confuse a coil with a spiral stick. The characters are a compressed manual.

## Three tiers of vocabulary

> **Direct answer:** The words sort into three tiers — physical formats, practice methods, and quality or ingredient terms — and mixing the tiers is what makes product pages feel impenetrable.

The first tier is the shape: stick, coil, cone, resin, powder. These are the words you need to buy the right object. The second tier is the method: 香道, 合香, 隔火焚香, 篆香. These are the words you need to understand how the object is used. The third tier is the material and grade vocabulary: species names, grade names, origin names, and the adjectives used to describe scent. These are the words you need to judge what the object is worth.

A product page often mixes all three tiers in one sentence: "premium Hainan agarwood coreless stick, hand-rolled in the traditional 隔火 style." The sentence contains a grade claim, a species claim, a format, a production method, and a practice reference. A buyer who can separate the tiers can ask five distinct questions. A buyer who cannot may treat the whole sentence as a single quality signal, which is exactly what the seller wants.

The tier model also explains why glossaries proliferate. A format glossary, an ingredient glossary, and a practice glossary each cover a different tier. Combining them into one list produces a dictionary that is long but not necessarily useful, because the reader cannot tell which words are needed for which decision. The better structure is a short format list, a short practice list, and a pointer to the ingredient reference.

## The small kit behind the formats

> **Direct answer:** Most formats need one accessory beyond the incense itself — a holder, a stand, a heat-safe dish, a heater, or a seal template — and the accessory is part of the format's cost.

A stick wants a tray with a small hole or a notch, so the rod can lean without falling. A coil wants a metal stand or a clip that keeps the spiral flat and lets ash drop clear. A cone wants a dish that will not tip and will not scorch the table. Resin wants a heater or a bed of charcoal under a mica plate. Powder wants a template and a leveled ash bed if it is to be pressed into a seal. None of these tools is expensive in itself, but each belongs to a different way of burning, and buying the incense without the tool is buying half a format.

## Common mistakes

> **Direct answer:** The recurring errors are buying powder without a heater, buying a coil without a stand, assuming a coreless stick is always better, and treating a backflow cone as a scent upgrade.

The first mistake is a mismatch between format and equipment. Powder and resin need a heat source; a stick and a cone do not. A buyer who orders powder because it looks artisanal, then discovers that it cannot be lit like a stick, has bought a format problem rather than a scent problem.

The second is a missing accessory. A coil needs a stand or a clip; without one, it will not burn evenly. A cone needs a heat-safe dish. A seal needs a template and an ash bed. These are small costs, but they are part of the format, and a product page that does not mention them is incomplete.

The third is the assumption that coreless is superior. A coreless stick is purer in scent and produces less smoke, but it is also more fragile and shorter-burning. A bamboo-core stick is sturdier and can be longer. The right choice depends on the session, not on a hierarchy.

The fourth is the backflow fallacy. The downward smoke is a visual effect, not a sign of better material or a stronger scent. A backflow cone made from ordinary powder is still ordinary powder. The format is a performance; the ingredient is the substance.

> **Direct answer:** Most format mistakes are equipment mistakes in disguise; the shape tells you what else you need to buy.

## FAQ

### Q: Which formats count as "incense" in the Chinese trade?

Five: the straight stick, the coiled spiral, the standing cone, solid resin or chips, and loose powder. Two prepared styles — coreless sticks and backflow cones — are variations on the stick and the cone rather than separate categories. The format tells you how the piece is lit, how long it runs, and what kind of holder or heater belongs with it.

### Q: What does 隔火焚香 mean in practice?

The phrase translates as "incense across fire." A piece of charcoal is covered with ash, a thin mica or silver plate is set on the ash, and the aromatic material rests on the plate. Heat reaches the material without flame, so the scent rises slowly and the smoke stays low. The technique is old, associated with Song-dynasty practice, and it is the direct ancestor of the Japanese kōdō ceremony.

### Q: How long does a stick last next to a coil?

A stick usually runs somewhere between twenty minutes and an hour; thickness, density, and room airflow move it within that range. A coil is built for a much longer session, often several hours, because the ember follows a spiral rather than a straight line. The maker's specification is the number to trust; the format only sets the order of magnitude.

### Q: Is coreless always the better stick?

No. A coreless stick is pressed from fragrant material alone, so it usually smells cleaner and throws less smoke, but it is thinner, easier to break, and shorter-burning. A bamboo-cored stick is stronger and can be made longer, though the core adds a neutral woody note and extra smoke. The right choice is the one that fits the session, not the one with the better reputation.

### Q: What makes a backflow cone different?

A backflow cone has a hollow center, and it sits on a burner with a hole. Smoke travels down the channel, cools, becomes denser than the room air, and spills out at the base instead of rising. The effect is visual; the material and burn time are close to a standard cone. It needs a compatible burner, and a draft will spoil the waterfall.

### Q: Why do the same words carry different meanings?

Because practice changes faster than vocabulary. A term coined for one object gets reused for the nearest new thing, and both senses survive. 香道 can name the broad appreciation of incense or a formalized ritual; 盘 can mean a plate, a tray, or a coil. A useful glossary records the senses and the contexts instead of pretending one meaning has always held.

## What the vocabulary cannot tell you

> **Direct answer:** Format words describe shape and burn, not quality; a "coreless stick" can be made from poor material, and a "coil" can be badly formed.

The names on this page are a map of objects and methods, not a guarantee of craft. A coreless stick tells you there is no bamboo core; it does not tell you whether the powder is fresh, whether the binder is balanced, or whether the stick was dried slowly enough to avoid cracks. A coil tells you the burn path is long; it does not tell you whether the cross-section is even. Those questions belong to the material and the maker, and they need a different set of checks: a burn test, a look at the ash, and a supplier who can name the powder and the binder. A format word is a starting point for those questions, never a substitute for them. The shape tells you what kind of object you are holding; the material and the craft tell you whether it is worth burning.

## Format words in a shop

> **Direct answer:** In a shop, the format words do the work of a specification sheet: they tell you what will arrive, how it will burn, and what else you must buy.

A listing that says "coil" and nothing else leaves out the stand, the burn time, and the diameter. A listing that says "coreless" leaves out the length and the fragility. A listing that says "backflow cone" leaves out the burner. The buyer who knows the vocabulary can fill those gaps with questions; the buyer who does not may treat the noun as a complete description. The habit worth building is simple: for every format word, ask what it implies about the object's size, support, heat source, and session length.

## Summary

> **Direct answer:** Five formats, five practice terms, and a short list of near-pairs cover most of what a buyer needs.

The formats divide by heat source and geometry: stick, coil, and cone burn on their own, while resin and powder need a heater, charcoal, or a template. The shape sets the burn path, and the path sets the session. The practice words add the cultural layer — 香道 for the discipline, 合香 for blending, 隔火焚香 for indirect warming, 篆香 for the powder seal, and 四般闲事 for the literati setting. The characters are descriptive, so the names double as a small manual. Separate the tiers — format, method, material — and product pages become readable.

**Direct answer:** Walk into a shop and the inventory resolves into five physical formats — 线香 sticks, 盘香 coils, 塔香 cones, 香脂 or 香片 resin, and loose 香粉 powder — alongside prepared variants such as coreless sticks and 倒流香 backflow cones. Shape decides how the piece burns, how long it lasts, what holds it, and how much smoke it releases.

**Key facts:** What follows is **format and practice vocabulary**. Material words — sandalwood, frankincense, makko — are covered in the [ingredients glossary](/blog/incense-ingredients-glossary/), and the long Chinese–English list in the [terminology reference](/blog/incense-terminology-reference/). One rule applies throughout: a format word describes shape and heat, never quality.

## Three Tiers, Not One List

**Direct answer:** Format words, method words and material words answer three different questions, and most confusion comes from reading them as one.

Tier one is the object: stick, coil, cone, resin, powder. These are the words you need to order the right thing. Tier two is the method: 香道, 合香, 隔火焚香, 篆香. These describe how the object is used, and they change the burn as much as the shape does. Tier three is grade and material: species, origin, grade names, scent adjectives. These decide what the object is worth.

A single product line often stacks all three: "single-origin agarwood, coreless, 香道 grade" carries a material claim, a format, and a practice reference in nine words. A reader who can separate the tiers asks three questions. A reader who cannot treats the sentence as one blur of prestige — which is what the copy is written to encourage.

The tiers also explain why glossaries multiply. A format list, a method list and an ingredient list each serve a different decision. Merged into one alphabetical block, they produce length without usability, because nothing tells the reader which word belongs to which choice.

## Five Formats, Sorted by What Feeds the Ember

**Direct answer:** The useful split is not shape but heat: stick, coil and cone carry their own fuel, while resin and powder need an external source.

| Format | Chinese | Fed by | Rests on | Session |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stick | 线香 | its own body | shallow tray or notch | a single sitting |
| Coil | 盘香 | its own body | flat stand or hanging clip | most of an evening |
| Cone | 塔香 | its own body | non-flammable saucer | brief, burns to nothing |
| Resin | 香脂 / 香片 | outside heat | charcoal or electric warmer | repeatable, uneven |
| Powder | 香粉 | outside heat | coal, warmer or template | free heap or drawn line |
| Backflow cone | 倒流香 | its own body | burner drilled to match | smoke falls, not rises |

The first three are complete objects: light one end and they finish without help. The last two are raw material, and the heat source you choose changes the experience more than the material does. A beginner who buys powder expecting to strike a match to it has bought a format problem, not a scent problem.

The second sorting principle is geometry, and it is the one that predicts session length. A stick is a line; a coil is that line folded into a spiral; a cone is a line whose cross-section narrows as it goes; a seal is a line pressed into a pattern. Every format is a burn path, and the path is the clock. Once the shapes are read as paths, the vocabulary stops looking arbitrary.

## Geometry Is the Clock

**Direct answer:** Cross-section, path length and airflow set the pace; the ingredient only decides what the smoke smells like.

The chain is short enough to memorise: **cross-section → surface area exposed to air → ember temperature and speed → burn time and smoke volume.** A thin stick exposes little material at once, so it burns hot per unit of length but delivers a modest total. A thick coil exposes more, burns cooler per unit, and adds hours through path length alone. A cone tapers, so the wide base advances more slowly than the narrow tip, which is why a cone seems to slow near the end rather than race.

Formulation sits underneath geometry. A stick with a high proportion of aromatic wood and little binder may struggle to stay lit; one with generous binder burns steadily and smells of the binder instead of the material. A coil needs enough green strength to hold its spiral without sagging. A cone needs a density that lets air through without tunnelling. None of these are vocabulary problems, but they explain why two sticks of equal length can behave nothing alike.

Smoke follows the same logic. A smouldering piece with a large exposed face throws more visible smoke than a small, tight one. A cone makes a concentrated plume at its tip, a coil a long thin trail, a stick a steady column that follows the room's air. If smoke volume is the concern, change the format before you change the ingredient — that is the variable with the largest effect.

## The Two Practice Words That Change the Burn

**Direct answer:** 隔火焚香 and 篆香 are the two method terms worth learning first, because each describes a different way of controlling heat rather than a different object.

隔火焚香, literally "fire kept apart from incense", is warming rather than burning. A bed of ash is levelled over live charcoal; a mica or silver plate goes on the ash; the aromatic piece rests on the plate. No flame ever touches the material → resin and oil evaporate gradually → almost no particulate smoke, and the scent unfolds in stages instead of arriving in one wave. The method belongs to Song-era practice and sits directly behind the Japanese kōdō ceremony, which codified the same act of listening. See [Japanese kōdō](/blog/japanese-incense-kodo/).

篆香 is the powder seal. A metal or ceramic template with a cut-out design is laid on levelled ash, powder is sifted into the cut-out, and the template is lifted to leave a continuous line. Light one end and the ember walks the pattern. Because the line has a known length and a roughly steady rate, the pattern works as a rough clock — but only roughly: damp powder slows or stalls, a draught accelerates it, and density varies with the hand that sifted it. The template controls the path, not the speed.

The seal is also a visual form. A stick can be left alone; a seal asks to be watched, and the slow consumption of the line is part of the practice. That is why it appears in tea rooms and meditation settings where the burn is meant to be seen as well as smelled.

## The Cultural Frame in Three Words

**Direct answer:** 香道, 合香 and 四般闲事 name a discipline, a method and a social setting — context rather than equipment.

香道 is a discipline of preparation and appreciation: the material, the tools, the room and the act of smelling all count. 合香 is the method underneath it — several materials composed into one formula, traditionally ranked by the 君臣佐使 (chief, minister, assistant, guide) principle rather than combined at random. For the structure itself, see [the blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/).

四般闲事 — the "four leisure arts" — places incense beside tea, hanging scrolls and flower arrangement. The list comes from 吴自牧's 梦粱录, a Song-dynasty record of urban life, and it is a compact statement of what a cultivated household was expected to enjoy. It also corrects a modern assumption: incense was not always a solitary sensory hobby. It belonged to a social and aesthetic setting, and it was judged in company.

## Look-Alikes That Cost Money

**Direct answer:** Four near-pairs cause most beginner purchases of the wrong object: stick against coreless stick, coil against spiral stick, cone against backflow cone, resin against powder.

| Pair | The real difference | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Stick / coreless stick | Bamboo spine versus pressed solid | Coreless is thinner, shorter-burning and cleaner, but snaps easily |
| Coil / spiral stick | Hours of folded path versus a decorative shape | A short decorative spiral sold as a coil ruins a long session |
| Cone / backflow cone | Upward burn versus a channelled downward plume | The waterfall needs its own burner; a plain dish kills the effect |
| Resin / powder | Solid gum or chip versus ground material | Powder cannot be lit like a stick; it needs heat or a template |

The stick pair carries the largest practical consequence. A bamboo core adds a woody, neutral note and keeps the stick straight; a coreless stick gives a cleaner perfume at the cost of fragility. Neither is better in the abstract — a coreless stick suits a short, precise session, a cored one suits a longer, sturdier burn. See [incense sticks](/blog/incense-sticks/) for the full comparison.

The coil pair is mostly a retail problem. A coil is engineered for duration; a spiral-shaped stick is engineered for appearance. If a listing shows a spiral and omits the burn time, the buyer cannot tell which object is for sale. The cone pair works the same way: a backflow cone is a cone with a hole, and the hole redirects airflow rather than improving the material. Resin and powder are two states of the same substance — one solid, one particulate — and the format name describes the state, not the source.

## Matching Format to Session

**Direct answer:** Choose by session length and attention first, scent second; a format that does not fit the occasion gets used badly no matter how good the material is.

| Session | Best fit | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Short reading or meditation block | Stick or coreless stick | Predictable 20–60 minute burn, easy to let finish |
| Long evening, whole-room scent | Coil | Hours of hands-off burn |
| Brief offering or single note | Cone | Burns down completely in a compact window |
| Careful appraisal of a material | Resin chip on a heater | Gentle heat shows top, body and dry-down |
| Timed, decorative burn | Seal powder | The pattern itself measures the session |
| Visual centrepiece | Backflow cone | The falling smoke is the point; scent is secondary |

The table is a starting point, not a rule. A stick can be snapped to shorten a session, a coil can be extinguished and relit, a cone can be moved to a smaller room. The real variable is attention: a stick asks for almost none, while a chip on a heater asks for management because the heat and the scent keep changing. Match the format to the attention you actually have.

Cost per session is a separate axis from unit price, and it is where most buying errors hide. A long coil looks expensive until it is divided by six hours of burn; a premium stick looks cheap until it is divided by twenty minutes. A dense resin chip can be warmed twice. Comparing formats by the price on the box, rather than by the cost of the session they deliver, is the mistake to avoid.

## From Dough to Stick: Where Batches Fail

**Direct answer:** A stick is aromatic powder, binder and water, shaped and dried; the binder holds the shape together and the drying step decides whether the batch survives.

The chain: **aromatic powder + binder + water → kneaded dough → extrusion or hand-rolling → cutting to length → controlled drying → bundling and storage.** Powder carries the scent, binder carries cohesion and often part of the burn, water makes the mix workable. Proportions are formula-specific: too much binder and the stick smells of paste, too little and it crumbles or goes out.

Drying is where more batches fail than anywhere else. A stick that dries too fast cracks at the surface while the core is still wet → the crack runs deeper as the core shrinks → the stick sheds powder when handled. A stick that dries too slowly in still, warm air can mold. The window between the two is narrow, which is why commercial drying corridors are controlled for both temperature and airflow rather than heat alone.

A bamboo core adds a fifth element: the splint. Dough is applied around it, which gives the stick strength and a straight axis. Coreless sticks are pressed from dough alone, so they are thinner and more fragile. That is a manufacturing choice with sensory consequences, not a quality hierarchy — the core buys durability and adds a neutral woody note; its absence buys purity and costs strength.

The same dough becomes other formats. A cone is moulded, a coil extruded and wound, a stick extruded straight. Consistency of moisture and particle size is what lets one blend leave the factory as all three; a plant that cannot hold those steady produces sticks that crack, cones that tunnel and coils that sag.

## The Characters Are a Compressed Manual

**Direct answer:** The Chinese format names are descriptive, so a reader who knows a handful of characters can often guess the shape before seeing it.

香 (xiāng) is the constant — incense. 线 (xiàn) means thread, so 线香 is the thread incense, the stick. 盘 (pán) covers plate, tray and coil, so 盘香 is the wound one. 塔 (tǎ) is a pagoda, which is what a standing cone resembles. 片 (piàn) is a slice or flake, which is why 香片 can name a flat piece of material.

The method words are equally literal. 隔 (gé) means to separate or interpose, 火 (huǒ) is fire, 焚 (fén) is to burn — so 隔火焚香 is burning with the fire kept apart. 篆 (zhuàn) is seal script or a carved pattern, hence 篆香. 合 (hé) means to join, so 合香 is joined incense, a blend. The names are not arbitrary labels; they are short instructions, and that is one of the small pleasures of learning them.

## What a Format Word Cannot Tell You

**Direct answer:** A format word describes shape and heat path; it says nothing about whether the powder is fresh, the binder balanced, or the batch dried slowly enough.

"Coreless" means no bamboo spine. It does not mean good material. "Coil" means a long folded path; it does not mean an even cross-section. The questions that decide quality belong to the material and the maker, and they need different checks: how the piece burns, what the ash looks like, whether the supplier can name the powder and the binder without deflecting.

That is the boundary this glossary will not cross. Vocabulary gets you to the right object and the right question; it cannot grade the object for you. A shop listing that says only "coil" omits the stand, the diameter and the burn time. A listing that says "backflow cone" omits the burner it requires. Fill those gaps with questions before you pay, because the format noun is a starting point, never a specification. For a decision path built on these words, see [which incense format](/blog/which-incense-format/).

## Summary

> **Direct answer:** Five formats, five practice terms and a short list of near-pairs cover most of what a buyer needs.

Heat divides the five formats: stick, coil and cone are self-sufficient once lit; resin and powder wait for charcoal, a warmer or a template. Geometry decides how long the ember walks, while formulation and drying decide whether it walks at all. The practice words carry the culture — 香道 the discipline, 合香 the method, 隔火焚香 the indirect route, 篆香 the powder clock, 四般闲事 the social frame. Hold the three tiers apart and a product page turns legible.

## FAQ

### Q: How many formats count as incense in the Chinese trade?
A handful of shapes cover the trade: straight stick, wound spiral, standing cone, solid resin or chip, loose powder. Coreless sticks and backflow cones are refinements of the first and third, not new categories. Each shape tells you the ignition method, the approximate duration, and the accessory — holder, stand, dish or heater — that has to come with it.

### Q: What does 隔火焚香 mean in practice?
"Incense across fire." Charcoal is covered with ash, a thin mica or silver plate sits on the ash, and the aromatic material rests on the plate. Heat arrives without flame, so the scent rises slowly and the smoke stays low. The method is old, associated with Song-dynasty practice, and it is the direct ancestor of the Japanese kōdō ceremony.

### Q: How long does a stick last next to a coil?
A stick usually runs between twenty minutes and an hour, with thickness, density and airflow moving it inside that band. A coil is built for a much longer session, often several hours, because the ember follows a spiral instead of a straight line. Trust the maker's stated figure; the format only sets the order of magnitude.

### Q: Is coreless always the better stick?
No. A coreless stick is pressed from fragrant material alone, so it usually smells cleaner and throws less smoke, but it is thinner, easier to break and shorter-burning. A bamboo-cored stick is stronger and can be made longer, though the core adds a neutral woody note and extra smoke. Choose by the session you have, not by the reputation of the word.

### Q: What makes a backflow cone different?
A hollow core and a burner with a matching hole. Smoke is drawn down the channel, cools, grows denser than the room air, and pours out at the base rather than rising. The visual effect is the whole point; material and burn time stay close to a standard cone of the same powder. It needs the right burner, and moving air will scatter the plume.

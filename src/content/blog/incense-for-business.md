**Direct answer:** Trade buying of Chinese incense splits three ways. You can take **stock** lines in bulk, commission an **OEM** build that follows your brief and carries your name, or apply **private label** branding to a formula the factory already runs. The three differ in how much of the production chain you pay to change — and none of them quotes from a printed sheet.

**Key facts:** Every page in this commercial cluster starts here. The three routes are set out below, matched against the kind of buyer each one serves, with seven deeper guides hanging off them — supplier checks, order quantities, sampling, packing and customs, fragrance construction. Nothing is sold on this page; the point is to make the mechanics legible before anyone commits money.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The factory does three different jobs.** Clearing a rack, printing new cartons and starting from a written recipe cannot be swapped, so their terms cannot be either.
- **A printed list is a symptom, not a service.** Totals are assembled from four inputs; a sheet would have to ignore every one.
- **Know who you are dealing with first.** Legal identity is settled before money is discussed.
- **Samples settle the goods, not the maker.** Smell and burn can be judged from one; repeatability at volume cannot.
- **Where evidence is missing, the page says so.** Figures appear as "on request" or as typical practice, never as invented precision.

## Three Routes, Three Different Production Promises

**Direct answer:** Ask what the factory must physically do and the three paths stop blurring: take cartons off a rack, print new ones, or start from a written recipe.

| Path | What the factory does differently | What you own afterwards | Smallest run | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stock (wholesale) | Picks existing cartons off the shelf | Nothing beyond the goods | Quoted case by case, typically the largest | Shops that need something sellable this month |
| Private label | Reprints the carton, leaves the recipe alone | The branding, not the formula | The packaging run sets it | A brand with no product team |
| OEM | Builds from a written brief | Recipe, dimensions, pack, exclusivity | The project sets it | A brand that knows exactly what it wants |

Stock buying is shelf-space arithmetic. Someone else has already paid for the tooling, so the buyer's only task is selection. Re-badging inserts a single extra operation, printing, into an otherwise untouched line. OEM begins earlier: a formula is agreed, a trial is run, and only after that does a saleable unit exist.

Cost and calendar climb along the same line: **shelf pick → print run → build.** What separates the three prices is the work behind them, not a mark-up habit. Read it as a mark-up habit and you will either pay for control you never needed or assume control you were never given.

Where to go deeper: [bulk buying](/blog/wholesale-guide/) covers volume ordering and inspection, [the build-to-brief route](/blog/oem-private-label-guide/) covers the build side. The head-to-head between the latter two is set out in the [OEM vs private label](/blog/oem-vs-private-label/) comparison.

## Matching the Route to What You Already Have

**Direct answer:** What you already hold decides it — premises, a scent brief, or a name. Cheapness on a spreadsheet is a distant second.

- **A shop that needs sellable stock now** → wholesale. Someone else's product, your shelf, your shop's name on the signage.
- **A defined scent, format or recipe** → OEM. Development time buys a formula you own.
- **A brand with no product team** → private label. An existing formula, your name on the pack.

Each route purchases a different quantity of work. Wholesale buys finished goods; private label buys finished goods plus branding; OEM buys development, tooling and exclusivity.

One thing is worth saying outright: **neither path carries a quality guarantee.** A thoughtfully run re-badging programme can outclass a carelessly briefed development job, since a factory's long-settled recipe may be more refined than a newcomer's first attempt. Authorship and exclusivity are what the second path purchases; competence is a separate question, answered only by smelling and burning what comes back.

## Control, Cost and Speed: The Trade-off in One Table

**Direct answer:** Three things move together — say, spend, and speed — and picking a path means deciding which of the three you are willing to give up.

| If this describes you | Take this path | What you are buying | What you surrender |
|---|---|---|---|
| You need revenue before you need a signature scent | Stock | Speed and proven sell-through | Any say in what is inside |
| You have a brand and a launch date | Private label | A market entry without R&D | Recipe ownership |
| You have a brief and the patience for it | OEM | A formula nobody else can copy | Time, and the largest upfront spend |

Read the third column down and the trade becomes visible: each path sells something the others cannot, and charges for it in the fourth. **Say over the recipe costs calendar and cash; speed costs say.** Wanting OEM-level authorship on a wholesale timetable is a request for a product that has not been developed.

The table is an editorial reading, not a claim about any named factory. Actual jobs combine them in uneven shares, so treat the grid as an opening question.

## Reading a Quote as a Five-Line Document

**Direct answer:** A quote is a short document, not a figure. Five lines — unit price, order quantity, lead time, payment terms and Incoterms — each limit what the others can mean, and reading only the headline leaves four risks unchecked.

The five lines interlock. **Unit price → the quantity it assumes → the lead time that quantity implies → the payment terms attached to that lead → the Incoterms that fix who pays freight and who carries risk.** Shift one and the others shift with it. A figure at a thousand units will not sit beside a figure at ten thousand, and a date quoted ex-works will not sit beside one quoted delivered.

Every line hides one thing in particular. The price hides the quantity it assumes. The quantity hides the setup behind it — a line that must be cleaned and reset recovers that cost somewhere. The date hides the queue the job joins. The payment terms hide who is financing the run. The Incoterms hide the moment the goods cease to be the seller's problem.

Two guides take this apart: [quoting variables](/blog/moq-pricing-guide/) for what moves the figure, and [export mechanics](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/) for what happens once the goods leave the factory.

## Why "On Request" Is the Honest Answer

**Direct answer:** "On request" is a statement about how a figure is produced, not a refusal to produce one. Until the inputs are fixed, any number offered would be a guess wearing a quotation's clothes.

Four inputs decide the figure. **Specification → quantity → destination → material grade → one computed number.** Fix three and the fourth becomes determinate; leave them open and the only honest reply is a range wide enough to be useless.

What follows is a change in what the buyer should expect. "On request" translates as "supply the variables and the arithmetic follows." A supplier who asks for product, quantity and destination before quoting is doing that arithmetic in the open. One who produces a confident fixed number before knowing any of the three is either guessing or quoting a different product from the one you described.

That distinction sorts suppliers faster than any price list. That is also why quoting gets a page to itself further along: the inputs are where the shocks hide.

## Landed Cost: The Layers Above the Unit Price

**Direct answer:** The unit price is one layer of the real cost. Freight, duty, packaging and — on an OEM project — development sit above it, so comparing unit prices compares a fraction of what will actually be paid.

Write the stack out and it stops being abstract. **Unit price + freight + duty + packaging + development = landed cost.** Carriage climbs with kilograms and kilometres, and sticks are light but bulky, so it rarely disappears into the total. Duty comes from the classification the destination assigns. Packing follows the format and the labelling rules of that market. Development is a one-off on a commissioned build, spent before anything can be sold.

The consequence is practical: **two quotes with matching unit prices can land at very different totals**, and the cheaper-looking one can be the more expensive order. A buyer comparing per-unit figures alone has compared the part the supplier controls and ignored the parts the route, the destination and the packaging control.

## The Sequence That Avoids Expensive Mistakes

**Direct answer:** First purchases go best in one order: settle the brief, check the supplier, approve a sample, obtain the figure, run a trial, then scale. Each stage leans on the one before, and skipping is where money disappears.

The order is not bureaucratic. **No brief → nothing to vet. No fixed spec → no standard for judging a sample. No approved sample → no basis for trusting a figure. No trial run → no evidence for volume.** Run it backward and the usual error shows itself: a buyer who starts with price has arrived at stage four while the first three sit empty.

Defining the product is the step most often rushed, and it decides everything after it. Format, dimensions, expected burn time, aroma profile, material grade and packaging all belong in the definition, because each one moves the quote. The [custom fragrance development](/blog/custom-fragrance-development/) guide covers the case where the scent itself has to be built rather than selected from a range.

There is a second reason to write the definition down: it becomes the document you check a delivery against. Conversation is not a document. When a delivery arrives wrong, the written brief is the only thing that can establish what was agreed, and it earns its keep exactly at the point where goodwill runs out.

## What a Sample Proves, and What It Cannot

**Direct answer:** A sample confirms the formula, the finish and the burn. It does not confirm that the factory can reproduce those qualities at volume, on schedule, to the agreed specification.

The gap is structural. **A sample is small, hand-selected and made under close attention → a production run is large, machine-paced and made under time pressure.** Those are different tests of different capabilities. Approval of the first is evidence about the product; it says nothing about the second.

That is why [the sampling page](/blog/samples-program/) treats sampling as one checkpoint rather than a finish line. A buyer who stops at the approved sample has validated the thing and skipped the supplier. A buyer who treats the sample as the first of several gates has the order the right way round — and the trial order, not the sample, is where delivery actually gets tested.

---

## What a Trial Order Actually Tests

**Direct answer:** A trial order tests four things at once — whether the recipe survives a production line, whether cartons and labels match the approved artwork, whether the export paperwork clears, and whether the promised date holds.

Smell is the least of it. Volume reveals whether the whole batch still matches the piece that was approved, rather than only the units picked for the courier. It reveals whether the carton and label copy are the versions that were signed. It reveals whether the export documents agree with the load — the difference between clearing and sitting at a border. It reveals whether the promised date survives the queue.

None of those answers can come from a sample, because a sample is never pushed through the same constraints. That is why the trial is the gate before scale, and why a supplier who resists one is telling you something about where the risk would land.

## Where the Three Routes Blur in Practice

**Direct answer:** The three-way split describes clean cases. Actual orders drift between them — stock lines with bespoke cartons, a re-badged formula with one note altered — which is normal, not a defect in the framework.

Nothing stops abruptly at a boundary; the work slides across. **Order stock with a printed carton → still a stock purchase, with printing attached. Take a factory recipe and alter one note → private label shading into OEM. Write a formula and cut fresh tooling → OEM in the full sense.** Most jobs sit between those points, so the question is not which label applies but how far the factory is being asked to depart from what it already runs.

Distance has a price. Each move away from the catalogue brings development weeks, a bigger floor on quantity and a larger invoice. Buy too much distance and you fund tooling nobody needed; buy too little and the product is indistinguishable from what sits on a competitor's shelf. Where the line falls depends on what the brand must be able to claim.

## Red Flags Worth Slowing Down For

**Direct answer:** Pause on three things: a figure volunteered before anyone has described the goods, silence about the company behind the trading name, and pressure to move straight to a bulk order.

The pattern behind them is the same. **Commitment first, information later → the risk quietly changes owner.** A figure quoted before quantity and market exists will be revised once both appear. A refusal to state a legal identity removes the one fact a buyer can check independently. A push past sampling removes the only test that exposes delivery failure.

None of these is proof of bad faith by itself. An under-resourced supplier can produce all three without intending harm. Each of them points the same way — at an arrangement that discourages checking — and the least costly place to see it is before funds move.

## Certifications: Check the Paper Against the Product

**Direct answer:** Paperwork is worth requesting, then worth checking against the consignment. A document describing something other than what is in the carton demonstrates almost nothing.

Any such document describes a plant or a product at one moment. Its worth rests on three checks: whose name is on it, exactly which goods it covers, and when it was issued. Miss any of the three and it is decoration.

The [supplier vetting](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/) treats certifications as one item inside a wider audit, alongside capability, references and quality control. Collecting documents without checking them produces the look of diligence and none of the substance.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** A new buyer gains more from one changed opening line than from any negotiation tactic. Lead with what you intend to check, not with what you hope to pay.

**Reasoning:**
- Botanical incense is a craft category, and in craft categories the number falls out of the specification. A figure produced before that specification exists is a guess, and haggling over a guess settles nothing.
- Opening with checkable questions tells the other side that the work will be audited. A supplier who knows the job prefers that, not from flattery but because a checked order is less likely to fail after shipping.

**Disclosure:** Editorial judgment from inside the category, not a verified fact. This site publishes buying guidance as part of its reference material and also trades. The advice is a stance on buying, not a pitch.

## FAQ

### Q: What separates OEM from private label?
One route hands the factory a brief you wrote — recipe, dimensions, pack — and returns it under your name. The other leaves the recipe untouched and changes only what a customer sees: the carton, the label, the tin. Calendar and control follow: authorship costs development time, while launch speed comes off a formula that already runs. What decides between them is whether a scent or format brief exists yet. The [build-to-brief route](/blog/oem-private-label-guide/) takes it further.

### Q: How do I buy incense wholesale from China?
Three inputs come before anything else: what the item is (form, materials, scent family), how many, and which market it lands in. A quotation is assembled from those. After them comes identity — legal name, plant capability, references worth calling — and only then a sample. Order minimums, production calendar, packing and freight terms are confirmed per inquiry. [Bulk buying](/blog/wholesale-guide/) and [supplier vetting](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/) take each stage in turn.

### Q: What is a typical minimum order quantity for incense?
No single figure exists, and there is no published list to look one up on. The smallest run follows the form, whether the goods come off a shelf or off a drawing board, and how much setup the line needs before it can start, so it is settled per inquiry instead. The useful question is narrower than the general one: what is the smallest run for this item, at this specification, bound for this market. [Quoting and minimums](/blog/moq-pricing-guide/) sets out what moves the number.

### Q: Can I get samples before placing a large order?
Yes. Sampling is routine, and its job is narrow: let you smell, handle and burn the thing before ordering volume. What it settles is the product. What it leaves open is whether the same factory can repeat that quality across a full run, on schedule, to the agreed spec. Treat it as a gate rather than a verdict — [the sampling page](/blog/samples-program/) is explicit about the difference.

### Q: What should the first message to a supplier contain?
Three fields: what you want made, how many units, and where they are going. Those are the minimum a supplier needs in order to answer with something real rather than a catalogue link. A first message missing any of them forces a round of questions before the useful conversation can begin, while a buyer who supplies all three arrives at the substance in one exchange. With the answers already fixed, the inquiry runs through [the inquiry form](/request-a-quote/).

## Summary

Read the cluster once and it becomes a line: pick a path, write the brief, check the supplier, sign off a sample, obtain the figure, name the market, then prove delivery before volume. **Path → brief → check → sample → figure → market → trial** is that line, and every stage is a small commitment that can be walked back.

The route fixes the control-cost-speed trade-off. The definition makes every later step possible. Vetting catches risks no sample can reach. A quote read line by line exposes the cost a headline figure conceals. The destination fixes the landed number. The trial order is the only stage that tests delivery rather than intention.

The pace is deliberate. Every stage heads off a failure whose repair costs more time than the stage itself consumes, which is why the buyer who keeps the order tends to arrive first.

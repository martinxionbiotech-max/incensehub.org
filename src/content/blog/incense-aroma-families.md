**Direct answer:** The 150 botanical incense ingredients in this reference are sorted into **ten aroma families** — woody, resinous, sweet, herbal, spicy, floral, citrus, earthy, smoky, and balsamic. Each family holds materials that share a scent character, and most materials appear in more than one family, because a single wood or resin can smell several ways at once.

**Key facts:** The ten families come from `aroma.json`, a Schema.org `Dataset` served from `data.incenseherbs.com`. Each family is a `DefinedTerm` listing its member ingredients by slug. A family is a **scent character, not a botanical class** — the same plant part can carry several characters, and one character can span woods, resins, flowers, and roots.

## Key Takeaways

- Ten families: **woody, resinous, sweet, herbal, spicy, floral, citrus, earthy, smoky, balsamic**.

## What an Aroma Family Is (and Is Not)

**Direct answer:** An aroma family is a shared scent character — woody, floral, spicy, and so on — not a botanical category. One ingredient can belong to several families, and one family can contain woods, resins, flowers, and roots that happen to smell alike.

The family system is a *sensory* grouping, which is why it does not map cleanly onto botany:

- **Agarwood** is woody, resinous, and sweet at once — layered, so it sits in three families.
- **Vetiver** is earthy, woody, and smoky — a grass root, not a wood, yet it reads as woody.
- **Frankincense** is resinous, citrus, and earthy — a resin that also carries a bright citrus edge.

This is a feature, not a bug: the families describe how a material *smells and behaves in a blend*, which is what a blender or a buyer actually works with. The canonical membership is in [aroma.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/aroma.json).

## The Ten Families, Defined

### Woody (木香) — the backbone of Chinese incense

> **Direct answer:** The woody family covers the dry, warm, clean smell of aromatic timber — sandalwood's creamy softness, agarwood's deep layered sweetness, cedar's pencil-box sharpness. It anchors classical Chinese incense.

Canonical members: agarwood, benzoin, cedar, orange-peel, patchouli, sandalwood, vetiver, borneol, pine-resin. Full treatment in the [woody family page](/blog/woody-aroma-family/).

### Resinous (树脂香) — deep, sticky, aromatic

> **Direct answer:** The resinous family is the smell of gum-resin itself — frankincense's clean bright gum, pine resin's sharp conifer edge, sweetgum's sweet balsam. These soften and melt under heat rather than burning like wood.

Canonical members: agarwood, frankincense, pine-resin, sweetgum. Full treatment in the [resinous family page](/blog/resinous-aroma-family/).

### Sweet (甜香) — sugar, honey, amber

> **Direct answer:** The sweet family is the sugared, honeyed warmth that rounds out a blend — benzoin's vanilla, osmanthus's apricot honey, rose's romantic sugar. It is the broadest family, reaching across resins, flowers, and woods.

Canonical members: agarwood, benzoin, cedar, chrysanthemum, cinnamon, jasmine, orange-peel, osmanthus, rose, sandalwood, angelica, calamus, sweetgum, ambergris.

### Herbal (草本香) — green, grassy, bitter-green

> **Direct answer:** The herbal family is the green, grassy, faintly bitter smell of leafy and rooty herbs — mugwort's camphor green, chrysanthemum's bittersweet blossom, angelica's aromatic root. It links the kitchen garden to the temple.

Canonical members: chrysanthemum, mugwort, angelica, calamus.

### Spicy (辛香) — warm, clove-cinnamon heat

> **Direct answer:** The spicy family is the warm, penetrating heat of cinnamon, clove, and the spicy edge of patchouli — the warming notes that lend a blend its bite and lift.

Canonical members: cinnamon, clove, patchouli, angelica, calamus.

### Floral (花香) — blossom, honeyed, heady

> **Direct answer:** The floral family is the blossom smell of rose, jasmine, and osmanthus — sweet, honeyed, and in jasmine's case heady. It joined a canon built on wood and resin mostly as a modern and symbolic note.

Canonical members: chrysanthemum, jasmine, osmanthus, rose. Full treatment in the [floral family page](/blog/floral-aroma-family/).

### Citrus (柑橘香) — bright, zesty, lemony

> **Direct answer:** The citrus family is the bright, zesty, lemony note carried by orange peel and partly by frankincense's fresh opening. Alongside smoky it is the smallest family, and it works as a lifting top note.

Canonical members: frankincense, orange-peel.

### Earthy (土香) — rooty, soil-like, grounded

> **Direct answer:** The earthy family is the rooty, soil-like, grounding smell of vetiver, patchouli, and myrrh — heavy, long base notes that steady the lighter materials above them.

Canonical members: frankincense, myrrh, patchouli, vetiver, calamus, ambergris.

### Smoky (烟香) — charred, burnt-wood

> **Direct answer:** The smoky family is the charred, burnt-wood smell, canonically carried by vetiver alone. It is the narrowest family, defined by one representative material.

Canonical member: vetiver.

### Balsamic (香脂香) — sweet-resinous, vanilla-amber

> **Direct answer:** The balsamic family is the sweet-resinous, vanilla-amber smell of benzoin, myrrh, and pine resin — materials that are sweet and resinous at once, and that act as fixatives.

Canonical members: benzoin, myrrh, patchouli, pine-resin, sweetgum.

## How the Families Overlap

**Direct answer:** The families are not a partition — most ingredients belong to several. A single material can be woody and resinous (agarwood), floral and sweet (rose), or earthy and smoky (vetiver). The overlap is what makes blending an art rather than a checklist.

Three examples show the overlap working:

- **Agarwood** appears in woody, resinous, and sweet — the deepest, most layered material in the canon.
- **Patchouli** appears in woody, spicy, earthy, and balsamic — a leafy herb that reads as rooted wood.
- **Vetiver** appears in woody, earthy, and smoky — the one material that spans the ground and the smoke.

This is why a blend is composed on character, not on a single family tag. The full relationship graph is in [relationships.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/relationships.json).

## The Data Layer

**Direct answer:** The ten families are published as a machine-readable `Dataset` — [aroma.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/aroma.json) — with each family a `DefinedTerm` listing its member ingredients by slug. The same family tags also appear in each ingredient page's `aroma` frontmatter field.

Three files carry the structure:

- **Canonical source:** [aroma.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/aroma.json) — ten `DefinedTerm` records, each with a `memberIngredients` list.
- **On each entity page:** the `aroma` frontmatter field carries that material's family tags, feeding the page's meta description and `DefinedTerm`.
- **Companion datasets:** [ingredients.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/ingredients.json) (150 entities) and [relationships.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/relationships.json) (806 typed edges, including aroma links).

The full dataset inventory is on the [knowledge map](/blog/landeng-knowledge-map/).

## Why a Material Can Sit in Three Families

**Direct answer:** A material lands in several families because scent is layered, not single — a piece of agarwood releases woody, resinous, and sweet facets at once, so tagging it with one family would discard two real qualities.

The multi-membership is a consequence of how a burn releases aroma. **A material's volatiles → several overlapping scent notes at once → several family tags.** A single family tag is a convenience, not a full description; the full description is the set of tags a material actually carries.

That is the deepest reason the family system is not a classification. A classification forces one bin; a family system lets a material keep all the facets it actually has, which is why the data can be honest about a layered material like agarwood without distorting it.

## Why the Family Count Is Ten and Not More

**Direct answer:** The number ten is not arbitrary — it is the smallest set of characters that covers the 150 ingredients without collapsing distinct smells into each other, and the data's narrow families prove the discipline.

The boundary logic is visible at the edges. If the families were fewer, the broad middle would swallow the sharp ends — smoky and citrus would fold into woody and sweet, and two genuinely distinct characters would disappear. If they were more, the new families would be splitting hairs rather than naming real differences. **Ten → the fewest characters that keep the sharp edges distinct.** The narrow families, not the wide ones, are the proof that ten is the right number.

That is why the count matters. A family system that cannot hold a single-material family like smoky is too coarse; one that invents families for every nuance is too fine. Ten holds the middle and the edges at once.

*The author's editorial view, not a verified fact.*

## The Two Axes of the Family Map

**Direct answer:** The ten families are not a flat list — they arrange along two hidden axes, brightness and depth — and reading those axes is what turns a memorized list into a usable map.

The first axis runs bright to dark: citrus and floral at the bright end, smoky and earthy at the dark end, woody in the middle. The second runs light to heavy: herbal and citrus at the light end, balsamic and earthy at the heavy end. **Bright-to-dark → the first axis; light-to-heavy → the second; a material's family → its coordinates on both.** A material that sits bright and light (citrus) reads completely differently from one that sits dark and heavy (earthy), even though both are single families.

That is the map behind the list. A buyer who knows the two axes can place any family in a second, and can predict how two families will combine, without memorizing every pairing.

## Reading a Family Tag the Right Way

**Direct answer:** A family tag is a pointer to a scent character, not a verdict — it tells you one facet of a material, and a buyer or blender should read it as "one of several," never as "the only one."

The practical rule is to treat every tag as partial. When a label says a stick is "woody," it is naming one facet of a blend that almost certainly has others; when a dataset lists an ingredient under three families, all three are true, and none is the whole story. **One tag → one facet; several tags → the fuller picture.**

That rule protects against the most common family error: assuming a material smells like only its most prominent family. A buyer who reads "sweet" and expects only sweetness will miss the resinous depth that the same material also carries, and the tag is not at fault — the single-facet reading is.

## How Two Families Combine in a Blend

**Direct answer:** Two families combine not by averaging but by layering — a bright top family over a deep base family produces a third character that is neither one alone — and understanding that combination is the real payoff of knowing the families.

The combination follows the axes. A citrus top over a woody base gives a bright lift over a warm floor; a floral top over a balsamic base gives sweetness over sweetness, which can turn cloying unless one side is held back. **Top family → the opening; base family → the finish; the combination → both, in sequence.** The result is not a midpoint; it is a sequence of two characters over the course of one burn.

That is why blending is an art, not a checklist. A checklist would just add the two families together; a blender knows the top arrives first and the base arrives last, and that ordering is what makes the pair work or fail. The family map supplies the vocabulary for that ordering.

## Why the Small Families Matter for Buyers

**Direct answer:** The small families are not a trivia detail — they are the sharpest buying signals in the whole map, because a single-material family tells you exactly what you are getting, with no room for a vague blend to hide.

A family with one member is a precise promise. A stick tagged smoky is pointing at vetiver, and nothing else; a stick tagged citrus points at two possible materials, not twenty. **Single-member family → a near-exact prediction; wide family → a broad hint.** The narrow families are where the map is most informative.

That flips the common assumption that the big families matter most. For a buyer trying to predict a smell from a label, the small families are the high-precision tool, and the wide families are the low-precision one — which is why a buyer should pay *more* attention to a smoky or citrus tag, not less.

## Why a Family Is Not a Grade

**Direct answer:** A family says what a material smells like, not how good it is — a "woody" tag carries no information about quality, price, or rarity, and reading it as a grade is the surest way to misuse the map.

The distinction is sharp. Family names the character; grade names the quality, and the two are independent axes. **Character → the family tag; quality → a separate judgment the family says nothing about.** A cheap woody material and a rare woody material share the family and differ in everything that matters to price.

That is why the map is a scent map, not a value map. A buyer who wants to know what something smells like reads the family; a buyer who wants to know what it is worth has to look elsewhere, because the family tag deliberately answers only the first question.

## How the Families Feed a Blend

**Direct answer:** A blend is composed from the families, not from single materials — a base from the woody or earthy end, a heart from the floral or sweet middle, a top from the citrus or spicy edge — and the family map is what makes that structure visible.

The three-part structure of a blend maps onto the families. **Base → woody, earthy, or balsamic; heart → floral or sweet; top → citrus or spicy.** The family of a material tells a blender roughly where it belongs in the structure, before any precise recipe is involved.

That is the practical use of the map for anyone who blends. The families are not just a catalog; they are a coordinate system, and a blender who can place a material in the right family already knows which third of the blend it belongs in.

## FAQ

### Q: How many aroma families does this reference use?
Ten: woody, resinous, sweet, herbal, spicy, floral, citrus, earthy, smoky, and balsamic. Each is defined in [aroma.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/aroma.json) as a `DefinedTerm` that lists its member ingredients.

### Q: Can one ingredient belong to more than one aroma family?
Yes — and most do. Agarwood is woody, resinous, and sweet; patchouli is woody, spicy, earthy, and balsamic; vetiver is woody, earthy, and smoky. The families name scent character, not a single botanical class, so a layered material carries several tags.

### Q: What is the difference between "resinous" and "balsamic"?
They are related but distinct. Resinous is the deep, sticky gum-resin character (frankincense, pine resin); balsamic is the sweet-resinous, vanilla-amber character (benzoin, myrrh). Many balsamic materials are also resinous or sweet — benzoin sits in woody, sweet, and balsamic — but the two families emphasize different aspects of the same physical material.

### Q: What is the difference between "woody" and "earthy"?
Woody is the dry, warm scent of aromatic wood (sandalwood, cedar, agarwood); earthy is the rooty, soil-like, grounded character of roots and resins (vetiver, patchouli, myrrh). They overlap — vetiver reads as both — but woody is the cleaner wood register, earthy the heavier, darker ground register.

### Q: Which aroma families are the smallest?
Smoky (a single canonical member, vetiver) and citrus (two — frankincense and orange-peel). They are deliberately narrow: smoky is a distinctive charred character carried by one material, and citrus is a bright top note that appears in only two canon members.

### Q: Where can I see the full membership list?
The machine-readable list is in [aroma.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/aroma.json). The human-readable hub pages for the three flagship families are [woody](/blog/woody-aroma-family/), [resinous](/blog/resinous-aroma-family/), and [floral](/blog/floral-aroma-family/), and every ingredient's `aroma` field lists its own family tags.

### Q: Why is a family a "scent character" instead of a botanical class?
Because the same plant part can carry several characters, and one character can span very different plants. A grass root (vetiver) reads as woody; a resin (frankincense) reads as citrus; a flower (osmanthus) reads as sweet. If the families were botanical, these crossovers would be impossible; as scent characters, they are exactly the point.

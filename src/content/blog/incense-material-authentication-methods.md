**Direct answer:** Authentication is a sequence, not a laboratory. Documents, senses, physical tests, microscopy, chromatography, and DNA each answer a different question at a different cost. The skill is choosing the cheapest test that can falsify the specific claim in front of you — and stopping there.

**Key facts:** Most material fraud is detectable before any instrument is involved. The expensive tests are for the claims that survive the cheap ones: species in a powder, dilution in an oil, and reconstitution that smells right. The [materials pillar](/incense-materials/) sets out the four-question frame this page operationalises.

## Key Takeaways

- **Test the claim, not the material.** "Is this good?" is unanswerable; "is this *Santalum album* heartwood?" is a test.
- **Sampling error outranks method error.** A perfect assay on a rigged sample is worse than no assay, because it buys false confidence.
- **The cheapest decisive test wins.** Water, cut, and heat settle most wood questions for the price of a knife and a lighter.
- **Instruments answer identity and dilution, not origin.** No GC-MS run proves "wild" or "vintage."
- **Keep the sample.** The retained reference is what turns a one-time result into a supply-chain capability.

## Start with the claim, not the kit

**Direct answer:** Every authentication programme begins by writing down the exact claim being tested. Vague claims produce vague tests, and vague tests produce confident invoices.

A supplier says "wild Hainan agarwood, sinking grade." That sentence contains four separable claims: species or region, wild versus planted, resin density, and grade language. Each has a different test and a different reliability. Testing "the material" as a whole is how buyers end up paying for a chromatography report that answers none of them.

Write the claim as a list, then mark each line with the test that could disprove it. If a line has no test, it is not a claim you can verify — it is a story you are choosing to accept. That is a legitimate decision, as long as it is made consciously and priced accordingly.

The second preparatory step is deciding what happens if the test fails. A test without a consequence — return, credit, renegotiation — is market research, not quality control. Suppliers notice which buyers never act on results.

## The six-rung ladder

**Direct answer:** The ladder runs documents, senses, physical tests, microscopy, chromatography, and DNA. Each rung is more expensive and more specific than the one below it.

| Rung | Test | Answers | Cost band | Sample destroyed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Documents | declared identity, legality | minimal | no |
| 2 | Senses | gross substitution, treatment | minimal | no |
| 3 | Physical | density, surface treatment, ash | low | partly |
| 4 | Microscopy | tissue identity, fillers, dye | low–medium | small |
| 5 | Chromatography | molecular profile, dilution | medium–high | yes |
| 6 | DNA | species, sometimes genus | high | yes |

The order is not a claim that lower rungs are more truthful. It is a claim about information per dollar. A visual sort eliminates bark-heavy frankincense in ten minutes; a GC-MS run on the same lot would also detect it, at perhaps two orders of magnitude more cost and a week of waiting.

There is also a strategic reason to start low. Rungs 1–3 can be run on the supplier's premises, in front of the supplier, which changes the conversation. A seller who watches you cut a chip and smell the interior knows that the next shipment will be inspected the same way. Deterrence is cheaper than detection.

## Rung 1: what documents actually prove

**Direct answer:** Documents prove that a declaration was made and processed. They do not prove that the material in the bag matches the declaration.

Three document types matter, and they fail differently.

**CITES permits and certificates.** *Aquilaria* and *Gyrinops* have been listed on CITES Appendix II since 2005, so cross-border movement requires export permits and, on import, certificates. The permit is tied to a consignment: a quantity, a species listing, an exporter, an importer. Once a consignment is split, the paperwork describes the parent shipment, not the split. Ask for the permit number and check that the quantity and date are consistent with what you are buying.

**Phytosanitary and fumigation certificates.** These address pests and plant health, not identity. A fumigated shipment can be the wrong species. They matter for storage risk, which is a different problem.

**Certificates of analysis.** A COA is a scope document: sample identity, method, specification, result, and issuing laboratory. The common failures are a certificate for a different material, a method with no specification attached, and an in-house document presented as independent verification. Read the sample description first. If it says "essential oil" and you are buying chips, the certificate is irrelevant no matter how official it looks.

## Rung 2: training the senses as an instrument

**Direct answer:** Sensory assessment is a real method with real limits: it is good at categories and bad at percentages, and it degrades with fatigue.

The nose is a classifier, not a scale. It can say "this is not *Boswellia sacra*" or "this has been in contact with kerosene" with useful reliability. It cannot say "this is 12% adulterated." Buyers who expect quantification from smelling are asking the wrong instrument.

A workable sensory protocol has four parts:

1. **A reference set.** Small, labelled samples of known-good material for each species you buy. Without references, you are comparing today's sample to a memory, and memory drifts.
2. **Blind order.** Smell the unknown before the reference, not after. Priming is real and it is the main source of false confirmation.
3. **Short sessions.** Five to eight samples, then a break. Olfactory fatigue sets in quickly and flattens everything toward "woody."
4. **Temperature control.** Warm the material gently and let it rest. Most resins and woods release a different profile at 40–60 °C than at room temperature, and the warm profile is closer to what the customer will experience.

Record the descriptors immediately, in your own words, before discussing with anyone else. Group discussion is useful afterward and contaminating during.

## Rung 3: physical tests and their blind spots

**Direct answer:** Physical tests are cheap, fast, and easy to over-read. Each one measures a proxy, and every proxy can be gamed.

**The water test.** Density separates agarwood into sinking, half-sinking, and floating fractions. It measures the average density of the piece, including any material that was added. Oil loading raises density, so a loaded piece can sink. The test is a filter for resin load, not a test for authenticity, and it says nothing at all about powders or oils.

**The cut test.** Cut a fresh surface and smell it immediately. Treatment concentrates at the surface; the interior is harder to fake. Look for a colour gradient, a oily sheen that stops abruptly, or a core that is pale while the outside is dark. This is the single most informative cheap test for resinous wood.

**The heat test.** Warm a small piece on a hot plate or a mica sheet. Authentic resinous wood usually softens, beads, and releases a layered scent; synthetic loading often produces a sharp top note that collapses into a flat, acrid tail. The test is comparative: run it beside a reference.

**The damp-cloth test.** Rub a piece with a white cotton cloth dampened with water, then with a little alcohol. Dye transfer is a red flag. Some natural woods release colour, so interpret against a reference rather than in isolation.

**The ash test.** Burn a small quantity and examine the residue. High inert filler content leaves disproportionate ash; pure resin leaves little. The test is crude and confounded by binder in finished sticks, so reserve it for loose powders.

## Rung 4: microscopy, the underused rung

**Direct answer:** Microscopy identifies tissue and detects fillers, and it is the cheapest way to catch powder fraud that sensory testing misses entirely.

A basic transmitted-light microscope with 40× to 400× magnification is enough for most questions. The preparer mounts a small amount of powder in water or glycerol, or sections a chip with a razor.

What you can see:

- **Wood anatomy.** Vessel arrangement, fibre type, and the presence of oil or resin deposits in parenchyma differ between genera. A powder that claims *Santalum* but shows anatomy inconsistent with Santalaceae is a finding, even without species-level identification.
- **Starch grains.** Starch is a common filler and shows characteristic birefringence under polarised light. If the material is not a starch-bearing plant, starch is contamination.
- **Dye crystals and pigment particles.** Dyes often appear as discrete crystals or amorphous deposits on the surface of fibres rather than within tissue.
- **Bark and leaf fragments.** These indicate a different plant part than the one claimed, which is a separate fraud from species substitution.

Microscopy cannot identify species in most powdered samples. It can, however, tell you that the material is not what the label says at the tissue level, which is often enough to stop a purchase. Start with the [ingredient glossary](/blog/incense-ingredients-glossary/) to confirm which plant part the supplier claims to be selling.

## Rung 5: chromatography and what a profile means

**Direct answer:** Chromatography produces a molecular fingerprint. It is decisive for oils and useful for resins, and it is only as good as the reference library it is compared against.

**GC-MS** separates volatile compounds and identifies them against mass spectra. For essential oils it is the workhorse: santalols in sandalwood oil, sesquiterpenes in agarwood oil, monoterpenes in frankincense. Two things matter in reading a report. First, the method: column type, temperature programme, and internal standards determine what can be seen. Second, the comparison: a list of compounds is not an authentication; a match against a validated reference profile is. The [essential oils in incense](/blog/essential-oils-in-incense/) page explains what a distillate can and cannot carry.

**LC-MS and HPLC** handle the less volatile fraction. For agarwood, the 2-(2-phenylethyl)chromones are the interesting class, and their presence and ratio carry information about resin formation. For pharmacopoeial materials, the monograph's marker assay is the relevant benchmark — the Chinese Pharmacopoeia monograph for 沉香, for example, specifies thin-layer identification and a chromatographic assay, which is a defensible standard even for buyers outside the medicine trade.

**Raman spectroscopy** has moved from the physics lab into practical oil authentication. Huang and colleagues combined Raman spectra with chemometrics to authenticate agarwood essential oils and detect adulteration, which is valuable because agarwood oil is expensive, variable, and frequently stretched. The method is fast and non-destructive, but it depends on a model trained on known samples. Without that model, a spectrum is not an answer.

The honest limit of all chromatography is the same: it describes the sample, not the story. A perfect santalol profile does not prove Indian origin; a chromone pattern does not prove wild harvest. Instruments are good at "what is in this," poor at "where did this come from."

## Rung 6: DNA barcoding and its material problem

**Direct answer:** DNA barcoding can identify species in wood, and it fails most often on exactly the processed forms where fraud is easiest.

Tanaka and Ito used *trnL–trnF* and *matK* sequences to identify agarwood source species in commercial samples, and a companion study applied barcoding to Indonesian agarwood. The approach works because short, standardised regions of the chloroplast genome vary between species and can be amplified from degraded tissue.

The limitation is the substrate. Wood is dead tissue; DNA degrades with time, heat, and processing. Powders may contain a mixture of species, and oils usually contain no amplifiable DNA at all. A barcoding result is therefore strongest on chips and unfinished wood, weaker on powder, and often unavailable for oils.

Sampling also matters more than the method. A barcode from one chip describes one chip. If a bag contains mixed species, the result depends entirely on which piece was sampled — which is why barcoding should be paired with an increment sampling plan rather than a single grab.

## Sampling: the error that outranks every method

**Direct answer:** Most authentication failures are sampling failures. The laboratory answers the question it was given, about the sample it received.

A defensible plan for a bagged lot has three features:

1. **Increments, not grabs.** Take small portions from multiple bags — a common rule of thumb is one increment per ten bags, with a minimum of five — and combine them into a composite. For heterogeneity questions, keep the increments separate instead.
2. **Top, middle, bottom.** Fraud clusters at the top and the seams. Sample where the bag was opened and where it was sealed.
3. **Retention.** Keep a sealed reference sample of every accepted lot, labelled with date, supplier, lot, and measured values. Retain it for at least the life of the products made from that lot.

The composite tells you the average. The retained sample tells you what you accepted. When a customer complaint arrives eight months later, only the second one helps. The [bulk ingredient sourcing](/blog/bulk-incense-ingredient-sourcing/) guide covers how to write the sampling plan into the order.

## The claim-to-test matrix

**Direct answer:** Match the test to the claim. This table is the working version of the ladder.

| Claim | Cheapest decisive test | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| "This is *Santalum album*" | microscopy, then DNA | reject or reprice as *Santalum* spp. |
| "This is Indian sandalwood" | documents + chromatography | reprice as unspecified origin |
| "This agarwood sinks" | water test on a cut piece | reprice by density fraction |
| "No oil was added" | cut and heat test, then GC-MS | reject; loading is not reversible |
| "This is 100% heartwood" | visual + microscopy for sapwood | reprice; sapwood is a different material |
| "This powder is 100 mesh" | sieve analysis | reject or adjust formula |
| "This oil is pure" | GC-MS or validated Raman | reject; dilution changes the formula |
| "This is wild-harvested" | no reliable test | treat as story, price accordingly |
| "This is CITES-cleared" | permit + quantity check | stop; legality precedes quality |

The last two rows are the important ones. Some claims are untestable at any price, and the correct response is to stop paying a premium for them. Others are legal questions where a failed check ends the transaction before quality is even relevant.

## Building a lab-lite kit

**Direct answer:** A buyer can run rungs 1–4 with a small, cheap kit and reserve rungs 5–6 for disputes and high-value lots.

A practical kit: a sharp knife or scalpel, a cutting board reserved for samples, a jeweller's scale, a set of sieves, a hot plate or mica sheet, a lighter, white cotton cloth, isopropyl alcohol, glass vials, a hand lens, a basic microscope, and a labelled reference box.

The reference box is the asset. Ten to twenty known-good samples, each with provenance and date, turn every future comparison into a test against a standard rather than an opinion. Replace references when they oxidise or lose aroma, and record the replacement.

For instruments, the rational approach is outsourcing with a specification: name the method, the marker compounds, the reference standard, and the reporting limits in the purchase order. A laboratory will deliver exactly what you specify, and "test it for authenticity" is not a specification.

## Where testing goes wrong

**Direct answer:** Testing fails in five predictable ways, and none of them is the laboratory's fault.

**Testing the wrong claim.** A COA for oil, a purchase of chips. The document is real and irrelevant.

**Single-sample inference.** One good chip in a ten-kilo lot proves one chip. Heterogeneous materials need increment sampling.

**Method without specification.** "GC-MS passed" means nothing without a reference profile and numeric limits.

**Instrument worship.** A chromatography report is treated as a certificate of quality, when it only describes composition. It cannot speak to origin, age, or harvest method.

**No consequence.** Results are filed and nothing changes. Suppliers learn that inspection is theatre, and the fraud returns at the next shipment.

The fix for all five is procedural, not technical: write the claim, sample properly, specify the method, interpret within scope, and attach a commercial consequence.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** I think most incense buyers should spend less on laboratory testing and more on cutting materials open in front of the supplier — because the fraud that costs them money is visible at the cut, and the tests they are paying for answer questions they never asked.

**Reasoning:** First, the dominant frauds in this trade are physical — oil loading, dyeing, sapwood, bark, fillers — and a fresh cut plus a warm sample exposes them at almost no cost. Second, the expensive instruments are strongest on oils and powders, which are a minority of what most small buyers purchase and the form where a supplier's own specification matters more than a one-off test. Third, a test result without a retained sample and a batch record is a single photograph of a moving object; the procedural discipline is what actually changes supplier behaviour.

**Disclosure:** This is an editorial judgment about resource allocation for small and mid-sized buyers, not a claim that laboratory testing is unnecessary. High-value oils, disputed lots, and regulated materials justify instrumental testing; the argument is about the order of spending, not the value of the instruments.

## FAQ

**Can I authenticate agarwood with a water test alone?**
No. Density is one proxy for resin load and can be raised by oil loading. Use it as a filter alongside a cut and heat test.

**Is GC-MS worth it for a small buyer?**
For a single low-value purchase, usually not. For a recurring oil purchase, yes — one baseline profile plus periodic checks is cheaper than one bad batch.

**How much sample does DNA barcoding need?**
Very little material, but the DNA must be amplifiable. Chips and unfinished wood work best; powders are inconsistent and oils usually fail.

**Do I need to test every batch?**
No. Test the first lot, then audit with periodic checks and retained samples. The batch record and the retention programme do more work than continuous testing.

**What if the supplier refuses to allow a cut test?**
That refusal is itself information. A supplier who will not let you cut a chip is telling you where the risk sits.

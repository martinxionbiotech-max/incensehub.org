## 1. Research question and editorial position

The material layer is usually written as a catalogue: 150 ingredients, scent families, a few warnings about fakes. The editorial position, formed before the source sweep, was:

> The trade sorts materials by scent or by plant part. Neither predicts how a material fails. Sort by physical class and by the layer of the claim, and most fraud becomes a question with a test attached.

The sources were then used to test that position — particularly whether instrumental methods actually close the gap, or only move it.

## 3. Contradiction log

| # | Conflict | Why it exists | Resolution used |
|---|---|---|---|
| X-01 | Instrumental testing presented as definitive vs. species-only scope | Labs answer the question asked; marketing treats "passed" as a quality certificate | Pages separate composition from origin/age; instrument limits stated explicitly. |
| X-02 | "Sinking = best" vs. loaded pieces that sink | Density measures average mass, not resin origin | Water test demoted to a rule-out filter; cut and heat test required alongside. |
| X-03 | "Natural = safer" vs. smoke exposure evidence | Origin and combustion risk are different variables | Safety deferred to the safety pillar; "natural" framed as origin claim. |
| X-04 | Grade words as measurements vs. as marketing | Five different grade systems share one vocabulary | Five-grade taxonomy (density, purity, age, particle, prestige) published as the resolution. |
| X-05 | Barcoding "works" vs. fails on processed material | Literature reports success on chips; trade fraud concentrates in powder/oil | Published as a substrate limitation, not a method failure. |

## 4. Information gain

1. A physical-class table (8 classes) that predicts failure mode rather than scent family.
2. The four-question claim frame: identity → form → grade → treatment.
3. A six-rung authentication ladder with cost band and destructiveness, plus a stop rule.
4. Claim-to-test matrix, including two rows where the honest answer is "no test exists."
5. A five-grade taxonomy that explains why grade words do not compare across markets.
6. The denominator test: price per aroma-bearing unit, with a worked sandalwood example.
7. A PO specification with six lines that converts labels into contract terms.
8. A label-grammar taxonomy (category, rank, origin, form, process) with burden-of-proof table.
